Q 1 · 5 marks · ~9 min

Sahyadri Urban Co-operative Bank, Satara, publishes two tenders in June 2026 on the GeM portal. Tender I invites firms of Chartered Accountants for the statutory audit of its 14 branches for FY 2026-27. It states "professional fee not to exceed ₹45,000 per branch" and requires an EMD of ₹25,000. Tender II invites "Chartered Accountants, Cost Accountants and Company Secretaries" for a review of loan documentation at a fee to be quoted, with an EMD of ₹3 lakh. CA Prakash Jadhav, in practice for 12 years, plans to bid for both. He is unhappy that the bank's earlier panel ignored him despite four letters he wrote "requesting allotment of at least two branches". His articled assistant suggests also listing the firm on a mobile app that lists electricians, caterers and tax consultants, "to get visibility with the bank's members". Advise CA Prakash on the bids and on his other conduct, with reference to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949 and the guidelines of the Council.

Model answer (ICAI format): Provision: Clause (6) of Part I of the First Schedule: a CA in practice is guilty of professional misconduct if he solicits clients or professional work, directly or indirectly, by circular, advertisement, personal communication, interview or any other means. The proviso allows responding to tenders or enquiries. However, under the Council's guideline (No. 1-CA(7)/03/2016 dated 7 April 2016, also Chapter XIII of CGG 2008), a member shall not respond to any tender in areas exclusively reserved for Chartered Accountants, such as audit and attestation, unless a minimum fee is prescribed in the tender document or the area is open to other professionals as well. The minimum fee must commensurate with the size, value, volume, manpower and nature of the work. A cost sheet must be kept for the Institute's perusal. Reasonable EMD is not interfered with; for exorbitant EMD, the Ethical Standards Board may look into the case.

Analysis: Tender I is for statutory audit, an area reserved for CAs, and it prescribes only a maximum fee. The restriction applies, and CA Prakash cannot respond. Tender II is open to Cost Accountants and Company Secretaries too, so he may respond and pay the EMD. If he thinks ₹3 lakh is exorbitant, he may refer it to the ESB. Registering on GeM is not empanelment or an aggregator listing, because the client itself operates it, so it is permitted. Writing to an organisation to be placed on a known panel is allowed. Writing repeatedly to request allotment of work is solicitation under Clause (6). Listing on an application-based aggregator that also lists other trades is not permissible under the Advertisement Guidelines, 2008.

Conclusion: He should not bid for Tender I. He may bid for Tender II and keep a cost sheet. He must stop requesting allotment and must not list on the aggregator app; doing either would be misconduct under Clause (6) of Part I of the First Schedule.

Marks go for: exclusive area + minimum fee rule (maximum fee ≠ minimum fee); "open to other professionals"; EMD and ESB; roving requests = solicitation; aggregator not permitted.

Q 2 · 5 marks · ~9 min

Malwa Cables Ltd, an unlisted public company, held its AGM on 28 September 2026. The retiring auditor, CA Sunil Datar, had served one term. At the AGM, he was replaced by Raghuvanshi & Co. on a resolution moved under a special notice. Raghuvanshi & Co. received the appointment letter on 30 September. They wrote to CA Datar on 1 October by speed post and kept the postal receipt. Because the company's bankers wanted the stock statements certified quickly, they began the audit on 2 October. They relied on a certificate from the Company Secretary that "all provisions of Sections 139 and 140 have been complied with". On 10 October, CA Datar told the Institute that his FY 2025-26 fee of ₹3.2 lakh, provided in the accounts signed by both, was still unpaid. Malwa has been registered since 2009 and has positive net worth. Comment on the conduct of Raghuvanshi & Co. Would your answer change if Malwa were a government company whose appointment letter, received in late September, required the audit to begin within three days?

Model answer (ICAI format): Clause (8) of Part I of the First Schedule: a CA in practice is guilty if he accepts a position as auditor previously held by another CA without first communicating with him in writing. The communication must give positive evidence of delivery: Registered A.D., by hand against acknowledgement, an acknowledgement from the e-mail address registered with ICAI, or UDIN. A certificate of posting is not enough (J.S. Bhati v Council of ICAI). Clause (9): guilty if he accepts appointment as auditor of a company without first ascertaining whether Sections 139 and 140 of the Companies Act, 2013 have been complied with. "Ascertain" means to find out for certain. A management certificate is not sufficient; he must verify the special notice, its dispatch to members at least 7 days before the meeting, its service on the retiring auditor forthwith, the circulation of the representation, and the minutes. Chapter VII, CGG 2008: do not accept an audit where the undisputed audit fee of another CA for a statutory audit is unpaid. A provision in accounts signed by both is "undisputed". The only exception is a sick unit, i.e. registered for at least 5 years with accumulated losses equal to or exceeding its entire net worth.

Analysis: The firm started work before any proof of delivery. A speed post receipt is no better than a certificate of posting, and writing after acceptance does not cure the breach (SM Illus 15). It relied on the Company Secretary's certificate. The fee is undisputed and Malwa, with positive net worth, is not a sick unit.

Conclusion: Raghuvanshi & Co. are guilty under Clauses (8) and (9) of Part I of the First Schedule. They are also guilty of breaching Chapter VII of CGG 2008, i.e. Clause (1) of Part II of the Second Schedule. If a government company: the duty to communicate applies equally. Where the time schedule leaves no time to wait for a reply, the incoming auditor may give a conditional acceptance after sending the communication, stating that acceptance is subject to professional objections from the previous auditor, and decide finally after considering them. So the timing would be defensible only if the letter was sent first, with proof of delivery, and the acceptance was expressly conditional. Clause (9) and the fee issue would remain.

Marks go for: positive evidence of delivery (not posting proof); "first" = before acceptance; "ascertain" = verify records, not a certificate; undisputed fee + sick-unit definition; conditional acceptance for government audits.

Q 3 · 4 marks · ~7 min

CA Ananya Rao runs a practice in Mysuru. In January 2026 she received from clients: ₹60,000 as an advance against a GST annual return engagement; ₹18,000 for ROC filing fees, which she paid three days later; ₹9.5 lakh as liquidator of Kaveri Silks Pvt Ltd; and ₹5 lakh from a temple trust "to be invested in RBI bonds once the trustees meet in July". She deposited all four in her firm's current account. The liquidation money was used to clear the firm's GST dues for two weeks and then replaced. Her senior manager argues that "the money was never lost, so nothing is wrong". Examine.

Model answer (ICAI format): Provision: Clause (10) of Part I of the Second Schedule: a CA in practice is guilty if he fails to keep moneys of his client, other than fees or remuneration or money meant to be expended, in a separate banking account, or fails to use such moneys for the purposes for which they are intended within a reasonable time. Council suggestions: (i) an advance against services to be rendered is not covered; (ii) money for expenses to be incurred within a reasonably short time (statutory fees, stamp paper) need not go to a separate account; (iii) money for expenses not intended to be spent within a reasonably short time must go to a separate account immediately; (iv) money received as trustee, executor, liquidator, etc. must go to a separate account immediately.

Analysis: The ₹60,000 advance falls under (i), so there is no breach. The ₹18,000 was spent within three days, under (ii), so there is no breach. The ₹9.5 lakh as liquidator falls under (iv); it had to be kept separately and was also used for her own purpose. The ₹5 lakh is to be used only after about six months, under (iii), so it had to be kept separately. Replacing the money later is no defence. Using a liquidator's money for the firm's dues may also be examined under Clause (4) of Part II of the Second Schedule (defalcation or embezzlement of money received in a professional capacity).

Conclusion: She is guilty under Clause (10) of Part I of the Second Schedule for the liquidation money and the temple trust money. The manager's argument fails, because the clause is breached by not keeping the money separately and not using it for its purpose, whether or not there is a loss.

Marks go for: the four Council suggestions; classifying each receipt; "immediately" for liquidator/trustee money; no loss is no defence.

Q 4 · 5 marks · ~9 min

Vindhya Cement Ltd is listed on NSE. Its Director (Finance), CA Kiran Mehta, is a key managerial person. While reviewing quarry expenses, he found that ₹2.6 crore had been paid through a "logistics vendor" to obtain a mining-lease renewal. He also found that blasting continues beyond the permitted limits near a village school. The MD tells him the matter is "routine in the industry" and that the board's audit committee "need not be troubled". The statutory auditors are in the middle of a long dispute with the company over the valuation of a captive power plant and have not noticed the payments. Advise CA Kiran on his responsibilities under the Code of Ethics. How would your answer differ if Vindhya Cement were an unlisted public company?

Model answer (ICAI format): Provision: NOCLAR under the Code of Ethics covers acts of omission or commission, intentional or unintentional, contrary to laws or regulations, committed by the employer, TCWG, management or others working for it. It includes fraud, corruption and bribery, environmental protection and public health and safety. In India, Section 260 applies to senior professional accountants in service, being employees of listed entities. Senior professional accountants are directors, officers or senior employees able to exert significant influence over the employer's resources, i.e. KMP. Turning a blind eye is not an appropriate response.

Analysis: Vindhya is listed and CA Kiran is a director and KMP, so Section 260 applies. He should: (1) obtain an understanding of the matter; (2) address the matter by discussing it with his superior and, since the MD himself is involved, with those charged with governance (the audit committee), and not accept the MD's instruction; (3) seek advice (within the organisation, from the Institute or from legal counsel); (4) determine whether further action is needed; (5) determine whether to disclose to an appropriate authority; (6) in the case of an imminent breach causing substantial harm to the public (the blasting near the school), after first considering whether to discuss it with management or TCWG, use professional judgment on whether to disclose immediately to an appropriate authority, and such disclosure is permitted; (7) document how management or TCWG responded, the courses of action, judgments and decisions considered under the reasonable and informed third party test, and how he is satisfied that he has met his public-interest responsibility. Confidentiality under Clause (2) of Part II of the Second Schedule is not breached where disclosure is required by law.

Conclusion: CA Kiran must act under Section 260 and cannot leave the matter to the auditors. If unlisted: Section 260, as applicable in India, would not apply, because it is limited to employees of listed entities. He would still be bound by the fundamental principles (integrity, professional behaviour) and by the confidentiality clause, subject to disclosure required by law.

Marks go for: Section 260 conditions (listed + KMP); the seven response steps; imminent breach → disclosure permitted; extra documentation; unlisted → not applicable.

Q 5 · 5 marks · ~9 min

Pillai Varghese & Co., Kochi, is the statutory auditor of Malabar Ports Ltd, a listed company. Its audit fee is ₹40 lakh and its turnover is ₹310 crore. In May 2026, the firm joined "Coastal & Associates", a group of six firms in Kerala and one firm in Muscat. The firms share client and billing software, a common technical cell whose opinions are binding, and a common brand that appears next to each firm's name on audit reports. No form has been filed with ICAI because "it is only a co-operation arrangement". Another firm in the group, Nair & Co., is offered Malabar Ports' internal audit at ₹30 lakh. A third firm, Jacob & Co., is offered ERP advisory at ₹95 lakh. Malabar Ports' regulator requires rotation of auditors in 2027. The group plans for Nair & Co. to take over the audit from Pillai Varghese then. Advise.

Model answer (ICAI format): Network test (CGG 2008, Chapter XV): a larger structure aimed at co-operation is deemed a network where the entities share, among other things, a common brand name (including use with the firm name when signing audit reports), a significant part of professional resources (common systems for client data and billing, or a technical department whose advice must be followed) or common quality control. The test is whether a reasonable and informed third party would conclude that a network exists. Here the shared software, the binding technical cell and the common brand on audit reports make it a network.

Compliance gaps: (1) The name must use "& Affiliates", not "& Associates". It needs approval in Form A (ICAI decides within 30 days) and is reserved for 3 months, during which the network must register in Form B. Registration is mandatory. (2) For the arrangement with the Muscat entity, the Indian firms must file a declaration in Form D within 30 days of entering the network. Any change in constitution goes in Form C within 30 days. (3) A firm may join only one network.

Ethical compliance: Because Pillai Varghese is the statutory auditor, no network firm (Nair & Co.) may accept the internal audit. The non-audit fee ceiling for other firms of the network collectively is three times the statutory audit fee, i.e. ₹1.20 crore. Jacob & Co.'s ₹95 lakh is within it, provided the total for all other network firms does not exceed ₹1.20 crore. Where rotation is prescribed by a regulator, no member firm of the network can replace a retiring member firm, so Nair & Co. cannot succeed Pillai Varghese.

Conclusion: Not filing is a breach of the Council's guidelines, i.e. misconduct under Clause (1) of Part II of the Second Schedule. The internal audit must be declined, the rotation plan dropped, and the ERP fee kept within the 3× ceiling.

Marks go for: network deemed (brand on audit report, shared resources); Forms A/B/D with 30 days / 3 months / 30 days; "& Affiliates"; no internal audit by network firms; 3× ceiling; rotation bar.

Q 6 · 5 marks · ~9 min

CA Rohini Kale holds a full-time COP in Nagpur and has three articled assistants. From 1 April 2026 she: (a) became managing director of "Kale Advisory Pvt Ltd", a company engaged only in management consultancy, approved and registered with ICAI, in which she holds 70% of the shares; (b) became a whole-time director of a non-profit educational society on a monthly salary, applying to the Council on 20 April 2026; (c) began editing a fortnightly film magazine; (d) took up private tuition for CA Foundation students for 6 hours a week; (e) holds an LIC agency licence inherited from her father, only to receive renewal commission; and (f) continues as a director simplicitor of Orange City Foods Ltd, whose tax audit her proprietary firm has just accepted. Her friend says the MD role means she can no longer sign audits or train articles. Examine each.

Model answer (ICAI format): Provision: Clause (11) of Part I of the First Schedule: a CA in practice is guilty if he engages in any business or occupation other than the profession of CA unless permitted by the Council. Proviso: he may be a director (not MD or WTD) of a company unless he or any partner is interested in it as auditor. Regulation 190A and the resolutions in Appendix 9 set out the general and specific permissions.

(a) Under the Guidelines for Corporate Form of Practice (CGG Chapter XVII), a member may be MD, WTD or Manager of a company engaged exclusively in Management Consultancy and other services, approved and registered with ICAI. There is no restriction on equity holding. She retains a full-time COP and may do attest work and train articles. Not misconduct; the friend is wrong. (b) Full-time or part-time employment in a non-business concern needs the specific and prior approval of the Council. Applying after joining is too late (SM Illus 21), so she is guilty for the period without permission. (c) Editorship of journals other than professional journals needs specific permission. She is guilty unless approved. (d) Private tutorship is generally permitted. Total direct teaching hours must not exceed 25 a week for her to do attest work; 6 hours is within the limit. (e) Holding an LIC agency licence for the limited purpose of renewal commission is generally permitted. (f) A director simplicitor is generally permitted, but the Council prohibits such a member from auditing the company. Clause (4) of Part I of the Second Schedule applies to all attest functions, including tax audit, on the principle of independence.

Conclusion: She is guilty under Clause (11) for (b) and (c), and under Clause (4) of Part I of the Second Schedule for the tax audit in (f). (a), (d) and (e) are in order.

Marks go for: corporate-form MCS company keeps full COP; specific + prior approval; general list items; 25-hour limit; director simplicitor cannot audit (any attest function).

Q 7 · 5 marks · ~9 min

Seth Bansal & Co., Jaipur, has hired a branding agency after losing two bank audits. The agency's plan, which the partners have accepted, includes:

  • (i) a newspaper write-up headed "Rajasthan's AI-first audit firm". It states "audited 40+ companies with turnover above ₹500 crore", lists a 2025 award from a private business magazine, prints the firm's vision statement and shows the firm name in 18-point type;
  • (ii) visiting cards for partner CA Neeraj Bansal reading "CA Neeraj Bansal, FCA, Registered Valuer (Securities or Financial Assets), Practising since 1998";
  • (iii) a WhatsApp broadcast to 2,000 local traders listing the firm's services;
  • (iv) website pages showing partners' photographs and an "Industries we serve" page displayed only on a specific request.

CA Neeraj insists everything is true. Comment.

Model answer (ICAI format): Provision: Clause (7) of Part I of the First Schedule prohibits advertising professional attainments or services, and using any designation other than CA on professional documents, visiting cards, letterheads or sign boards, unless it is a university degree or a title of a recognised body. The proviso allows a write-up under the Council Guidelines for Advertisement, 2008. Clause (6) prohibits soliciting by any means. Truth is no defence. The write-up must also avoid exaggerated claims, client names, testimonials and fees, and must not show awards except those given by the Central or State Government or regulatory bodies. Monograms and catch words are not allowed. The Membership No. or FRN is mandatory, and the font must not exceed 14.

(i) "AI-first audit firm" is a catch word and an unsubstantiated claim. The client-turnover claim is an exaggeration and refers to clients. A private magazine award is not permitted. Under an ESB decision, a vision or mission statement may appear only in the firm profile on request. The 18-point font exceeds 14. So the write-up breaches Clause (7) and the Guidelines. (ii) "Registered Valuer" may be mentioned by an empanelled member, and FCA is permitted. "Practising since 1998" breaches Clause (7), because the date of setting up practice should not appear on letterheads or other professional documents. (iii) Under an ESB decision, using messaging applications to make people aware of one's practice and services is not permissible. It is solicitation under Clause (6). (iv) A passport-style photograph is permitted. The nature of services and the areas of experience may be shown on a "pull" request, so the website is in order if the rest of it follows the pull model.

Conclusion: The partners are guilty under Clauses (6) and (7) of Part I of the First Schedule for (i), (ii) and (iii). Only (iv) is compliant. If the write-up's content is false, Clause (3) of Part III of the First Schedule also applies.

Marks go for: write-up conditions (catch words, awards rule, font 14, client names); vision statement ESB; RV allowed / date of practice not allowed; messaging apps = solicitation; pull model.

Q 8 · 5 marks · ~9 min

CA Tanmay Joshi audits Sangam Agritech Ltd, a listed SME. Most of his file deals with a complex Ind AS 115 contract with a government agency. Inventory of ₹22 crore (40% of total assets) was counted on 31 March 2026, but he was appointed only on 20 April 2026. He neither attended a count later nor performed any roll-back procedure, and he relied on the past trend of stock levels. 95% of sales went to one distributor that had been shown as a related party in FY 2022-23 but not since; he accepted management's word that it was "now independent". Management refused to let him send balance confirmations. He did not inquire into the reasons or do any alternative procedures. He issued an unmodified opinion. Explain the provisions of the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949 that he has breached. Would it have helped if he had issued a qualified opinion saying only "we did not attend the physical count"?

Model answer (ICAI format): Provisions (Part I of the Second Schedule): Clause (7): does not exercise due diligence, or is grossly negligent in the conduct of his professional duties. Clause (8): fails to obtain sufficient information necessary for expressing an opinion, or its exceptions are sufficiently material to negate the expression of an opinion. Clause (9): fails to invite attention to any material departure from the generally accepted procedure of audit applicable to the circumstances. The Standards: SA 501 requires attendance at the count when inventory is material, unless impracticable; if impracticable, alternative procedures; if still not possible, a modified opinion under SA 705. SA 550 requires sufficient appropriate evidence that related parties are identified and disclosed. SA 505 requires the auditor to inquire into management's refusal, evaluate its effect on the risk of material misstatement (including fraud), and perform alternative procedures.

Analysis: These facts mirror the NFRA case in the SM. He did no alternative inventory procedures and relied on trends. He applied no professional skepticism to a former related party taking 95% of sales. He did not respond to the confirmation refusal. An unmodified opinion without sufficient evidence, and without drawing attention to the departures, breaches Clauses (7), (8) and (9). In that case the engagement partner was also penalised and debarred for two years under Section 132(4) of the Companies Act, 2013.

Qualified opinion: The SM states that failure to perform a statutory duty is not excused merely by qualifying the report. For example, a mere statement that cash was not verified is not enough where verification was necessary, feasible and material. He would still have had to perform alternative procedures. If they were not possible, he should have modified the opinion as SA 705 requires and stated clearly why generally accepted procedures could not be followed. Where the limitations negate an opinion, he should have disclaimed rather than written "subject to".

Conclusion: He is guilty under Clauses (7), (8) and (9) of Part I of the Second Schedule. A bare qualification would not have cured the breach.

Marks go for: Clauses 7, 8, 9 worded correctly; SA 501/550/505 requirements; NFRA outcome; "qualification does not excuse failure of duty"; disclaim, not "subject to".

Q 9 · 4 marks · ~7 min

CA Gaurav Arora has been offered the statutory audit of Doaba Foods Pvt Ltd, Ludhiana, for FY 2026-27. He resigned as a director simplicitor of Doaba in August 2025. His wife holds Doaba's equity shares of face value ₹85,000. His brother is an accounts officer at Doaba, but not a KMP. Gaurav's firm has an overdraft of ₹1.4 lakh from Doaba's group finance company, which is not Doaba itself. Separately, his partner is a trustee of the Arora Charitable Trust, whose audit the firm has also been offered. Gaurav is a part-time lecturer at a commerce college, whose audit he wants too. Advise on each offer.

Model answer (ICAI format): Provision: Clause (4) of Part I of the Second Schedule: a CA in practice is guilty if he expresses an opinion on the financial statements of an enterprise in which he, his firm or a partner has a substantial interest. The Council's guidelines under this clause apply the principle of independence to all attest functions. Doaba: (1) Cooling-off: a member shall not accept the audit of a company for two years from the completion of his tenure as director or his resignation. He resigned in August 2025, so FY 2026-27 audit acceptance falls within the two years and he should decline. The SM also records the ESB's 2017 review, which leaves such decisions to the Companies Act, 2013 and the Code of Ethics, but the SM's own guideline and TYK apply the two-year bar. (2) A relative's securities are a disqualification under Section 141(3)(d)(i) read with Rule 10 only if their face value exceeds ₹1 lakh. ₹85,000 does not. (3) A relative who is a director or KMP disqualifies (Section 141(3)(f)); a brother who is an accounts officer, not KMP, does not by himself. Familiarity threats should still be evaluated under the Code. (4) Indebtedness: CGG Chapter X bars appointment while indebted to the concern beyond the statutory limits, otherwise beyond ₹1,00,000. The overdraft is from a group company, not Doaba, so Chapter X as worded is not attracted to Doaba. He should assess Section 141 for the group company. Trust: the Council has decided that a CA should not accept the audit of a trust where his partner is an employee or a trustee, so he should decline. College: the Council has decided that a CA should not accept the audit of a college where he is working as a part-time lecturer, so he should decline.

Conclusion: He should decline all three offers. For Doaba, the only operative bar is the cooling-off period; the shares and the brother's job alone would not disqualify him.

Marks go for: two-year cooling-off; ₹1 lakh face value; relative as director/KMP only; indebtedness to "the concern" ₹1,00,000; trust-partner-trustee and college-lecturer decisions.

Q 10 · 4 marks · ~7 min

Konkan Fisheries Pvt Ltd has applied to Coastal Bank for a ₹25 crore loan. The bank's credit officer phones the auditor, CA Sameer Desai, and asks him (a) to share his working papers on receivables and (b) to explain over the phone the reasons for a large write-off. The Managing Director, whose powers include giving such consents, e-mails Sameer: "Please share whatever the bank needs." Konkan's group auditor, who audits the holding company, separately demands access to Sameer's working papers. A week later, Sameer finds evidence that the company's purchase manager has been diverting funds through fake vendors. Advise Sameer on each matter.

Model answer (ICAI format): Provision: Clause (1) of Part I of the Second Schedule: a CA in practice is guilty if he discloses information acquired in the course of his engagement to anyone other than his client, without the client's consent or otherwise than as required by law. The duty continues after the engagement ends. For a company, consent may be given by the Board (Section 179), or by the MD if the Board's powers are delegated to him comprehensively enough to include giving such consent. Otherwise a Board resolution is needed.

(a) and (b) Working papers are the auditor's property. There is no duty to give the client or third parties access, although at his discretion he may give extracts to the client. Sharing information differs from sharing working papers. Information may be given to the bank with the client's valid consent. The MD's e-mail is valid consent here because his delegated powers cover it. Sameer may therefore explain the write-off, and should limit himself to what is consented, but he is not bound to hand over his working papers. Group auditor: an auditor is not required to give other auditors of the same enterprise or a related enterprise, such as the parent, access to his working papers. The other auditor may rely on his work without that access. Fraud: under Section 143(12) of the Companies Act, 2013, if the auditor has reason to believe an offence involving fraud is being or has been committed against the company by its officers or employees, he must report it to the Central Government within 60 days of his knowledge, following the prescribed procedure. Disclosure required by law is outside Clause (1).

Conclusion: He may share information with the bank under the MD's valid consent, but not his working papers. He may refuse the group auditor's demand. He must follow the Section 143(12) procedure for the fraud, which is not a breach of confidentiality.

Marks go for: Clause (1) wording; who can consent for a company; working papers vs information; no access for other auditors; Section 143(12) as a "required by law" exception.

Q 1 · 5 marks · ~9 min

Ratnagiri Marine Exports Ltd closes its books on 31 March 2026. CA Farhan of Qureshi & Co. signed the audit report on 22 May 2026 and handed it to the Board the same day; the AGM is fixed for 14 August 2026 and the annual report is to be posted on 18 July 2026. The company's processing unit at Jaigad had been inspected by the Export Inspection Agency in February 2026 and samples were taken. On 3 July 2026 the Agency cancelled the unit's export approval with effect from the inspection date, citing the February samples, and the EU buyer rejected ₹ 11 crore of shipments that were in transit at the year end and recorded as sales. The CFO tells CA Farhan that "your report is signed; the auditor's job ended on 22 May; we will mention it in next year's accounts".

(a) Comment on the CFO's view and state CA Farhan's responsibilities. (b) What should he do if management amends the financial statements? (c) Would your answer change if the annual report had already been posted to shareholders on 1 July 2026?

Model answer (ICAI format): Provision: SA 560 — the auditor has no obligation to perform audit procedures after the date of the report. But when, after that date and before the FS are issued, a fact becomes known that, had it been known at the report date, may have caused the auditor to amend the report, the auditor shall (i) discuss with management and, where appropriate, TCWG; (ii) determine whether the FS need amendment; and (iii) if so, inquire how management intends to address it. If management amends, the auditor carries out procedures on the amendment, extends procedures, and gives a new report not dated earlier than the date of approval of the amended FS (or amends the report with an additional date restricted to the amendment). If management does not amend and the report is already with the entity, he notifies TCWG not to issue the FS to third parties before amendment; if issued anyway, he takes appropriate action to seek to prevent reliance on the report. Analysis: the cancellation relates to samples taken in February, and the shipments were in transit at the year end — it gives evidence of conditions existing at 31 March 2026 and would have changed the report. The FS are not yet issued (posting on 18 July). The CFO's view is wrong: the "respond" objective of SA 560 continues till issue. (b) Audit the amendment, extend procedures, and issue a new report dated on or after the approval of the amended FS, with an EOM/OM paragraph referring to the amendment. (c) After issue: discuss, decide on amendment, inquire. If amended, review the steps management takes to inform everyone who received the earlier FS and report, and issue a new report with EOM/OM. For FY 2025-26, the statutory auditors, CA Joseph & Co., are based in Mumbai and plan to complete most of the audit remotely. The CFO is keen to finish early because the company's export incentive claims of ₹38 crore are pending and the bankers want audited figures by 15 May 2026. He has offered the audit team "full admin access" to the ERP through a link e-mailed to the partner, and has said the team may record all video calls with staff and take screenshots as needed. The audit manager plans to keep all documents on a public file-sharing service "until the file is closed next year". The company also asks the team to rely on video tours to count 1,200 tonnes of pepper and cardamom in warehouses at year-end.

Advise CA Joseph & Co. on the key considerations for conducting the remote audit effectively and securely. Would your advice on the year-end stock count change if the warehouses were remote and hard to reach?

Model answer (ICAI format): Provision: As per the SM (Digital Auditing — Remote Audit), remote audit is where the auditor uses online or electronic means, partially or completely, to obtain audit evidence or review documentation with the auditee's participation. Auditors must develop tailored strategies so that results are equivalent to onsite audits. Considerations: (i) Feasibility and planning — agree timelines, meeting platform, data exchange mechanism and access authorisation requests; check technology, competencies and resources; evidence transferred through a document-sharing platform. (ii) Confidentiality, security and data protection — access to the sharing platform restricted and data encrypted; information once reviewed is removed from the platform and stored per archiving and data-protection requirements; consider laws that may require agreements (e.g. no recording of sound and images); no screenshots of auditees; screenshots of documents only if previously authorised; access to the auditee's IT system through VPN. (iii) Risk assessment — clear communication; risks to audit objectives identified and managed; whether remote audit is sufficient assessed and documented for each audit with the team and auditee representative. Facts: an e-mailed link with admin access is not a VPN and gives access beyond need; recording calls and screenshots of staff need prior agreement/are not to be taken; a public sharing service kept till next year breaches restriction and removal requirements. The SM also notes that remote audit increases the opportunity to present doctored documents, which may call for additional procedures. Stock count: SM lists among disadvantages that physical verification of assets and stock taking cannot be performed remotely; the answer does not change merely because warehouses are remote. SM separately mentions drone technology for stock counts at remote locations, combined with manual counts and bar-code scanners. Conclusion: the firm should use VPN-based, approved, limited access; encrypted restricted platform; authorised screenshots of documents only; document the sufficiency assessment; and not substitute remote video tours for stock taking.

Marks go for: feasibility and planning · encryption + VPN + no screenshots of auditees · documented sufficiency assessment · stock taking cannot be performed remotely.

Q 1 · 5 marks · ~9 min

Meghdoot Hospitals Ltd, a listed hospital chain in Hyderabad, plans to acquire 65% of Shravan Diagnostics Pvt Ltd (SDPL), which runs 42 pathology labs in Telangana and Andhra Pradesh. SDPL's FY 2024-25 financial statements carry an unmodified audit report, and its revenue grew from ₹96 crore to ₹151 crore in two years. The board of Meghdoot signed a term sheet on 3 July 2025. Its CFO says: "SDPL's accounts are already audited with a clean opinion. A due diligence is a waste of money. At most, let their statutory auditor do a quick second audit." The promoter of SDPL has sent five-year projections showing revenue of ₹410 crore by FY 2029-30, prepared on a single "expected" basis. Three of SDPL's five senior pathologists are on fixed-term contracts that end in March 2026, and SDPL's gratuity liability is not funded.

You are the engagement partner of the firm approached by Meghdoot. (a) Advise the board on whether the audited statements remove the need for due diligence. (b) Explain the matters a full-fledged financial due diligence would cover, applying them to the facts where relevant.

Model answer (ICAI format): Provision (SM Ch 17 paras 1–3): Due diligence is a measure of prudence activity, or assiduity, expected from and ordinarily exercised by a reasonable and prudent person in the circumstances, not measured by any absolute standard. It differs from audit: an audit is an independent examination of financial statements to express an opinion; due diligence examines a potential investment to confirm all material facts, reviewing financial and non-financial records. Detailed due diligence is done after in-principle agreement but before a binding contract. Reasons include confirming the business is what it appears to be, finding "deal killer" defects, and gaining information for valuation, representations and warranties, and price concessions. Analysis (a): SDPL's clean opinion only says the FS are true and fair. It does not look behind the veil of information for Meghdoot's investment decision, does not assess projections or future maintainable results, and does not identify hidden liabilities for the price and indemnity clauses. A "second audit" by the target's own auditor is therefore not a substitute. The term sheet stage (July 2025) is the right time. Analysis (b) — full-fledged financial due diligence covers: (1) history of SDPL and background of its promoter; (2) accounting policies, including recent changes and differences from Meghdoot's policies, with their effect; (3) review of FS — compliance with statute and AS, operating results over four to five years, extraordinary items, benchmarking, indicative valuation, and a search for hidden liabilities and over-valued assets; (4) taxation; (5) cash flow — whether growth is funded by internal accruals; (6) financial projections — ask for projections on optimistic, pessimistic and most likely bases, comment on assumptions, report if ₹410 crore is not achievable or aggressive, check arithmetic; (7) management and employees — whether key pathologists will continue after March 2026; the unfunded gratuity needs an actuarial valuation from a reputed actuary; (8) statutory compliance — list of laws and a checklist, with non-compliance quantified. Conclusion: The CFO's view is not correct. Meghdoot should commission a due diligence before signing the binding agreement, and the report should suggest indemnities or warranties for the unfunded gratuity and key-staff risk.

Marks go for: prudent-person definition · due diligence vs audit (opinion vs confirming material facts) · timing before binding contract · scope (a)–(h), with projections on three bases and actuarial valuation of unfunded gratuity.

Q 2 · 5 marks · ~9 min

Ambika Textiles Ltd, Surat, has a turnover of ₹640 crore. For eleven years, its Accounts Head, Mr. Bhavesh, has approved vendor bills, signed cheques up to ₹25 lakh, maintained the cash book and reconciled the bank. No one else reviews his work. In FY 2025-26, the new MD noticed that cash expenses of the yarn godown doubled, while wage sheets of contract labour show round-number totals every week. Several suppliers say they never received a few payments shown as made to them. The statutory auditor gave an unmodified opinion for FY 2024-25. The MD appoints you to investigate and says: "Rely on the audited accounts. Just check this year's vouchers on a sample basis, as an auditor would."

Comment on the MD's instruction. Explain what you will do before starting detailed checking, and how you will verify the cash payments.

Model answer (ICAI format): Provision (SM Ch 17 paras 9 and 11.4): If an investigation is launched because of doubt about the audited accounts, no question of relying on them arises. On 100% or selective checking: where the investigator must establish the amount defalcated, he has no option but to examine all relevant vouchers. Where selection is possible, statistically recognised sampling is safer than "test checks". The investigator seeks conclusive evidence and is not limited to prima facie evidence. Before starting: ascertain the exact duties of the suspected person, his relationship to the general office routine, and the circumstances in which the defalcation came to light. The greater the authority of the suspect, the wider the field of investigation. Study the line of responsibility and the internal control system to spot weaknesses, and direct enquiry to areas where there is excessive control in the hands of a single person without supervision — here, Mr. Bhavesh controls approval, cheque signing, cash book and reconciliation. Obtain written instructions on objectives, scope and period. Verification of cash payments: scrutinise all evidence of payments, including acknowledgements by parties. Where a receipt shows erased or altered figures, confirm the amount actually paid with the party; do the same for amounts acknowledged on blank paper. Examine all payments by bearer cheques. Review the wage recording system for over-totalling of wage sheets and dummy workmen. Review the ordering and receiving system to confirm no payment was made for supplies not received. Obtain confirmations from directors for amounts shown as paid to them. Vouch and total the petty cash book, with special attention to salaries and wages, and get management's confirmation that payments went to persons actually in service. Check all bank withdrawals with the pass book and all bills with the Bills Books. Because suppliers deny receiving payments, also obtain statements of account from all suppliers. Conclusion: The MD's instruction is not appropriate. Since the facts doubt the very accounts produced, the audited statements cannot be relied on, and the payments controlled by Mr. Bhavesh need complete, not sample, examination.

Marks go for: no reliance when investigation arises from doubt · 100% check to establish defalcation · duties of suspect and wider field for greater authority · cash payment verification points (altered receipts, bearer cheques, wage sheets, goods not received).

Q 3 · 5 marks · ~9 min

A consortium of four banks led by Bharat Commercial Bank declared Rajhans Gems Pvt Ltd's ₹310 crore exposure an NPA in January 2026. The lead bank appoints CA Firoz, a forensic professional, to examine possible diversion of funds through sister concerns and forex transactions that do not match imports and exports. The bank's GM says: "The statutory auditor already gave an unmodified opinion for three years. A forensic exercise is just a more detailed audit. Please give us an opinion, as auditors do, on whether the promoters are guilty, so that we can file a police complaint." Halfway through, Rajhans denies access to e-mail servers, citing a court order in a separate dispute.

(a) Is the GM right that forensic accounting is a more detailed audit? (b) How should CA Firoz deal with the GM's request for an opinion on guilt and with the denial of access in his report?

Model answer (ICAI format): (a) Provision (SM Ch 17 para 12): Forensic accounting is a separate mandate, designed mainly to support legal cases. It is not an audit. Statutory audit is a legal mandate, done only by a CA, to express an independent opinion on true and fair view through control and substantive tests on a sample, with professional scepticism, targeting material misstatements. Forensic accounting aims to discover facts and evidence. Its focus is narrow (validating transactions and balances), its approach is focused testing to confirm a suspicion, and it aims to identify the nature of the violation. It uses scrutiny, fact-finding and interviews, presumes neutrality, may look at seemingly immaterial transactions and is not limited to one accounting period. It examines all relevant transactions so that the evidence is suitable for a court. Its outcome is evidence presented to a court, which judges; no audit opinion is given. Audit procedures are designed to detect material misstatements, not immaterial frauds, and rely on samples and the audit trail. Conclusion (a): The GM is wrong. A clean audit opinion does not rule out diversion, and forensic accounting is a different engagement, not a more detailed audit. (b) Provision (FAIS 510, p.17.68–17.70): The report shall not express an opinion or pass any judgement on guilt or innocence. Determining culpability is an internal disciplinary process or a judicial process. The report can, at best, highlight circumstances and facts that may aid a stakeholder's decision or further a civil or criminal investigation. Assumptions and limitations — such as restricted or denied access to records because of court orders — must be listed as key elements of the report. Other key elements: title, addressee and distribution; scope and objectives; approach; executive summary; use of experts; statement of conduct under FAIS or material departures; findings with key evidence and sources; conclusions, if any. Conclusion (b): CA Firoz should decline to give an opinion on guilt. He should report the facts and evidence on fund diversion, which the bank may use to file its complaint, and disclose the denial of access to e-mail servers as a limitation.

Marks go for: separate mandate to support legal cases · point-wise forensic vs audit (objective, focus, approach, presumption, outcome) · FAIS 510: no opinion on guilt · limitations (denied access, court order) stated in report.

Q 4 · 5 marks · ~9 min

Vindhya State Road Transport Corporation bought 120 air-conditioned buses in FY 2023-24 at ₹62 lakh each, after open tender and with every sanction in order. Because the depots had no charging and maintenance bays, 70 buses stood idle for 19 months. The corporation also built a ₹9 crore guest house in the capital, used mainly by its directors' relatives. In FY 2024-25, the Chairman approved a ₹2.4 crore advertising contract with a firm owned by his nephew, within his financial powers. During the C&AG's audit, the MD argues: "Every rupee was spent under the rules and with sanction, so audit has nothing to say. Your norms are subjective, and you are judging us with hindsight."

Comment on the MD's argument, stating the kind of audit involved and its principles. Would your answer be different if the same transactions occurred in a private-sector company?

Model answer (ICAI format): Provision (SM Ch 15 para 8): Propriety audit verifies transactions on the tests of public interest, commonly accepted customs and standards of conduct. It brings out improper, avoidable or infructuous expenditure even when it was incurred in conformity with existing rules. A transaction may meet all the requirements of regularity audit and still be highly wasteful. The principles are: (i) expenditure not prima facie more than the occasion demands, with every official showing the vigilance a person of ordinary prudence shows with his own money; (ii) no authority sanctions expenditure that accrues directly or indirectly to its own advantage; (iii) funds are not used for the benefit of a particular person or group; (iv) apart from agreed remuneration, no other avenue is kept open to indirectly benefit management personnel, employees and others. To deal with subjectivity, the C&AG has developed norms of propriety for public expenditure. Decisions are judged with reference to the circumstances when they were taken, not hindsight; the auditor reconstructs those circumstances. Analysis: The idle buses are avoidable/infructuous expenditure arising from poor planning; the auditor's function is to see that expenditure is properly planned. The guest house benefits a particular group, breaching principle (iii) and possibly (iv). The advertising contract with the Chairman's nephew breaches principle (ii) (indirect advantage), even though it is within his powers. Compliance with rules answers only regularity. The C&AG's norms answer the subjectivity point, and the missing bays were known when the buses were bought, so this is not hindsight. Conclusion: The MD's argument fails; these are proper propriety audit observations. Would it change for a private company? Yes, in part. The SM says the norms of propriety for governmental transactions do not ipso facto apply to the private sector, whose objectives differ; each management may frame its own norms of propriety. The government norms could not be applied automatically; the auditor would assess against the entity's own objectives and norms.

Marks go for: propriety audit definition and "even though in conformity with rules" · four principles applied to each fact · C&AG norms and no hindsight · private sector: norms not ipso facto applicable.

Q 5 · 5 marks · ~9 min

The State of Malwa launched "Har Ghar Nal" in 2022 to give a piped water connection to every rural household by March 2026, with ₹4,800 crore of State and Central funds. The office of the Accountant General plans an audit covering 2022-23 to 2025-26. Newspapers report that many connections give water for less than an hour a day, that pipes were bought at rates 30% above a neighbouring State's, and that the number of connections per crore spent is lower than in other districts. The Director of the scheme says the department never fixed measurable targets: "That is audit's job; you should first set our indicators."

(a) Identify the audit and the issues it will address. (b) How should the Accountant General decide the audit criteria, and which methods may the audit use? (c) Comment on the Director's statement. (d) Would your answer change if the audit only checked whether the water cess collected followed the State notification?

Following C&AG guidelines it addresses: economy (pipes at rates 30% above a neighbouring State — best price, due time, quantity, quality); efficiency (input-output — connections per crore compared with other districts); effectiveness (extent to which objectives are achieved and intended vs actual impact — connections that give water for less than an hour). Q 6 · 5 marks · ~9 min

CA Nidhi audits the CFS of Orbit Engineering Ltd (listed, Ind AS) for FY 2025-26. Of its nine subsidiaries, six are audited by other auditors: total assets ₹2,350 crore, total revenues ₹1,720 crore and net cash outflows ₹44 crore, all before consolidation adjustments. Two of these six are in Germany and Vietnam; they prepare statements under local GAAP and are audited under local auditing standards. Orbit's management converted them to Ind AS. A seventh subsidiary, Orbit Solar Pvt Ltd (total assets ₹38 crore against consolidated total assets of ₹6,900 crore), is unaudited because its auditor resigned in April 2026. In their CARO 2020 reports on standalone statements, the auditor of subsidiary Orbit Castings Ltd qualified clause 3(vii)(a) and the auditor of Orbit's joint venture gave an adverse remark under clause 3(ix)(a). The Finance Director wants Nidhi to "repeat each CARO clause in the consolidated report and also qualify the opinion, since we used other auditors".

Guide CA Nidhi on reporting these matters in the auditor's report on the CFS, and draft the paragraph on the other auditors.

Model answer (ICAI format): Provision (SM Ch 13 para 8; SA 600, SA 706, SA 705): Where the parent's auditor is not the auditor of all components, he considers SA 600. If he refers to other auditors, the report discloses the magnitude of the portion they audited — aggregate rupee amounts or percentages of total assets, revenues and cash flows not audited by him — presented before permanent and current period adjustments. This reference is not a qualification; it indicates divided responsibility. For foreign components under a different GAAP, management converts the statements, and the principal auditor audits the conversion adjustments. For unaudited components, the auditor considers a possible modification using SA 705, weighing qualitative and quantitative factors. CARO 2020 (SM Ch 7) does not apply to the report on CFS, except clause 3(xxi). Analysis: (1) The six subsidiaries are dealt with in an Other Matter paragraph, not a qualification; the FD is wrong. (2) Nidhi must audit the Ind AS conversion adjustments of the German and Vietnamese subsidiaries. (3) Orbit Solar's ₹38 crore is about 0.55% of consolidated assets. Q 7 · 4 marks · ~7 min

Trident Agro Ltd (listed, Ind AS) prepares CFS for FY 2025-26. Management has excluded: (i) Sonal Seeds Ltd, in which Trident bought 52% in May 2025 "only to resell it", and of which it sold 30% in February 2026; and (ii) Kaveri Cold Storage Ltd, in which Trident holds only 9% of the voting power but, under a shareholders' agreement, appoints five of its seven directors. Management says: "Sonal was temporary and Kaveri is below 20%, so neither is our subsidiary." Trident's own intermediate subsidiary, Trident Agri Exports Ltd, has non-convertible debentures listed on the Singapore Exchange and wants to skip its own CFS because Trident files CFS.

As auditor of Trident's CFS, how would you deal with these matters, and what procedures will you perform to confirm that all components are included?

Model answer (ICAI format): Provision (SM Ch 13 paras 1.1, 5): Ind AS 110 has no exemption for "temporary control" or for "operation under severe long-term funds transfer restrictions". Consolidation begins when control is obtained and ceases when it is lost (para 20). The auditor verifies management's assessment of control, including control over the composition of the board, using Board minutes, shareholder agreements and technology agreements; de facto control is also considered. The s.129(3) exemption for an intermediate company needs all three conditions, including that its securities are not listed, or in the process of listing, on any stock exchange in India or outside India. Analysis: (i) Sonal Seeds must be consolidated from May 2025 until February 2026, when control was lost on the sale of 30%. The "temporary" plea fails under Ind AS. (ii) Through its control of the board's composition, Trident controls Kaveri despite only 9% voting power, so Kaveri is a subsidiary and must be consolidated. Exclusion for reasons not allowed by the framework requires the auditor to consider the effect on his report. (iii) Trident Agri Exports has securities listed outside India, so it does not meet condition (ii) and must prepare its own CFS. Completeness procedures: review prior-year working papers for known components; review the parent's procedures for identifying components; enquire about new and outgoing components; review investments of the parent and components; review JVs, joint arrangements and other arrangements not consolidated; review registers under ss.186 and 190; identify changes in shareholding; review post year-end Board minutes; document these procedures. Conclusion: Both exclusions are not in order. Unless corrected, the auditor considers modifying his opinion.

Marks go for: no temporary-control exemption; consolidate till control lost · board composition gives control despite 9% · listing "in India or outside India" defeats intermediate exemption · completeness procedures incl. ss.186/190 registers.

Q 8 · 5 marks · ~9 min

Pinnacle Retail Ltd, a listed company, appointed CA Tanvi as Chief Internal Auditor in June 2025. Her appointment letter says she will report to the CFO and will "lead the process re-engineering of the billing system" in FY 2025-26. The statutory auditors, M/s KVR & Co., face a tight deadline after sales fell 28% and two lenders recalled loans. The engagement partner asks CA Tanvi's team to give direct assistance by (i) evaluating whether management's use of the going concern assumption is appropriate, (ii) assessing the risks of material misstatement for inventory, and (iii) vouching 400 sample purchase invoices to goods received notes. He tells his article assistant: "Do not keep the internal audit team's working papers in our file; they are not our work."

Comment on (a) CA Tanvi's terms of appointment and (b) the engagement partner's plan and instruction.

Model answer (ICAI format): (a) Provision (SM Ch 16 paras 1, 3): The internal auditor must be free from undue influence, independent in mind and in appearance. The position and reporting line of the Chief Internal Auditor establish this. In a listed company, the internal auditor may be required to report directly to the Audit Committee instead of the CEO or CFO. The internal auditor should not review an activity for which he was previously responsible, and should not perform executive functions, so that a vested interest does not obscure objectivity. Advisory roles are allowed only if independence is not compromised. Analysis: Reporting to the CFO, whose function she audits, impairs independence; she should be outside the functions she audits and report to the Audit Committee. Administrative reporting to the MD/CEO is possible with Board approval. Leading the billing re-engineering is an operational responsibility. She may accept only a limited role for a short period and should not later audit that system. (b) Provision (SA 610, SM para 7.3–7.4): The external auditor shall not use internal auditors for direct assistance on procedures involving significant judgements, including assessing the risks of material misstatement and evaluating management's use of the going concern assumption. Before using direct assistance, he must evaluate threats to objectivity and competence, and obtain written agreements from the entity (internal auditors will follow his instructions without the entity's intervention) and from the internal auditors (confidentiality and reporting threats to objectivity). His documentation must include the evaluation of objectivity and competence, the basis for the nature and extent of work, who reviewed the work and when (SA 230), the written agreements, and the working papers prepared by the internal auditors who gave direct assistance. Analysis: Tasks (i) and (ii) cannot be assigned. Task (iii), a routine procedure involving limited judgement, may be assigned after the evaluation and the written agreements. The instruction to exclude the internal auditors' working papers is wrong. Conclusion: CA Tanvi's terms need revision to protect independence, and KVR must restrict direct assistance to task (iii) and keep the internal auditors' working papers in its documentation.

Marks go for: report to Audit Committee, not CFO · no executive/operational role · SA 610 bar on significant judgements (going concern, RMM) · written agreements · documentation (a)–(e) incl. IAs' working papers.
