1International capital budgeting — complexities (SM 1.1)

  1. Foreign cash flows must be converted into parent's currency
  2. Parent cash flows differ from project cash flows
  3. Remitted profits taxed in home and host country
  4. Effect of forex risk on parent's cash flow
  5. Changing inflation shifts the competitive environment
  6. Host-country restrictions on distribution of cash
  7. Initial investment in host country to benefit from release of blocked funds
  8. Political risk reduces chance of expected cash flows
  9. Host-country concessions / benefits raise profitability
  10. Terminal value hard to estimate (buyers have divergent views)

2Problems in foreign investment analysis (SM 1.2)

  • Forex risk: forecast host inflation; inflate local-currency cash flows; convert at spot × expected depreciation from PPP
  • Restrictions on profit transfer, depreciation charges, technical differences → project ≠ parent flows; diluted via internal transfer prices, overhead payments; adjust blocked funds at their opportunity cost
  • Taxes: two regimes, royalties / dividends / management fees, withholding tax, treaties, discrimination between remitted vs re-invested profit. Use higher of home / host tax rate; if feasible, acceptable under a more favourable regime; if not, look for other tax savings. MNCs cut tax via transfer pricing.

3Project vs parent cash flows (SM 1.3)

  • Project's own cash flows: must beat local competitors (else invest in local equity / bonds) — but foreign projects often replace imports, so comparison is hard; local evaluation avoids currency conversion
  • Parent's view: operating + financial flows actually remitted are the yardstick (dividends, debt service). Evaluate on net after-tax operating cash flows; don't mix financial flows into operating flows.

4Discount rate vs adjusting cash flows (SM 1.4)

  • Earlier MNCs raised the discount rate for political & FX risk. SM: not proper to combine all risks in one rate — political risk relates to future events that can't be foreseen now, so (SM's words) adjusting the rate for it "penalises early cash flows more than distant cash flows"; raising it for FX risk ignores favourable currency moves.
  • Better: adjust cash flows — convert remitted flows into certainty equivalents (probability + decision tree), discount at host or parent rate.

5Adjusted Present Value (SM 1.5)

APV = −I0 + Σ Xt ÷ (1 + k*)t + Σ Tt ÷ (1 + id)t + Σ St ÷ (1 + id)t

k* = unlevered cost of capital (operating CF) · Tt = tax saving from financing mix · St = before-tax interest subsidies · id = before-tax cost of debt (for the last two terms)

Value-additive: each component discounted at a rate matching its risk. Test basic viability first; if feasible stop, else add the other complexities.

Blocked funds: balances abroad that can't be remitted — direct (exchange control) or indirect (local / withholding taxes on remittance). If released for the project, they reduce the investment outlay.

May 2026 Q3(a) (ICAI): pick cheapest borrowing (forward by IRP), then APV with tax shield discounted at cost of debt.

7FCCBs (SM 2.1)

Convertible bond in a currency other than issuer's; regular coupon + principal, with option to convert into stock (via warrants activated at a price). Equity feature → lower coupon.

Advantages

(i) investor flexibility — convert or redeem (normally 3 years) · (ii) delayed dilution of equity / EPS · (iii) easily marketable — conversion upside + minimum fixed interest

Disadvantages

(i) exchange risk on interest → only low D/E, large forex earners opt · (ii) more debt + forex outgo · (iii) low rate (3–4%) but FX risk on interest & repayment if not converted; benefit only if growth lets shares be issued above current price

8ADRs & GDRs (SM 2.2–2.3)

  • ADR: issued in USA under stringent SEC rules; non-US company's shares deposited with custodian in home country; US$-denominated, traded like US securities; same rights as underlying holders
  • GDR: negotiable certificate in a currency not native to issuer, representing local-currency equity; mostly USD (some Euro / £); can trade in more than one currency, inside and outside US. Shares delivered to custodian → depository (eg Bank of New York) issues DRs. Indian issues took GDR form — marketed globally; fetched higher prices than domestic issues; no other term forex funding available.

Impact on Indian market (TYK Q2)

(i) market shifting from Bombay to Luxembourg (ii) arbitrage possible (iii) market no longer independent of the world (iv) retail investors sidelined — free pricing ends discounted issues. Large foreign inflow.

Markets

(i) mainly institutional investors (ii) emerging-market funds dominate (iii) FIIs switch from shares to GDRs (iv) UK, USA (QIBs), SE Asia (HK, Singapore), France, Switzerland

Characteristics: (i) economic benefits but no voting rights (ii) settled via CEDEL & Euro-clear (iii) listed on Luxembourg SE (iv) OTC trading by market makers (v) freely traded (vi) marketed globally, multi-currency (vii) dividend in issuer currency converted to $ → FX risk with investor (viii) cancel after 45-day cooling period; cost = BSE / NSE price on redemption-advice date.

Illus 4: need $10 m net, 2% float → gross $10.204 m; GDR = 2 sh × 250 × 90% = ₹450 = $7.50 → 1.3605 m GDRs; Ke = 40 ÷ 441 + 12% = 21.07%.

9Euro-convertible bonds & others (SM 2.4–2.5)

  • ECB: convert into predetermined shares (conversion premium), fixed interest, interest & redemption in US$. Call (issuer) — redeem early, forces conversion when share price ~130–150% of conversion price. Put (holder) — sell back at set price & date.
  • Euro-convertible zero coupon (no interest, converts at maturity, ~5 yrs, deferred equity) · Euro-bonds with equity warrants (market coupon, detachable warrants) · Syndicated bank loans (5–10 yrs, SOFR + spread, covenants) · Euro-bonds (currency issued outside its country, eg Yen bond in France; tax / regulation refuge; bearer — fixed, FRN, convertible) · Foreign bonds (in lender country's currency, sold there, restricted) · Euro CP (< 1 year, discount) · Credit instruments (rates differ by speed / risk)

10International Financial Centre (SM 3)

IFC caters to customers outside its own jurisdiction — hub for funds, products, services on own land under a different set of regulations; flexibility in currency trading, insurance, banking.

Benefits (5)

(i) professionals abroad can come and practise here (ii) global opportunities without leaving homeland (iii) stops brain drain (iv) brings back transactions now done abroad (v) complex derivatives can be traded from India

Constituents (6)

(i) highly developed infrastructure (ii) stable political environment (iii) strategic location (airport, seaport, weather) (iv) quality of life (v) rational, fair, transparent regulation (vi) sustainable economy absorbing shocks

  • GIFT City: idea 2007 (rival Dubai, Hong Kong) — retain financial business moving out of India. IFSC operationalised at GIFT Multi Services SEZ in April 2015; competitive tax regime in Budget 2016
  • India INX (BSE subsidiary) inaugurated 9 Jan 2017; NSE began at GIFT 5 June 2017 (equity, currency, IRF, commodity derivatives)
  • Low cost, competitive tax, single-window clearance, relaxed company law, international arbitration centre; moving to unified regulation. FEMA not applicable at GIFT City.

11Sovereign wealth funds (SM 4)

State-owned fund of government money, usually from surplus reserves; legal basis varies (constitutive law, fiscal law, constitution, company law, others).

Sources (5)Surplus from state natural-resource revenues & trade surpluses · bank reserves from budget excesses · foreign currency operations · privatisation money · government transfer payments
Objectives (8)Protect & stabilise budget / economy from revenue volatility · diversify from non-renewable commodity exports · beat returns on forex reserves · help monetary authority absorb unwanted liquidity · savings for future generations · fund social & economic development · sustainable long-term growth for target countries · political strategy
Classifications (5)Stabilisation · savings / future generation · public benefit pension reserve · reserve investment · strategic development SWF (SDSWF)
Sovereign vehicles (4)SWFs · public pension funds · state-owned enterprises · sovereign wealth enterprises (SWEs)

Risk management ranges from very conservative to high risk tolerance, depending on assets and objectives.

12Why international WC is complex (SM 5)

(1) wider financing options — local or global (2) interest & tax rate differentials (3) forex risk on flows and trade (4) home / host restrictions on cash & inventory movement (5) limited knowledge of host conditions (6) only with full capital convertibility can funds be positioned freely (not India).

13Multinational cash management (SM 5.1)

  • Traditional objectives: (1) manage & control cash (2) optimum utilisation & conservation of funds
  • Effective system objectives (5): minimise currency exposure · minimise overall cash need · minimise transaction costs · minimise political risk · economies of scale & superior knowledge
  • Centralised system helps: (a) minimum cash balance (b) manage centre's liquidity (c) optimal hedging (d) max returns on cash (e) multinational netting (f) transfer pricing (g) exploit currency correlations — receivables & payables with positive correlation; payables with negative correlation; pooling → group variance < sum of individual variances
  • Two basic objectives: optimise cash flow movements + invest excess cash. Six ways: accelerate inflows (lockboxes, pre-authorised payment) · manage blocked funds (local bank finance, transfer pricing, negotiation, leads / lags) · leading & lagging · transfer pricing · netting · invest excess cash (Euro-currency market, pooled deposits; weigh conversion costs)

Leading / lagging: adjust timing of intra-group payments on currency expectations — pay hard-currency payables early (lead), delay soft-currency payables (lag). Indian subsidiary paying Japanese one: expects ₹ to fall vs ¥ → lead; expects ₹ to strengthen → lag. Check host-country restrictions.

14Netting (SM 5.1.5)

Advantages: fewer cross-border transfers (admin cost) · less FX conversion (transaction cost) · better cash forecasting (net transfers at period end) · accurate reporting and coordinated settlement.

  • Bilateral: X buys $20 m from Y, Y buys $30 m from X → Y pays X $10 m only (not $50 m)
  • Multilateral: each affiliate nets all receipts against all payments; needs centralised communication + disciplined subsidiaries. P → Q → R → P, $50 m each → transfers fully eliminated

15International inventory (SM 5.2)

MNCs hold more than EOQ — stock piling — due to import bottlenecks (political) and expected depreciation of the importing country's currency. Decide by weighing cumulative carrying cost vs expected input price rise; if supply interruption is very likely, stockpile even if costly. Longer lead times + customs → re-order point much earlier; reliance on imports largest in a vertical set-up.

16International receivables (SM 5.3)

  • Inter-firm sales: exporter wants a strong currency, importer a weak one; exporter may invoice in a weak currency if it has debt in that currency (natural offset). Weak-currency invoice → short credit; liberal credit lets exporter borrow against bills, or is forced by competition (financing importer's inventory) → more sales.
  • Intra-firm sales: global allocation of resources; timing doesn't matter to the group — cash-rich unit pays early, cash-short pays late → leads & lags.
