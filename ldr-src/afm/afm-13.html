Ch 13 — Business Valuation

SM TYK theory Qs: EVA vs MVA · relative valuation is a "relative" value by comparative analysis — elaborate. Numericals: NAV / NRV, P/E & capitalisation, DCF + TV, asset beta, relative (EBITDA multiple, EV), chop-shop, EVA, SVA, LBO, distressed firms, startup methods.

1Conceptual framework & terms (SM 1–2)

  • First corporation valued / IPO: Dutch East India Company (United East India Company). Valuation = science + art; part of strategic FM
  • Purposes (5): (a) information for internal stakeholders (b) comparison with similar enterprises — management efficiency (c) future public listing (d) strategic planning — value drivers, deploying surplus cash (e) ball-park price for acquisition
  • PV (₹1,000 in a year at 10% ≈ ₹909) · IRR (rate making NPV zero; higher IRR more likely selected) · ROI: (1,400 − 1,000) ÷ 1,000 = 40%; with additions use average investment: 1,000 + 200 during year, value 2,000 → 800 ÷ 1,100 = 72%; historical ratio — course-correct only
  • Gordon perpetual growth: little used by investors — more parameters needed; dividends rarely grow steadily; "darling of academicians". Terminal value = "horizon value" at exit, usually by the Gordon model

2Asset-based approach (SM 3.1)

For M&A: unlisted → negotiated price; listed → market cap is a guide, but depends on market efficiency and may reflect only a small quoted portion. Three approaches: asset, earnings, cash flow (+ others). Asset approach values income-generating assets, not income; least useful for IT companies (IPR, people).

MethodSM
Net asset value (book value)Fixed assets + net current assets − long-term debt. Simple (historical cost) but balance sheet isn't a valuation device → lower limit; ignores intangibles (brand, IPR)
Net realisable value (liquidation / adjusted book value)Realisable value of assets − liquidation expenses − liabilities (expenses ignored if bought as going concern); buyer offers lowest prices; implies break-up; useful when acquirer will sell one part and integrate the rest. SM eg: NRV 1,05,000 ÷ 5,000 shares = ₹21
Replacement valueCost of equivalent assets & liabilities in the open market; usually slightly above NRV; seen as the maximum an acquirer would pay; ignores staff loyalty

Conclusion: shows net worth fairly on going-concern basis but ignores future revenue-generating ability and market dynamics.

Ch 13 · Income & cash-flow approaches, cost of equityFORMULA CORE

3Income-based approach (SM 3.2)

For acquirers continuing the business; include extra earnings from the acquisition.

  • P/E (earning-yield multiplier): price = EPS × P/E; for listed cos; captures expected growth & market expectations; for unlisted use peer / industry P/E. Gives the minimum acceptable price to target's holders. Steps: (i) P/E of equivalent quoted company (ii) adjust down for non-listing risk (iii) future maintainable EPS (iv) multiply
  • Capitalisation of earnings: value = expected annual maintainable profit (weighted average of past profits, adjusted for synergy / scale) ÷ capitalisation rate (required earning yield = EPS ÷ price = 1 ÷ P/E). Forward-looking; but future profit hard to estimate, extraordinary items treated differently

4Cash-flow (DCF) approach (SM 3.3)

Free cash discounted to match risk; avoids over-reliance on history. 5 steps: (a) free cash flows (b) forecast (c) discount rate from cost of capital (d) terminal value (e) PV of FCF + TV, interpret.

SM eg: EAT 600 − one-time income 200 + one-time expense 100 + depreciation 100 = FCF 600; grow 5%, less ΔWC & capex → 500 / 550 / 651.50; at 8% PV 463 / 471.35 / 517.29; TV = 517.29 × 1.03 ÷ (0.08 − 0.03) = 10,656.17; total 12,107.81. Note: SM grows the year-3 PV, so this TV figure is already a present value. TV is often the major part of value → validate g and WACC.

5Cost of equity (SM 4)

  • CAPM: R = rf + β(rm − rf) — popular but over-simplifies risk into two buckets
  • APT (Stephen Ross, 1970s): multiple macro factors, each with its own β: Rf + β1RP1 + β2RP2 + … + βnRPn; APT doesn't name the factors — analysts choose them

6Asset beta & unlisted firms (SM 4.3)

Private firms (often little debt) and a listed firm entering a new line of business shouldn't use their own WACC → use asset (ungeared) beta = business risk only.

βa = βe × E ÷ [E + D(1 − t)] + βd × D(1 − t) ÷ [E + D(1 − t)]; with βd = 0: βa = βe × E ÷ [E + D(1 − t)]; regear: βe = βa × [E + D(1 − t)] ÷ E. βe ≥ βa (equal if debt-free)

Steps: (1) find pure-play (proxy) firms and their βe (2) de-gear each (3) one proxy → use it; several → average (or most appropriate) (4) re-gear at own capital structure (5) CAPM → required return / value

Illus 1 (X Pvt Ltd): βa 1.8, D:E 40:60, t 30% → βe = 1.8[1 + 0.7 × 40/60] = 2.64; Ke = 5 + 2.64 × 6 = 20.84%; WACC = 20.84 × 0.6 + 12 × 0.7 × 0.4 = 15.864%; FCF 100/120/150 → value 272.15. EBITDA 90 − extraordinary gain 10 − pending write-off 20 = 60 × 5 = EV 300.

7Relative valuation (SM 5) — TYK Q2

  • Asset / earnings / cash-flow approaches give intrinsic value; relative valuation gives a "relative" value by comparison with peers — "valuation by multiples"; increasingly used to validate intrinsic value; aims at a value near market price (eg bond YTM close to the government index is more credible)
  • 4 steps: (1) find the drivers for the multiple (2) compute via financial ratios (3) find comparable firms & compare (4) iterate to smooth deviations
  • Step 1: EV multiples — EV/EBITDA (popular), EV/invested capital (capital-intensive), EV/sales (cash-rich, big order book, organic growth) · equity multiples — P/E (celebrated), PEG = P/E ÷ growth (high-growth / sunrise industries)
  • Step 2: right ratio — exporter's value swings with FX (P/E misses Brexit-type shocks); EV/invested capital misfits asset-light firms or those with investment property
  • Step 3 (hardest): no two firms alike — X (FTE pricing) vs Y (unit transfer pricing) differ in risk; niche firms → look across industries or regression; "take benchmarks with a pinch of salt"
  • Assumptions (3): (i) market is efficient (ii) fundamentals–multiples link is linear (iii) comparables similar in structure, risk & growth. EV via entity value adjusted for debt, or balance-sheet approach

8Contemporary approaches (SM 6.1)

  • Industry-specific metrics: price per page visited (internet), price per subscriber (online store) — still check cash-to-sales and DCF
  • Goodwill-based: asset value + goodwill as a multiple of sales or footfall (retail giant buying a mom-and-pop store)
  • PER: EPS 40, average price 50 → 1.25 (relative figure; use sector median)
  • LBO by PE firms: buy with ~70 : 30 debt : equity, sell in 3–5 years. SM eg: EBITDA 100 → … Y3 EBDAT 112 × exit multiple 7 = 784 − debt 100 = equity 684 (SM shows Y3 EBITDA 178; 20% growth on 144 would give 172.8). Risk: cyclical slowdown leaves heavy debt

Ch 13 · Chop-shop, EVA, MVA, SVATYK THEORY Q1

9Chop-shop / break-up (SM 6.2)

Find undervalued multi-industry firms worth more split up — buy assets below replacement value. Steps: (1) identify segments; average capitalisation ratios of pure-play firms (2) theoretical value per ratio (3) average the theoretical values. Illus 4 (Cornett, market €4 m): cap/sales €4,005,000 · cap/assets €2,880,000 · cap/operating income €5,650,000 → average €4,178,000 > €4 m.

10Economic Value Added (SM 6.3)

  • Holistic: valuation and economic contribution; value is created only if returns exceed the cost of capital; separates operations from financing. Negative EVA = destroying value
  • Why EVA: others are number-crunching; EVA makes management accountable for decisions (eg rejecting a positive-IRR but slower-payback project shows up)

EVA = NOPAT − (Invested capital × WACC) = NOPAT − capital charge. NOPAT = EBIT − tax (depreciation stays as operating expense) + non-cash items (eg bad-debt provision). Invested capital = total assets − non-interest-bearing current liabilities (creditors, advance billing) + non-cash adjustment = equity + long-term debt, generally at start of year.

Illus 5: EBIT 410 − tax 123 + provision 20 = NOPAT 307; IC = 1,300 − 400 + 20 = 920; WACC = 800/900 × 8.45% + 100/900 × 12% × 0.7 = 8.44%; charge 77.65 → EVA 229.35.

11Market Value Added (SM 6.4)

MVA = current market value of the firm − invested capital (eg 1,000 − 920 = 80). Addresses EVA issues (ignoring value drivers, book value); no basis for share value but gauges efficiency from market-cap view — value perceived by the market. EVA = derived value for discerning investors. High-MVA firms become market darlings / pricey; EVA and MVA may be briefly negatively correlated (eg ex-dividend) — gap closes.

12Shareholder Value Analysis (SM 6.5)

NOPAT is historical and misses future investment opportunities → SVA uses value drivers (sales potential, investments, cost of incremental capital). Steps: (a) FCF from value drivers (b) discount at WACC (c) add TV (d) add market value of non-core assets & marketable investments (e) less debt = equity. SM: PVs 57.54 + 43.46 + 44.86 + TV (40.45 × 1/0.12 = 336.95) = 482.81 + investment property 35 − debt 19 = 498.81.

Ch 13 · Fair value, going concern, distressed firmsTHEORY + FORMULA

13Fair value (SM 7)

Buy at fair price — "no less no more". CA: arm's length; analyst: PV in cash; speculator: arbitrage. Booms defy fair value (dot-com). No single correct method → give a range (seller's minimum, buyer's maximum); deal by negotiation. Exam approach: (i) value by as many methods as data allows (ii) comment on each (iii) add any further information to the conclusion.

14Going vs non-going concern (SM 8)

Going concern assumed (else different basis, disclosed). Non-going concern = liquidation value (assets sold, liabilities paid). Going-concern value = total value — usually higher: includes future profits, intangibles, goodwill (premium); liquidation also means lay-offs and reputation damage. Use non-going concern only when the firm has no value as a going concern.

15Distressed companies (SM 9)

Distress = unable / struggling to pay creditors (high fixed costs, illiquid assets, cyclical revenue); can spread to operations; still may have value. Why conventional methods fail: TV assumes infinite life and growing CF (negative CF here) · negative, declining revenue — CF hard to estimate, DCF values a going concern, may give negative equity · discount rates assume sound firms → adjust for failure probability.

MethodSM
Modified DCFProbability distribution of CF across outcomes, adjusted for default; Ke from updated D/E + unlevered β; Kd from updated default risk. Short-cut: Expected CFt = CFt × (1 − probability of distresst)
DCF + distress value(i) value as going concern on the path back to health (ii) probability of distress (iii) distress sale value (% of book or DCF value). Equity = DCF equity × (1 − p) + distress sale value × p
APVFirm value = unlevered value + (tax benefits of debt − expected bankruptcy cost); unlevered = FCFF at unlevered Ke; expected bankruptcy cost = (unlevered value − distress sale value) × p
RelativeRevenue & EBITDA multiples (P/E, P/BV often unusable); adjust down subjectively — telecom avg 2× revenue → 1.25× for a distressed firm

16Startups (SM 10)

Standard approaches (income, asset, market) presuppose an established, cash-generating business; startups have no history, value rests on untested growth and founders ("walking in the dark … night-vision goggles"). Why traditional methods fail: income — no / minimal positive CF · asset — mostly IP / intangibles, and going concern so not just realisable value · market — disruptors without established competitors (some market elements used in later rounds).

Value drivers: product (ready / prototype vs idea; market testing) · management (credentials, balance — over half of Indian unicorns have IIT / IIM founders) · traction · revenue streams · industry attractiveness (tourism amid lockdowns) · demand-supply of investors · competitiveness (first-mover; Ola after Uber).

MethodSM
Berkus (Dave Berkus)5 success factors — basic value · technology · execution · strategic relationships · production & sales; caps $2 m pre-revenue, $2.5 m post-revenue; simple, ignores market factors

17Digital platforms (SM 11)

  • Software infrastructure for many-to-many interactions: marketplace (Booking.com, Uber, Amazon) · search (Google, Bing, Baidu) · repository (Spotify, YouTube, GitHub) · communication (WhatsApp, Teams, Telegram, Slack) · community (Facebook, LinkedIn) · payments (Paytm, GPay)
  • Income: top-down — TAM → SAM → SOM, time to reach share (ambitious for nascent firms) · bottom-up — from limited resources. Cash burn early. FCFF at WACC, FCFE at Ke; beta hard (few comparables) → add company-specific risk premium (CSRP / alpha), higher for nascent platforms
  • Market: comparables scarce, profits / EBITDA negative, capital-light (low BV) → compare on revenue drivers (bookings, users, merchants, views per user, search speed) · Cost: hours of coding — ignores revenue capacity

Professional / consultancy firms (SM 12): compare historical data with industry KPIs and competitors (audited statements, tax returns); factor risk in projected growth (TV); normalise income and cash flows (add back non-cash and firm-specific items).

18Impact of ESG on valuation (SM 13)

  • E: climate change, water, waste, emissions, biodiversity · S: employee development, diversity & inclusion, community, health & safety, customer · G: board independence & diversity, anti-bribery, tax transparency, ethical conduct
  • Developments: ESG funds > $50 bn in 2020; ESG assets > $35 trillion · green bonds crossed $1 trillion (2020) · sustainability taxonomies (EU) · IFRS single global ESG standard · SEBI proposed ESG disclosure framework (Feb 2023)
  • High ESG → cheaper debt, access to green / social / sustainability-linked bonds; ESG moved from "good to have" to "must have"
  • In valuation: adjust the discount rate (practical, less explicit) or cash flows (more explicit): E — 2-degree scenario analysis, carbon points · S — cost of labour conditions, CSR, welfare · G — penalties, fines, taxes from poor governance
