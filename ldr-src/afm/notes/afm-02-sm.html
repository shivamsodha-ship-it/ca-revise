1. Identification of types of risk faced by an organization

  • Strategic · Compliance · Operational · Financial (counter-party, political, interest rate, currency, liquidity).

1.1 Strategic Risk

  • Company's strategy becomes less effective and it struggles to achieve its goal; even the best-laid plan becomes outdated if it cannot keep pace with latest trends.
  • Causes: technological changes, new competitor, shift in customer demand, rise in raw material costs, other large-scale changes.
  • SM examples: Kodak (developed digital camera by 1975, treated it as a threat, left behind); Nokia (late to touch screen, Samsung became leader). Positive: Xerox moved from photocopiers to laser printing and survived.

1.2 Compliance Risk

  • Every business must comply with rules (e.g. Companies Act, 2013, SEBI guidelines); non-compliance → fine and imprisonment.
  • Real problem: venturing into a new business line or new geographical area (e.g. cement company entering sugar in another state, with different laws). Failure to comply with the new area's laws threatens survival.

1.3 Operational Risk

  • Internal risk; failure to cope with day-to-day operational problems; relates to people as well as process.
  • Example: employee pays out ₹ 1,00,000 instead of ₹ 10,000. Control: another person to check the work, or an electronic system to flag unusual amounts.

1.4 Financial Risk

  • Unexpected changes in financial conditions: prices, exchange rate, credit rating, interest rate etc.
  • Political risk is not a financial risk in the direct sense, but included: an unexpected political change abroad → country risk → financial loss.

1.4.1 Counter Party Risk

  • Non-honouring of obligations by the counter party: failure to deliver goods for payment already made (or vice-versa), or non-repayment of borrowings and interest.
  • Also covers credit risk, i.e. default by the counter party.

1.4.2 Political Risk

Faced mainly by overseas investors: adverse action by the host-country government. Forms:

  • Confiscation or destruction of overseas properties.
  • Rationing of remittance to home country.
  • Restriction on conversion of local currency into foreign currency.
  • Restriction as to borrowings.
  • Invalidation of patents.
  • Price control of products.

1.4.3 Interest Rate Risk

  • Change in interest rate → change in assets and liabilities.
  • More important for banking companies: balance sheet items are interest sensitive and earnings are the spread between borrowing and lending rates.
  • Risk is inherent in both fixed and floating rates: floating-rate borrower's liability rises when floating rate rises, while a fixed-rate liability stays the same; when floating rate falls, the fixed-rate borrower's interest is comparatively higher.

1.4.4 Currency Risk

  • Affects organisations dealing in foreign exchange: cash flows change with exchange rate movement, adversely or favourably.
  • Rupee depreciates vs US$ → exporter's receivables gain (Infosys); importer paying in US$ loses (Indian Oil Corporation Ltd.).

1.4.5 Liquidity Risk

  • Inability to meet liabilities when they become due.
  • Arises when the organisation is unable to generate adequate cash, or there is a mismatch in period of cash flow generation.
  • More prevalent in banking: mismatch in maturities and pattern of receiving fresh deposits.

2. Evaluation of Financial Risk

StakeholdersEquity shareholders view financial gearing (debt in capital structure) as risk, since on winding up they are least prioritised. For a lender too, existing gearing is a risk: high gearing → more risk of default in interest and principal.

3. Value-at-Risk (VAR)

  • A measure of risk of investment: given normal market conditions over a set period (say one day), estimates how much an investment might lose. Investment can be a portfolio, capital investment, foreign exchange etc.
  • Answers two questions: (i) What is the worst case scenario? (ii) What will be the loss?
  • First applied in 1922 in New York Stock Exchange; entered the financial world in 1990s; now the world's most widely used measure of financial risk.

3.1 Features of VAR

  • Components of calculation: (a) time period, (b) confidence level — generally 95% and 99%, (c) loss in percentage or amount.
  • Statistical method: statistical tool based on standard deviation.
  • Time horizon: one day, one week, one month and so on.
  • Probability: assuming values are normally distributed, probability of maximum loss can be predicted.
  • Risk control: by setting limits for maximum loss.
  • Z score: how many standard deviations a value is away from the mean; Z × SD = VAR.

3.2 Application of VAR

  • To measure the maximum possible loss on any portfolio or trading position.
  • As a benchmark for performance measurement of any operation or trading.
  • To fix limits for individuals dealing in the front office of a treasury department.
  • To enable management to decide trading strategies.
  • As a tool for Asset and Liability Management, especially in banks.

4. Appropriate methods for identification and management of financial risk

SM takes each financial risk category separately (identification → management).

4.1 Counter Party Risk

Hints (identification)Techniques to manage
  • Failure to obtain necessary resources to complete the project/transaction.
  • Any regulatory restrictions from the Government.
  • Hostile action of foreign government.
  • Let down by third party.
  • Have become insolvent.
  • Due diligence before dealing with any third party.
  • Do not over commit to a single entity / group / connected entities.
  • Know your exposure limits.
  • Review limits and credit-approval procedure regularly.
  • Rapid action on any likelihood of default.
  • Use of performance guarantee, insurance or other instruments.

4.3 Interest Rate Risk

Identified mainly from:

  • Monetary policy of the Government.
  • Any Government action such as demonetization.
  • Economic growth.
  • Release of industrial data.
  • Investment by foreign investors.
  • Stock market changes.

Management: covered in the later interest-rate risk chapter.

4.4 Currency Risk Asked: Sep 2025 Q4(c) [or] 4m

Depends on Government action and economic development. Parameters to identify:

  • Government action: visual impact on currency (e.g. Brexit took the pound to its lowest since the 1980s).
  • Nominal interest rate: per interest rate parity (IRP), exchange rate depends on the country's nominal interest rate.
  • Inflation rate: purchasing power parity theory — inflation impacts currency value.
  • Natural calamities: negative impact.
  • War, coup, rebellion etc.: far-reaching impact on exchange rates.
  • Change of government and its attitude towards foreign investment.

Management: covered in the later foreign exchange risk chapter.
