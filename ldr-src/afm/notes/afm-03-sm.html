1. Current trends in capital budgeting

  • Intermediate level assumed no risk, cash flows certain. In practice projects are exposed to: (i) inflation, (ii) change in technology, (iii) change in Government policies.

1.1 Impact of inflation on capital budgeting decisions

Inflation raises revenues and costs. Two distortions:

  • (1) Depreciation is on historical cost → tax shield on depreciation does not keep parity with inflation. Effect: inflation reduces the actual (real) rate of return.
  • (2) Cost of capital contains a premium for anticipated inflation → if k is nominal, cash flows must also be inflated; if no inflation, discount at the real rate.

TYK T3 — Project appraisal under inflation: costs rise on all heads (labour, material, fixed assets, remuneration); purchasing power and demand fall; lenders revise rates. Guidelines (used by Govt agencies, banks, FIs):

  • (i) Provide for cost escalation on all heads, for the likely period of delay in implementation.
  • (ii) Scrutinise sources of finance for probable revision of interest rates → cost of funds goes up.
  • (iii) Adjust profitability and cash-flow projections for inflationary pressures.
  • (iv) Examine financial viability at revised rates; economic rate of return should accommodate the inflation rate.
  • (v) Prefer projects with early payback — long payback projects are riskier.
  • Two approaches: (a) adjust each year's cash flows to an inflation index (price and cost increases); or (b) adjust the acceptance (cut-off) rate, keeping cash flows at current prices. ICAI example: normal 15%, inflation 5% → "15.0 × 1.05 or 15.75%". Measuring inflation has no standard approach.

Note (app-written): ICAI's TYK T3 example multiplies the rate (15 × 1.05 = 15.75%). On the SM's own Illus 1 logic the inflation-adjusted rate would be (1.15)(1.05) − 1 = 20.75% (or 15 + 5 = 20% by RN = RR + P). Keep ICAI's text for theory; for numericals use the Illus 1 / TYK P18 method.

1.2 Impact of change in technology

Why analyse (qualitative factor that affects future cash flows):

  • Can significantly alter the production process.
  • Benefits: improved quality, delivery time, flexibility.
  • Can reduce cost of capital; can improve cash inflows.
  • May need additional capital expenditure.
  • Sales volume hit as product life cycle shortens (consumer preference).

How to incorporate (review the project continuously):

  • 1. At decision time, consider technology risk by sensitivity, scenario, simulation analysis.
  • 2. After launch, analyse positive/negative impact and revise estimates in money terms.
  • 3. If unviable, evaluate the abandonment option.
  • 4. Suitably adjust the discount rate.

1.3 Impact of change in Government policies

  • External factor; affects cash flows directly or through suppliers, buyers, customers, service providers. Impact can be +ve or −ve → revise estimates; if needed consider abandonment.
  • Some policies change over 5–10 years (e.g. New Industrial Policy 1991); others within a year:
  • Fiscal policy — government spending, taxation and borrowing to influence economic activity, aggregate demand, output, employment.
  • Monetary policy — central bank instruments to regulate availability, cost and use of money and credit (open market operations, reserve requirements, selective credit controls, currency supply, bank/repo rates). MPC meets at least four times a year → changes 2–3 times a year.

1.3.1 Domestic capital budgeting

  • (a) Interest rates (monetary policy) → cost of debt / YTM → cost of capital. Long-term projects less affected, but working capital (bank overdraft) is; small change ignored, a major change (about 100 basis points) calls for revising cash flows.
  • (b) Fiscal policy → tax rate and depreciation rate → annual cash flows; drastic change → revise estimates.

1.3.2 International capital budgeting

  • (a) Bank rate and money supply changes move the foreign exchange rate → revise estimates.
  • (b) Change in tax on foreign income or DTAA provisions (fiscal policy) → revise estimates.
  • Conclusion: forecast policy changes and build a provision into expected cash flows.

2. Dealing with risk in investment decisions

  • (i) Certainty — cash flows certain.
  • (ii) Risk — cash flows risky and probability can be assigned.
  • (iii) Uncertainty — probability cannot be assigned.

2.1 Risk and uncertainty

  • Risk = variability of actual returns vs estimated returns; measured mostly by SD and coefficient of variation. Risk: probability distribution known. Uncertainty: no information to form it. Terms used interchangeably.

2.2 Reasons for adjusting risk

  • 1. Opportunity cost for the level of risk — are returns proportionate to risk, and better than other options?
  • 2. To know the real value of cash inflows — higher risk → higher risk premium → higher expected return.

4.2 Conventional techniques — 4.2.1 Risk-adjusted discount rate (RADR)

  • Investors demand higher returns from riskier projects. Required return = compensation for delaying consumption + inflation (= risk-free rate) + compensation for risk. Riskier than a similar project → adjust discount rate upward.
  • Risk-free rate: return on investments bearing no risk (e.g. Govt securities 6%). Risk premium: return over risk-free rate for bearing extra risk; depends on investor's risk perception and risk aversion.
FormulaTerms / logicSM example figures
RADR = Risk-free rate + Risk premiumk used in the numerator-unchanged NPV.Illus 7: 7% + 7% = 14%.
NPV = Σ NCFt ÷ (1 + k)t − INCFt net cash flow; k = RADR; I initial investment.Illus 7: NPV at 7% 144.34; at 14% 99.79 lakhs.
Required Rate of Return = IRf + (ko − IRf) Risk FactorICAI TYK P16 (on the basis of CAPM): IRf risk-free rate, ko firm's minimum required rate of return, Risk Factor = project's risk index.TYK P16: 10% + (15% − 10%) × 1.80 = 19%.
  • Advantages: (1) easy to understand; (2) incorporates risk premium in the discounting factor.
  • Limitations: (1) difficulty in finding risk premium and RADR; (2) NPV can be found but not the SD of the project.

4.2.2 Certainty equivalent (CE)

  • CIMA: express risky future cash flows as the certain cash flow the decision maker considers equivalent — indifferent between the risky amount and the (lower) riskless amount. Guaranteed return management would accept instead of a higher uncertain one; incorporates the decision maker's utility function.
  • Coin example: win 10,000 on head, nil on tail → EV 5,000; indifferent at certain 3,000 → CE 3,000, coefficient 3,000 ÷ 10,000 = 0.3.
FormulaTerms / logicSM example figures
αt = Certain cash flow ÷ Risky or expected cash flowCE coefficient lies between 0 and 1; 1 = certain cash flow or management risk-neutral. Higher risk → lower α.Illus 8: 0.90, 0.85, 0.82, 0.78.
NPV = Σ αt × NCFt ÷ (1 + k)t − Iαt risk-adjustment factor (CE coefficient); NCFt forecast net cash flow for year t without risk-adjustment; k = risk-free rate (constant); I initial investment. Risk already in numerator → using cost of capital = double counting.Illus 8: NPV ₹ 5,34,570.
  • Steps: 1. Multiply each risky CF by αt. 2. Discount at risk-less rate. 3. Apply normal method; under IRR, compare IRR with the risk-free rate, not the required return.
  • Advantages: 1. simple, easy to understand and apply; 2. easily applied to different risk levels for different cash flows (a riskier year can be adjusted and NPV recalculated).
  • Disadvantages: 1. no objective/mathematical method — CEs subjective; 2. decided on management's risk perception; shareholders' risk perception ignored → not used often.
  • RADR vs CE: CE is superior — does not assume risk increases with time at a constant rate; each year's α reflects that year's risk. But RADR is preferred in practice — difficult to specify a series of α's, simple to adjust the discount rate.
  • TYK T1 (short note): adjusts estimated cash flows, discounts at risk-free rate; converts expected flows to riskless amounts; greater the risk, smaller the CE for receipts and larger for payments; superior to RADR as it measures risk more accurately; adjusts cash flows not discount rates; explicitly recognises risk but the reduction procedure is implicit and may be inconsistent between investments.

4.3 Other techniques — 4.3.1 Sensitivity analysis Asked: Sep 2025 MCQ CS-I 1-3 6m

  • CIMA: modelling and risk-assessment procedure in which changes are made to significant variables to see the effect on the planned outcome; attention then on variables of special significance.
  • Change only one variable at a time (others constant). The more sensitive the NPV (or IRR), the more critical the variable.
  • Steps: 1. find variables that influence NPV/IRR; 2. establish mathematical relationship among them; 3. analyse effect of change in each on NPV/IRR.

4.3.2 Scenario analysis

  • Extends sensitivity: brings in probabilities of changes in key variables and changes more than one variable at a time.
  • Start with base case (most likely); then worst case (low sales, low price, high VC) and best case; or mix some +ve and some −ve changes. Examines impact of alternative combinations of variables on NPV/IRR.
  • Worst-case probability (ICAI TYK P7, P8): cash flows perfectly dependent (positively correlated) over time → = probability of the low flow in year 1 (TYK P7: 0.3); independent → that probability raised to the number of years (TYK P7: (0.3)5) (general rule app-written from ICAI's figures).

4.3.3 Simulation analysis (Monte Carlo)

  • Simulation = exact replica of the actual situation via a model; very many calculations give possible outcomes and their probabilities.
  • Monte Carlo ties together sensitivities and probability distributions; came out of the first nuclear bomb work; named after casino mathematics. Gives a probability distribution of NPVs, not a single point estimate.
  • Identify key factors and their inter-relationships; model cash receipts and payments; specify a range/probability distribution for each assumption.

4.3.3.1 Steps (TYK T4):

  1. Model the project — NPV as a function of parameters (set by decision maker, constant over all runs) and exogenous variables (stochastic, outside decision maker's control).
  2. Specify values of parameters and probability distributions of exogenous variables.
  3. Select a value at random from each exogenous variable's distribution.
  4. Determine NPV for the random values and pre-specified parameters.
  5. Repeat steps 3 and 4 a large number of times.
  6. Plot the distribution of NPVs; compute mean and SD to gauge risk.
  • Random-number mapping: cumulative probability → two-digit ranges (e.g. CF 10,000 p 0.02 → 00–01; 15,000 p 0.03 → 02–04; 20,000 → 05–19 …; life 3 yrs p 0.05 → 00–04; 4 yrs → 05–14; 5 yrs → 15–44 …).

SM example: NPV = Σ CFt/(1 + i)t − I; i 10%, I 1,30,000 are parameters; annual CF and life are exogenous. Ten runs; run 1: RN 53 → CF 30,000, RN 97 → 9 yrs, PVAF 5.759 → NPV 42,770. Other runs: 85,075; (8,300); (66,600); (21,125); 40,380; 16,040; 16,040; 21,640; 650.

Note (app-written): the SM text says RN 53 gives "₹ 3,000"; the table maps 53 to ₹ 30,000 and the run uses 30,000 (SM p.32).

  • 4.3.3.2 Advantages (strength lies in variability): (1) can predict bad market situations beforehand; (2) handles (a) numerous exogenous variables with any distribution, (b) complex inter-relationships among parameters, exogenous and endogenous variables that defy analytical methods, (c) compels the decision maker to consider inter-dependencies and uncertainties.
  • 4.3.3.3 Shortcomings: (1) difficult to model and specify distributions; (2) inherently imprecise — rough approximation; misleading when a tail is critical; (3) complex model built by management experts, not the decision maker, who may not use it; (4) NPV in each run uses the risk-free rate (to avoid pre-judging risk) → NPV takes a different meaning, hard to interpret.

4.3.4 Decision tree analysis

  • For sequential decisions — present investment affects future decisions and events. Decision tree = graphic display of relationship between a present decision, future events, future decisions and consequences.
  • Two situations: Decision — manager has control; Event — no control → assign probabilities to outcomes. Manager acts rationally (most profitable in business; lowest cost in non-profit).

Steps:

  1. Define investment — e.g. launch new product (local/national/international); extend factory or outsource; dig for oil and to what depth.
  2. Identify decision alternatives clearly.
  3. Draw the tree — cash flows, probabilities, expected PV on the branches. Decision point = square (action available); event/chance point = circle (with probabilities and values); drawn left to right.
  4. Evaluate — from the last decision (extreme right) back to the first (left): at each final decision point pick highest NPV, truncate others, assign that NPV to the point; compute expected NPV at chance points; continue to the first decision point.
  • Drawing rules: 1. begins with a decision node (rectangle); chance node = circle; 2. alternatives shown as straight lines from the decision node; 3. drawn left to right, rectangles and circles numbered sequentially; 4. add values and probabilities for each branch.
  • Evaluation (right to left): (a) EMV at a chance node = Σ (value × probability) of its branches; (b) value at a decision node = highest of its branches' expected values.
  • Joint probability of a path = product of probabilities along it (app-written from ICAI TYK P20 figures, e.g. 0.4 × 0.2 = 0.08); expected NPV = Σ path NPV × joint probability.

Illus 11 (L&R, ₹ lacs, 10%): pilot 2.40; success 50%. Decision C (success): invest 20 for perpetual 4 → 4/0.10 − 20 = +20 → invest. Decision D (failure): invest 12 for perpetual 1 → 10 − 12 = −2 → don't invest. Outcome B: EMV = 20 × 0.5 + 0 × 0.5 = 10. At A: spend 2,40,000 to test for EMV 10 lacs → undertake the test.

5. Replacement decision

  • One of the most important classes of capital budgeting; funds are limited, so investment in long-term assets must be budgeted. Two types: 5.1 replacement of existing machine; 5.2 optimum replacement cycle.

5.1 Replacement of existing machine

  • Replace with newer version or different machine of the same function — usually to maintain existing operations; profitability may change via expenses.
  • Use incremental cash flows: cash flows of the old asset disappear (e.g. new sales 1,00,000 vs old 75,000 → incremental 25,000); sale value of old asset reduces the effective price of the new (old sold for 3,50,000 → new costs 3,50,000 less).
  • Include depreciation (affects taxes) and taxes — both change.

5.2 Optimum replacement cycle

  • When the asset is replaced in a continuous cycle, simple NPV must be modified: NPVs of different periods are not comparable → use Equivalent Annual Cost (EAC). Choose the cycle with the lowest EAC.
  • Assumption: as the asset ages, efficiency falls → operating cost rises and resale value falls.

6. Adjusted present value (APV)

  • Instead of adjusting the discount rate (WACC) for risk, APV separates the investment decision from the financing decision.
  • APV = Base case NPV + PV of tax benefit on interest.
  • Base case NPV — discount at unlevered cost of equity (all-equity firm); computing it is covered in the Business Valuation chapter.
  • PV of tax benefit on interest — adjusts for how the project is financed → broader view including the benefit of using more debt.

Exam steps — inflation NPV: check if k is nominal or real and if CFs are nominal or real → match them (Nominal Cash Flow = (1 + Inflation Rate)t × Real Cash Flow, or Real Discount Rate = (1 + Nominal Discount Rate)/(1 + Inflation Rate) − 1) → if revenue and cost inflation differ, compound each separately → (R − C)(1 − T) + DT (shield not inflated) → discount → NPV.

Exam steps — ENPV, SD, CV: ENCF = Σ NCFi × Pi (each year) → discount → ENPV → σ2 = Σ (NCFj − ENCF)2 Pj → σ (multi-year: Hillier σ2 = Σ (1 + r)−2i σi2) → CV = σ ÷ mean → same mean: lower σ; different mean: lower CV.

Exam steps — RADR / CE: RADR = Risk free rate + Risk premium (or IRf + (ko − IRf) Risk Factor; or table by CV) → discount unadjusted CF. CE: NCFt × αt → discount at risk-free rate only → NPV; lower Σα = riskier = higher RADR.

Exam steps — sensitivity: base NPV → change one variable (given % adverse) → revised NPV → % change = (base − revised) ÷ base → highest % = most sensitive. Or NPV-zero method: % = NPV ÷ PV of that variable → lowest % = most sensitive. k: (IRR − X) ÷ X.

Exam steps — scenario / best-worst: NPV for worst, most likely, best → expected NPV with probabilities → σ, CV → worst-case probability: dependent = year-1 probability, independent = that probabilityn → compare with acceptable risk level.

Exam steps — decision tree: draw left to right (□ decision, ○ chance) → path NPVs → joint probability = product along path → roll back right to left: chance node = Σ value × p; decision node = highest → decide at first node.

Exam steps — replacement: tax on sale of old = (BV − MV) × t → net outflow = cost of new − (MV + tax saving) → Δ CF = [(ΔS ± ΔC) − ΔD](1 − t) + ΔD → PV of Δ CF + PV of new salvage → NPV → accept if > 0.

Exam steps — optimum replacement cycle: for each life n: PV of cost + running costs − PV resale at n → EAC = PV ÷ PVAF(k, n) → lowest EAC = optimum cycle. Replace now vs later: compare PV of each timing with new asset's NPV included.
