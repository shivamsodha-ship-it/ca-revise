Introduction

  • Buying a security: highest return per unit of risk or lowest risk per unit of return. Selling needs no such test. Attractiveness changes with management policy or government economic policy → analyse continuously.
  • Security analysis: systematic analysis of risk-return profiles so a rational investor can estimate a value from all price-sensitive information and buy when the market under-prices.
  • Two approaches: fundamental (factors affecting risk-return) and technical (demand/supply of securities and share price trends).

1 Fundamental Analysis

  • Assumes share price depends on future dividends expected. PV of future dividends at an appropriate discount rate = intrinsic value (true value).
  • Price below intrinsic value → buy; price above → sell.
FormulaTerms / logic
P0 = D/(1+k) + D/(1+k)2 + D/(1+k)3 + … (eq. 2); P0 = D ÷ k (eq. 3)Uniform dividend D every year; k = desired return on share. Perpetuity.
P0 = D1 ÷ (k − g) (eq. 5); P0 = D0(1+g) ÷ (k − g) (eq. 6)DPS grows at g p.a. (perpetual geometric series). D1 ≈ D0(1+g). Valid only if g < k; g ≥ k gives infinite price (not restrictive — long-term dividend growth unlikely to exceed k). Gordon's dividend growth model (Myron J. Gordon). g = 0 → back to eq. 3.

1.1 Dividend Growth Model and the PE Multiple

FormulaTerms / logic
P0 = bE1 ÷ (k − g) (eq. 7); P0 = bE0(1+g) ÷ (k − g) (eq. 8)b = dividend payout ratio, so D1 = bE1; E = EPS. Used to estimate the fundamental price or fundamental P/E multiple.
  • Prevailing price / P/E higher than fundamental value → overpriced → sell (price falls once information is common knowledge). Lower → underpriced → buy.
  • Industries are interlinked and tied to the economy; a company depends on its industry and its competitive position in it → fundamental analysis = economy → industry → company analysis (three concentric circles = stages of the investment decision).

1.2 Economic Analysis

  • Assess macro-economic factors: past/present performance and future expectations, sector growth, signs of stagnation. Trends in people's income and expenditure reflect future industry growth; consumption affects profits, dividends and share prices.

1.2.1 Factors Affecting Economic Analysis

  • Growth rates of national income: nominal GDP growth vs real growth (after inflation); pointer to returns from shares.
  • Growth rates of industrial sector: by industry, estimated from demand for products.
  • Inflation: WPI or CPI; affects demand esp. consumer products → firms fine-tune pricing, distribution, promotion.
  • Monsoon: strong forward and backward linkages.

1.2.2 Techniques Used in Economic Analysis TYK T4

Forecast national income and components; GNP measures national income and is a forecasting tool.

  • (a) Anticipatory surveys: expert opinion on construction, plant & machinery spending, inventory levels, consumer spending habits. Drawbacks: (i) intentions may not materialise; (ii) not forecasts per se (consensus approach). Needs continuous monitoring.
  • (b) Barometer / indicator approach:
    • Leading — reach high/low points in advance of economic activity.
    • Roughly coincidental — peaks and troughs at about the same time as the economy.
    • Lagging — turning points after the economy.
    • Limitation: show direction, not magnitude; may give conflicting signals → use a diffusion / composite index (several indicators in one); difficult to compute and does not eliminate irregular movements.
    • Money supply: affects GNP, profits, interest rates, stock prices; more money supply fuels inflation; stocks are a hedge against inflation → prices rise in inflation.
  • (c) Economic model building (GNP model / sectoral analysis, national accounting framework). Steps: (i) hypothesise total demand via GNP (political stability, inflation, economic levels); (ii) forecast components — consumption, gross private domestic investment, government purchases, net exports; (iii) add up to forecast GNP; (iv) compare with an independent agency's forecast and test for consistency.

1.3 Industry Analysis

Industries do not grow at the same rate → examine demand, cost structure, economic and government constraints; company profitability depends on its industry's prospects.

1.3.1 Factors Affecting Industry Analysis

  • Product life-cycle: high profitability in initial and growth stages, medium but steady in maturity, sharp decline in last stage.
  • Demand-supply gap: excess supply cuts unit price realisation and profit; insufficient supply improves it.
  • Barriers to entry: high profits attract entrants; barriers innate to product/technology or created by existing firms.
  • Government attitude: crucial determinant.
  • State of competition: leaders and nature of competition (foreign/domestic), homogeneous vs differentiated products, demand by customer/area, changes in demand pattern, type of industry — growth, cyclical, defensive, decline.
  • Cost conditions and profitability: (i) cost allocation (material, labour, overheads) and controllability; labour cost (wages, productivity); (ii) product price; (iii) capacity — installed, idle, operating; (iv) capex for maintenance/efficiency. Ratios: G.P., operating profit margin, ROE, return on total capital.
  • Technology and research: (i) nature of technology; (ii) expected changes → new products, sales; (iii) capex vs sales over time; (iv) R&D spend — redundant or not; (v) sales and profitability in short, immediate, long run.

1.3.2 Techniques Used in Industry Analysis

  • Regression analysis: product demand analysis — GNP, disposable income, per capita consumption/income, price elasticity; regression and correlation.
  • Input-output analysis: flow of goods/services through the economy from raw material to final consumption → detect growth/decline of industries.

1.4 Company Analysis

Examine the company's quantitative and qualitative fundamentals.

  • (a) Net worth and book value: NW = equity + preference capital + free reserves − intangibles − carried-forward losses; ÷ shares = book value. Not intrinsic worth because (1) market price reflects future earnings, not assets; (2) book value is historical cost, may underestimate replacement/resale value.
  • (b) Sources and uses of funds (funds flow analysis): check if short-term sources fund long-term investments → liquidity crunch risk (maturity mismatch).
  • (c) Cross-sectional and time series analysis: compare firms, against industry benchmarks, and over time — common-size statements, ratio analysis.
  • (d) Size and ranking: net capital employed, net profit, ROI, sales vs peers; technical know-how, R&D, price leadership → risk.
  • (e) Growth record: growth in sales, net income, capital employed, EPS. Three indicators: P/E ratio, % growth of EPS p.a., % growth of net block. Net block growth shows capacity build-up/diversification. Assess capacity utilisation (P&L quantitative info), expansion plans (Directors' Report, Chairman's statement, capital commitments), technology. Growth is the single most important factor in company analysis.

SM example (P/E): A — price 150, EPS 25 → P/E 6; B — price 250, EPS 25 → P/E 10. Other factors similar → A preferable. EPS cannot be the sole basis, but P/E relates market price to EPS.

Note (app-written): the SM then says to ascertain "why the EPS in company B is comparatively low", but both EPS are ₹ 25 in the SM table; it is B's P/E that is higher.

  • (f) Financial analysis: solvency, liquidity, fund-use efficiency, profitability, operating efficiency, financial and operating leverage. Key five: EPS, P/E, yield, book value, intrinsic value — for ~10 years, vs peers and industry averages. Yield and asset backing decide if price is proper. Solvency: current ratio, liquidity ratio, debt-equity.
  • (g) Competitive advantage: drives long-term success; shield against competitors (SM: Apple's brand, Samsung's mobile domination).
  • (h) Quality of management: intangible; business houses command premium; experience, skills, integrity; dividend/bonus policy. Small investors can't meet top executives. Clues: conference calls (CEO/CFO Q&A), MD&A report, management holding shares (large unloading while saying otherwise = warning), past 5-year performance of executives.
  • (i) Corporate governance: compliance with SEBI (LODR) Regulations 2015; how policies serve stakeholders; quality and timeliness of disclosures; quality independent directors.
  • (j) Regulation: Companies Act, SCRA, SEBI Act; SEBI monitors listed companies; FEMA compliance for dealings outside India, monitored by RBI.
  • (k) Location and labour-management relations: power, skilled labour, raw materials, nearness to markets; state of labour relations.
  • (l) Pattern of existing stock holding: stakes of parties (SM case: PNB nationalisation — institutional investors' bargaining raised compensation for opting-out shareholders).
  • (m) Marketability: listing ≠ liquidity; see dispersal / public holding, speculative interest, exchange, trading volume.

Techniques used in company analysis (statistical):

  • Correlation and regression: simple (2 variables) / multiple (more); links economy, industry, company variables; gives forecasts and tests reliability.
  • Trend analysis: one variable over time using regression; historical behaviour.
  • Decision tree analysis: range of values with probabilities; sequential decisions — multiply probabilities along each sequence, then sum. Simulation also reduces limitations.
  • Fundamental analysis = examination of economic and financial aspects to estimate future earnings and dividends, incl. macro-economic and political factors → buy/sell decision.
  • Group analysis: SEBI wants disclosure of group companies' financial health and promise vs performance in offer documents; RBI focuses on Group Exposure Norms of banks.

2 Technical Analysis · 2.1 Meaning

  • Study of share price movements through price graphs/charts, assuming price trends are repetitive because investor psychology follows a pattern. Studies investor and price behaviour.
  • Two questions: (i) is there a discernible trend? (ii) are there indications it will reverse? Methods: visual (charts, patterns) and statistical (price and return data).

Note (app-written): the SM prints "The technical analyst is concerned with the fundamental strength or weakness of a company"; the rest of the SM (2.2(iv), section 3) shows technical analysis uses market data, not fundamentals — read as "not concerned".

2.2 Assumptions

  • Market value depends on supply and demand for a security.
  • Supply and demand governed by factors rational or irrational (e.g. Govt NPA initiatives → demand for bank stocks).
  • Prices move in trends that continue for a substantial period (in a bull market a substantial correction gives a buying opportunity).
  • Relies on chart analysis of past prices, not financial statements.

2.3 Principles of Technical Analysis

  • Market discounts everything: price already reflects everything incl. fundamentals (in line with EMH).
  • Price moves in trends: more likely to continue the past trend.
  • History tends to repeat itself: chart patterns repeat.

2.4.1 The Dow Theory TYK T2

  • Charles Dow (founder of Dow Jones), late 19th century; measures relative strength of the market; barometer of business.
  • Based on two indices: DJIA (Industrial) and DJTA (Transportation) — aggregate impact of all information.
  • Three simultaneous movements:
    • Primary — main trend (bull/bear), 1 year to 36 months or longer.
    • Secondary — shorter, opposite direction, 2 weeks to a month or more.
    • Daily fluctuations — not part of Dow interpretation, but study them as they make up longer movements.
  • Tells where the market is and where it is going, not how far. Successive higher highs and higher lows → bullish; lower highs and lower lows → bearish.
  • Primary uptrend: three moves up — (1) accumulation by far-sighted investors; (2) first reports of good earnings; (3) widespread reports of well-being + rampant speculation. Then far-sighted sell → first move down; non-sustainability confirmed → second; distress selling → third.

2.4.2 Elliot Wave Theory Asked: May 2026 Q4(c) 4m TYK T3

  • Ralph Elliot, 1934; inspired by Dow Theory and nature; based on 75 years of price movements and charts.
  • Wave = movement of market price from one change in direction to the next change in the same direction; caused by buying/selling impulses from demand-supply pressures.
  • Impulsive patterns (basic waves): 3 or 5 waves in the direction of the basic movement (bull or bear phase).
  • Corrective patterns (reaction waves): 3 waves against the basic direction; correct the earlier rise (bull) or fall (bear).
  • Waves 1, 3, 5 = directional; waves 2, 4 = corrective.
  • Complete cycle: five-wave impulse + three-wave correction (a, b, c) = 8 waves; two phases, bullish and bearish; then a fresh cycle starts.

2.4.3 Random Walk Theory

  • Dow-type theories assume trends give clues to future prices (technical analysis — much subjective judgment).
  • Random walk hypothesis: prices are unpredictable; no relationship between present and future prices; prices are independent — mere statistical ups and downs.
  • M. G. Kendell (British statistician): price changes behave as if generated by a roulette wheel, each outcome independent of past. Layman: a drunk walking in a blind lane.
  • Argument: (a) prices can never be predicted; (b) trends are not due to underlying factors but a statistical expression of past data; (c) no connection between successive peaks and troughs.

2.5 Charting Techniques

  • Line chart: closing price of each period (day/week/month) plotted and joined by a line.
  • Bar chart: vertical bar from low to high; short horizontal ticks for open and close.
  • Japanese candlestick: same OHLC data; body colour — white = close > open (bullish), black = close < open (bearish); open ≈ close = Doji (simple bar). Vertical line = high-low; rectangle = open-close.
  • Point and figure: more complex; detects reversals. Decide box size (e.g. ₹ 1, 2, 0.50 — smaller = more sensitive) and reversal criterion (boxes to be retraced to start a new column in the opposite direction). X = rising, O = falling.

SM example (A Ltd., 6 days, O/H/L/C): 01-Jan 58/72/52/68; 02-Jan 71/73/58/64.30; 03-Jan 66/67/56/57; 04-Jan 58.50/75.50/55/72; 05-Jan 73.50/75/58/71; 06-Jan 74.50/76/55/74.50 — plotted as bar and candlestick charts. P&F example: prices 24, 26, 27, 26, 28, 27, 26, 25, 26, 23 over 10 periods — a column of X then a column of O on a 22–30 grid.

2.6 Market Indicators

  • Breadth index: covers all securities traded = net advances or declines ÷ number of issues traded. Supports Dow Jones averages → technical strength; does not → weakness (market to move opposite). Addition to Dow Theory.
  • Volume of transactions: rising index with increasing volume → buy (unsatisfied demand); falling with increasing volume → bear, prices fall further; rising with decreasing volume → bull; falling with dwindling volume → bear. Best used with another indicator (Dow).
  • Confidence index: willingness to take a chance = high-grade bond yields ÷ low-grade bond yields. Rising index precedes rising market; fall precedes a drop (low-grade yields rising faster / falling slower). Usually, not always, leading → use with other indicators.
  • Relative strength analysis: some securities rise faster in bull / fall slower in bear; strength persists → invest in them. Measured by historical average returns, or ratios vs industry / vs market.
  • Odd-lot theory: contrary-opinion — the average person is usually wrong; used mainly to predict tops in bull markets and reversals in individual securities.

2.7 Support and Resistance Levels

  • Fall from a peak → peak = resistance; rebound from a trough → lowest value = support. Price moves between them.
  • Near resistance → selling pressure (those who missed selling at the high); near support → buying pressure. A breach = departure from status quo, new levels.

SM example (A and B, Dec 2005 – Feb 2006 prices): A — support about ₹ 100, resistance ₹ 125; B — support ₹ 140, resistance ₹ 160.

  • Shifts in demand and supply are gradual, not instantaneous; detected early.

4 Efficient Market Theory (EMH) TYK T1

  • Eugene Fama (University of Chicago), 1960s. All available price-sensitive information is fully reflected in prices → no one can consistently beat the market; stocks trade at fair value; can't buy undervalued / sell overvalued.
  • Expert stock selection or market timing can't outperform; higher return only by buying riskier investments.

4.1 Search for Theory

Randomness of prices is the result of an efficient market:

  • Information freely and instantaneously available to all.
  • Keen competition → prices reflect intrinsic values (impound all information).
  • Prices change only in response to new information, unrelated to previous → unpredictable.

4.2 Misconception about Efficient Market Theory

  • EMT implies prices factor in all information, so no consistent long-term returns from market operations.
  • Prices fluctuate and cannot reflect fair value, as the future is uncertain — surprises keep coming.
  • Failure of institutional portfolio managers to outperform is taken to mean lack of competence; in fact efficiency exists because managers do the job well in a competitive setting.
  • Random movement is taken to mean the market is irrational; but randomness ≠ irrationality — rational, competitive investors make price changes random.

4.4 Empirical Evidence on Weak Form

No relationship between past and future price movements. Three tests:

  • (a) Serial correlation test: correlate price change in one period with another; studies (different stocks, lags, periods — daily, weekly, fortnightly, monthly) found no significant serial correlation. Not long-term trends, where prices tend to move upwards.
  • (b) Run test: mark each change + (increase) or − (decrease). A run continues while the sign is the same; a change of sign ends it. Compare number of runs with a purely random series of the same size. Results strongly support the random walk model.
  • (c) Filter rules test: buy when price rises at least N%, hold till it falls at least N% from a subsequent high, then sell. Studies: filter rules do not outperform buy and hold, esp. after commissions.
Run test formula (TYK P2 answer)Terms / logic
µr = [2n1n2 ÷ (n1 + n2)] + 1Expected runs in a random series; n1 = no. of + changes, n2 = no. of − changes; r = actual runs.
σr = √{2n1n2(2n1n2 − n1 − n2) ÷ [(n1 + n2)2(n1 + n2 − 1)]}Standard deviation of runs.
Limits = µr ± t × σrTwo-tailed (too few runs → not random); t at n − 1 df (n = no. of changes). r within limits → random → weak-form efficient.

TYK P2 (index, Oct 2007, 20 closes → 19 changes): runs r = 8; n1 = 11, n2 = 8; µ = 176/19 + 1 = 10.26; σ = √[176 × 157 ÷ (192 × 18)] = √4.252 = 2.06. 5% (t 2.101, 18 df): 5.932 to 14.588; 10% (t 1.734): 6.688 to 13.832. r = 8 within both → market is weak-form efficient.

4.5 Empirical Evidence on Semi-strong Form

  • Prices adjust rapidly to all public information → no above-normal risk-adjusted return from public information. Tests: can public information earn above-normal returns, and how fast do prices adjust to earnings, dividends, mergers, acquisitions, stock splits?
  • Fama, Fisher, Jensen and Roll: 940 stock splits, NYSE, 1957–1959 — prior to the split, stock earns higher returns than predicted by any market model.
  • Ball and Brown (SM prints "Boll"): annual earnings announcements; group 1 earnings up vs average, group 2 down. SM: before the announcement group 1 earned positive abnormal returns; group 2 earned negative abnormal returns; stock in both groups earned normal returns (after the announcement).
  • Documented inefficiencies / anomalies: prices adjust gradually to unanticipated quarterly earnings; small firms outperform large; low P/E outperforms high P/E; Monday's return lower than other days.

Note (app-written): the SM sentence reads "stock in the second group earned negative abnormal returns after the announcement of earnings. Stock in both the groups earned normal returns" — the wording is muddled; read it as abnormal returns before the announcement and normal returns after it.

4.6 Empirical Evidence on Strong Form

  • All information, public or private, reflected — an extreme hypothesis.
  • Tested on returns of corporate insiders, stock exchange specialists, mutual fund managers. Insiders (internal information) and specialists (monopolistic exposure) earn superior risk-adjusted returns.
  • Mutual fund managers do not on average; professionally managed portfolios no better than random portfolios — Burton Malkiel, A Random Walk Down Wall Street.

4.7 Challenges to the Efficient Market Theory

  • Information inadequacy: not freely available or rapidly transmitted; companies circulate misinformation.
  • Limited information processing: Herbert Simon — millions of bits per second generated, perception admits not more than a thousand; David Dreman — under anxiety and uncertainty the market can become a giant.
  • Irrational behaviour: J. M. Keynes — considerations irrelevant to prospective yield enter valuation; L. C. Gupta — market works haphazardly, like a blind man firing a gun; hit or miss tactics.
  • Monopolistic influence: powerful institutions and big operators influence prices → less competitive.
