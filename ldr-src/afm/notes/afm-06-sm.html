1. Introduction

  • Investor never puts all funds in one security; a well diversified portfolio optimises risk-return; a portfolio can reduce risk without diluting returns.
  • Risk = possibility of actual return deviating from expected/average return; measured by standard deviation / variance of return.

1.1 Activities in Portfolio Management

  • (a) Selection of securities; (b) construction of all feasible portfolios from them; (c) deciding weights so that it is the optimal portfolio for that investor's risk appetite.

1.2 Objectives of Portfolio Management

  • Security/safety of principal (intact sum and its purchasing power); stability of income (plan reinvestment/consumption); capital growth (reinvest in growth securities).
  • Marketability (ease of buying/selling, flexibility); liquidity (nearness to money, to seize opportunities); diversification (reduce risk of loss of capital/income across securities and industries); favourable tax status (minimise tax to raise effective yield).

2. Phases of Portfolio Management (2.1–2.5)

PhaseWhat it does (SM points)
2.1 Security analysisInitial phase: risk-return of individual securities and their correlation; find under-priced (buy) and over-priced (sell). Fundamental analysis: EPS, payout, competition, market share, management, industry factors → intrinsic value vs CMP. Technical analysis: price movements systematic, past patterns predict future; ignores fundamentals. Select across sectors with no strong positive correlation so unsystematic risk is eliminated. Random Walk: price moves random, neither analysis gives sustained gains; EMH: no sustained long-term gains; markets never perfectly efficient.
2.2 Portfolio analysisCombine securities; each portfolio's risk-return is not the aggregate of constituents — computed from their risk-return and pair-wise correlations. All portfolios from the chosen set = Feasible Portfolios.
2.3 Portfolio selectionIdentify Efficient Portfolios (highest return for same/lower risk, or lowest risk for same/higher return), then the Optimal Portfolio suiting risk appetite (Markowitz).
2.4 Portfolio revisionMonitor so it does not lose optimality (economy, new securities); add/delete securities; also investor changes (extra funds, risk appetite, need of cash). Must be scientific and objective — as important as analysis and selection.
2.5 Portfolio evaluationMeasure actual return realised and risk borne over the period; identifies weaknesses in the process. Process is ongoing: analysis → construction → revision → evaluation.

3. Portfolio Theories — 3.1 Traditional Approach

  • Investor's study: age, health, responsibilities, other assets, portfolio needs; need for income, capital maintenance, liquidity; attitude to risk; taxation status.
  • Objectives: maximise wealth subject to risk; higher risk → higher expected return.
  • Investment strategy: balance fixed interest vs equities; high payout vs high growth companies; income of growth portfolio; income tax vs capital gains tax; transaction cost vs gains from rapid switching; retain liquidity for bargains.
  • Diversification reduces volatility; balance equities and fixed interest.
  • Selection: intrinsic value vs market (fundamental) or past prices (technical); expert advice; inside information; newspaper tipsters; companies with good asset backing, dividend growth, earnings record, quality management.
  • Followed by most Indian brokers. Says "don't put all money in one basket" — Markowitz showed only particular kinds of securities give diversification benefit; don't pick blindly.

3.2 Modern Approach (Markowitz Model or Risk-Return Optimization)

  • Harry Markowitz (early 1950s), father of Modern Portfolio Theory; Mean Variance Analysis (1952).
  • Two planks: (1) adding securities with low or negative correlation reduces risk; (2) higher return needs higher risk. Risk of an individual asset hardly matters — what matters is its contribution to overall portfolio risk.

4. Risk Analysis — 4.1 Types of Risk

  • Risk = variation of actual (realised) return from expected return; stable returns (Govt securities) = low risk; equity = risky.
  • Total risk = Systematic risk + Unsystematic risk. Systematic: external, macro, affects many securities, uncontrollable. Unsystematic: internal, micro, company-specific, largely controllable.
4.1.1 Systematic (non-diversifiable)4.1.2 Unsystematic ("idiosyncratic", diversifiable)
Interest rate risk: prices move inversely with rates; long-term securities vary more than short-term.Business risk: variability of operating profit; measured by operating leverage.
Purchasing power (inflation) risk: nominal return = real return + inflation premium; unexpected inflation erodes return; more for bonds/fixed income, less for equity (dividends rise, capital gains).Financial (leveraged) risk: due to debt; expressed by debt-equity ratio; highly geared → risk of winding up.
Market risk: share moves with the market — bullish (general rise) / bearish (general fall); seen in BSE Sensex, NSE index.

4.2 Diversion of Risk

  • Combining many securities diversifies away unsystematic risk; total risk falls as number of securities rises; beyond a limit the portfolio contains only systematic risk.

5. Markowitz Model of Risk-Return Optimization

  • Unlike CAPM, the optimal portfolio is investor specific. Two stages: (1) find mean-variance efficient portfolios; (2) select one.
  • Investors dislike risk; trade-off between risk and return; SD measures total risk; diversification removes a good deal of it.

5.1 Assumptions of the Model

  • Return adequately summarises the outcome; investors visualise a probability distribution of returns.
  • Risk estimates proportional to variance; decisions on two criteria only — expected return and variance.
  • Investors rational and risk averse (between equal returns, prefer smaller risk).
  • Yield in different scenarios is the measure of return; SD = deviation of yields from expected value.

5.2 Efficient Frontier

  • Built on the Mean-Variance Dominance Principle: a portfolio is dominated by another with higher (or same) return for same (or lower) risk. Diversification (Markowitz) = combining assets less than perfectly positively correlated to reduce risk without sacrificing return.
  • If not efficient, the investor may: increase return without more risk; reduce risk without lower return; or a combination — by switching to a portfolio on the frontier.
  • Efficient portfolio: highest return for identical risk and lowest risk for identical return. Figures: of A–F, B, C, D lie on the frontier; feasible set = shaded area; any frontier point dominates points to its right/below (B vs E: same return, B has less risk). A = least risk; D = highest return with highest risk.
  • Optimal portfolio = where the investor's indifference curve touches the efficient frontier; differs from investor to investor.

TYK P5 (dominance): A (8%, σ 4) dominates B and F (same return, more risk) and D (same risk 4, return 4%) → select A, C & E. 6. Capital Market Theory

  • B (tangent point on the frontier) = market portfolio; tangent line from Rf through B to Z = Capital Market Line (CML) — combinations of market portfolio with borrowing or lending.
  • Rf to B = lending portfolios (part risk-free, part market); B to Z = borrowing portfolios (borrow and invest in the market portfolio).
FormulaTerms / logic

8. Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) Asked: Jan 2026 MCQ CS-III 7-9 6m

  • Portfolio risk = unsystematic (averted by diversification) + systematic (market-related, measured by β). Only non-diversifiable risk is priced; market β = 1.0; portfolio β = Σ proportion × β.
  • Three aspects: market not concerned with diversifiable risk; not concerned with investor holding a diversified portfolio; compensation only for non-diversifiable risk.

Assumptions (TYK T3):

  • Efficient market (full information, prices reflect intrinsic value; Hampton — CAPM usable for capital budgeting); rational investment goals; risk aversion.
  • Assets divisible and liquid; borrow freely at risk-less rate; no brokerage, taxes or transaction cost; no bankruptcy; capital market in equilibrium.
  • Required return = minimum expected return for investors to buy and hold the asset.

12. Formulation of Portfolio Strategy — 12.1 Active Portfolio Strategy (APS)

  • Followed by most professionals and aggressive investors to earn superior risk-adjusted return and beat the index; most Indian funds are active. Four principles:
  • (a) Market timing — depart from long-run asset mix on a forecast of market movement (business cycle, moving average, advance-decline analysis, econometric models); few succeed; forecasting right 75% of the time only breaks even after costs; Fisher Black — investor loses by being out of the market part of the time.
  • (b) Sector rotation — overweight sectors expected to do well (e.g. steel, pharma); for bonds, shift in quality, coupon, maturity (expect rate rise → long to medium/short term).
  • (c) Security selection — search for under-priced securities by fundamental/technical analysis; overweight attractive, underweight unattractive; bonds: highest YTM at given risk.
  • (d) Specialised investment concept — growth, neglected/out-of-favour, asset, technology, cyclical stocks. Helps focus on one's ability, avoid distraction, master a style; disadvantage — may become obsolete.

12.2 Passive Portfolio Strategy

  • Capital market fairly efficient. Two guidelines: (a) create a well-diversified portfolio at a predetermined risk; (b) hold it relatively unchanged unless inadequately diversified or inconsistent with risk-return preference.
  • Index fund — invests in the target index in the same weights (e.g. Nifty fund in the 50 Nifty companies); returns track the index; does not mitigate market risk.

14. Asset Allocation Strategies

  • Integrated — examine capital market conditions and investor objectives/constraints; allocation that best serves needs with the market forecast.
  • Strategic — optimal mixes from historical returns, risk, covariances; adjusted periodically to restore target allocation.
  • Tactical — risk tolerance assumed constant; allocation changed on expectations about market conditions.
  • Insured — risk exposure adjusted with portfolio value; more wealth → more ability to take risk.

15. Fixed Income Portfolio — 15.1 Process; 15.2 Return

  • 15.1 Five steps: set objective → draft investment policy guidelines → select strategy (active/passive) → select securities and other assets → evaluate performance against benchmark.

16. Alternative Investment Avenues

  • AIs = investments other than stock, bond and cash. Features: high fees; limited historical data; illiquidity; less transparency; extensive research required; leveraged buying.
  • Types: real estates, gold, private equity, REITs, hedge funds, ETFs, mutual funds, commodities, distressed securities (others covered in their chapters).

16.1 Real Estates (16.1.1 Valuation approaches; 16.1.2 Valuation)

  • Tangible: land, buildings, offices, warehouses, shops; country-specific laws reduce appeal of foreign property (rising with globalisation).
  • 16.1.1 Why valuation is complex: inefficient market; illiquidity; only approximate comparison; high transaction cost; no organised market.
  • 16.1.2 Approaches: sales comparison (like P/E multiplier — benchmark similar property); income (like perpetual debenture — perpetual net income ÷ required return); cost (replacement cost of building + land value, adjusted for location etc.); discounted after-tax cash flow (PV of inflows − investment, like NPV).

  • Jewellery suffers making charges on buying and selling back. Alternatives:
  • Gold bars/coins — but cost of physical storage.
  • Sovereign Gold Bonds — Government securities in grams of gold, issued by RBI on behalf of GoI; pay issue price in cash, redeemed in cash at market price of gold; periodical interest; no storage risk/cost, no making charges or purity issue; held in RBI books or demat.

16.3 Distressed securities

  • Securities of companies in or near bankruptcy, bought cheap, to try to revive the company; suit investors who cannot participate in the market and want to avoid due diligence.
  • Profit: long position in debt + short position in equity — if condition improves, interest (priority) exceeds dividend on the short; if it deteriorates, both fall and the short gains.
  • Risks: liquidity risk; event risk (company-specific events); market risk (not important); human risk (judge's decision).
