1. Introduction

  • Firms with large credit sales carry huge receivables yet may face a liquidity crunch.
  • Borrowing changes the debt-equity ratio: may be unacceptable to stakeholders, adds financial risk, hurts future borrowing capacity. Securitization addresses this.

2. Concept and Definition

  • Creation of a pool of assets from illiquid financial assets (receivables, loans) = repackaging / rebundling of illiquid assets into marketable securities. E.g. auto loans, credit card receivables, residential mortgages, other future receivables.
  • RBI: transactions where credit risks in assets are redistributed by repackaging them into tradable securities.

Features (6):

  • Creation of financial instruments — additional financial product in the market, backed by collaterals.
  • Bundling and unbundling — combining assets in one pool = bundling; breaking into instruments of fixed denomination = unbundling.
  • Tool of risk management — on non-recourse basis, default risk shifts from originator to investor.
  • Structured finance — instruments tailor-structured to the investor's risk-return profile.
  • Trenching — portfolio split into parts (tranches) by risk and return; each tranche has a different risk/return.
  • Homogeneity — securities within a tranche are homogenous; small denominations suit small investors.

3. Benefits of Securitization

3.1 Originator (entity selling assets collectively to the SPV):

  • Off-balance sheet financing — releases capital tied up in assets → improved liquidity → business expansion.
  • More specialisation in main business — loan servicing moves to SPV; under non-recourse even default burden shifts.
  • Improves financial ratios — esp. banks/FIs: manage Capital-to-Weighted Asset Ratio.
  • Reduced borrowing cost — papers are rated due to credit enhancement, issued at lower interest; originator earns a spread.

3.2 Investor:

  • Diversification of risk — securities backed by different asset types.
  • Regulatory requirement — asset-backed securities of a particular industry (e.g. micro) help banks meet Priority Sector Lending norms of RBI.
  • Protection against default — under recourse, originator makes good the loss; insurance may also compensate.

5. Mechanism of Securitization (steps)

  1. 5.1 Creation of pool of assets — segregate assets backed by similar mortgages (for MBS) by interest rate, risk, maturity, concentration units.
  2. 5.2 Transfer to SPV — pooled assets transferred to the SPV created for this purpose.
  3. 5.3 Sale of securitized papers — SPV designs instruments by nature of interest, risk, tenure, pool; e.g. pass through / pay through.
  4. 5.4 Administration of assets — sub-contracted back to originator; collects principal and interest, transfers to SPV; acts as servicer/conduit for an agreed fee.
  5. 5.5 Recourse to originator — unless specified otherwise, on debtor default receivables go back to originator from SPV.
  6. 5.6 Repayment of funds — SPV pays investors interest and principal from the pool.
  7. 5.7 Credit rating to instruments — sometimes done before sale to assess risk.

6. Problems in Securitization (esp. India)

  • 6.1 Stamp duty — on mortgage debt under Transfer of Property Act, 1882, up to 12% in some states. PTCs do not evidence debt (only receivables) → exempt. Some states have reduced duty on securitized instruments.
  • 6.2 Taxation — no specific Income Tax provision; views differ: SPV taxed as trustee in representative capacity vs. investors taxed on their share. Capital gains on pass-on payments unclear.
  • 6.3 Accounting — reporting in originator's books; receivables removed from balance sheet; problem esp. when transfer is without recourse.
  • 6.4 Lack of standardisation — each originator uses its own documentation/administration format.
  • 6.5 Inadequate debt market — no well-developed debt market → weak secondary market for ABS.
  • 6.6 Ineffective foreclosure laws — lenders find it hard to transfer property on default → MBS less attractive.

7. Securitization Instruments Asked: Jan 2026 Q6(c) 4m

Divided by maturity characteristics into three:

7.1 Pass Through Certificates (PTCs)

  • Originator transfers the entire cash receipt (interest + principal) from assets sold → direct claim of investors on all securitized assets via SPV.
  • Investors hold proportional beneficial interest in assets held in trust by SPV.
  • Prepayment of principal distributed proportionately to all holders.
  • On final payment, all securities terminate simultaneously (single maturity).
  • Skewness of cash flows in early stage if principal repaid before schedule.

7.2 Pay Through Security (PTS) — overcomes PTC's limitation of all cash flows being passed on and of single maturity.

  • SPV issues debt securities backed by the assets → can restructure tranches from varying maturities of receivables.
  • Permits desynchronisation of servicing of securities from asset cash flows.
  • SPV may reinvest surplus funds short term (e.g. on early retirement of receivables, for short-term yield).
  • Freedom to issue several debt tranches with varying maturities.

7.3 Stripped Securities — cash flows of underlying securities divided into two or more new securities:

  • Interest Only (IO) — holder gets only interest. Principal Only (PO) — holder gets only principal.
  • Highly volatile → less preferred by investors.
  • Rates rise → PO price falls (borrowers postpone paying cheaper loans); IO value rises (more interest calculated).
  • Rates fall → borrowers prepay to borrow fresh at lower rates; IO tends to fall due to prepayments of principal.
  • Prices of IOs and POs mainly determined by perception of investors.

Note (app-written): SM p.10 sentence "However, when interest rate due to prepayments of principals, IO's tends to fall" is incomplete; read as: when interest rates fall, prepayments of principal rise and IO value falls (this matches the SM's own prepayment logic on p.9).

8. Pricing of Securitized Instruments

Price must be acceptable to both originator and investor.

  • 8.1 Originator's angle — priced at a rate (outflow) the originator can amortise over time by investing the amount raised.
  • 8.2 Investor's angle — price = expected future cash flows discounted at the YTM of a comparable security (credit quality, average life). Yield can be read off the yield curve, adjusted by spread points for credit quality.

9. Risks in Securitization

  • 9.1 Credit / counterparty risk — prime risk: bankruptcy and non-performance of the servicer.
  • 9.2 Legal risk — new in India; no conclusive judicial precedent or explicit statute → dispute on legal ownership → uncertain investor pay-outs.
  • 9.3 Market risks (external to the transaction):
    • (a) Macroeconomic — industry downturn / adverse asset price moves; e.g. fall in industrial production cuts CV use → CV loan repayments hit; fall in CV prices → wilful default and repossession.
    • (b) Prepayment — lower rates → more retail loan prepayment → reinvestment risk (money received early, cannot reinvest at same yield).
    • (c) Interest rate — floating-rate pool vs fixed pay-outs (or vice versa) → mismatch; even at 100% collection efficiency inflow may fall short. Hedge partly with interest rate swaps.

10. Tokenization — Blockchain first

  • Blockchain / Distributed Ledger Technology (DLT): shared, peer-to-peer, decentralised open ledger, no trusted third party.
  • Append-only — entries permanent; transactions irreversible; any change recorded as a new transaction.
  • Decentralised = not controlled by any bank, corporation or government; chain of blocks, each block = digital information in a public database.
  • Analogy: a shared Google Doc — distributed, not copied; everyone has access at once; changes recorded in real time, fully transparent.

Working of a blockchain transaction: transaction initiated (e.g. sending money) → broadcast via network → network validates using cryptography → represented online as a block → block added to existing chain → transaction complete.

10.1 Applications of Blockchain

  • Financial services — automated trade lifecycle / transaction log of any asset transfer, physical or digital (laptops, smartphones, automobiles, real estate).
  • Healthcare — secure data sharing; privacy, security, interoperability; no third party, lower overheads.
  • Government — land registration, vehicle registration, e-voting; transparency, better monitoring and audit.
  • Travel industry — money transactions; storing passports/IDs, reservations; travel insurance, loyalty and rewards.
  • Economic forecasts — decentralised prediction markets, decentralised voting, stock trading.

10.2 Risks associated with Blockchain Asked: Sep 2025 Q3(c) 4m

  • Wider risk perspective — members have different risk appetites → conflict in designing monitoring controls; who manages risk if no one is in charge; accountability.
  • Reliability depends on technology — if the consensus mechanism is tampered with, ledger information becomes inaccurate and unreliable.
  • No central authority to administer/enforce protocol amendments → difficult process control; users of public blockchains struggle to understand general IT controls and their effectiveness.
  • Information overload — huge, frequently updated data challenges monitoring; hard to find competent people for monitoring controls.

10.3 Meaning of Tokenization

  • Converting tangible and intangible assets into blockchain tokens; effective in conventional industries like real estate, artwork.

10.4 Tokenization and Securitization — similarities

Both convert illiquid assets into liquid, tradable products.

  • Liquidity — inject liquidity for otherwise illiquid assets.
  • Diversification — help investors diversify, manage risk, optimise returns.
  • Trading — both tradable → generate wealth.
  • New opportunities — fee income for financial institutions and agencies.

11. Securitization in India Asked: May 2026 Q3(c) 4m (NPAs)

  • Citi Bank pioneered it in India by bundling auto loans; later sales tax deferrals, aircraft receivables etc.
  • SARFAESI Act, 2002 (Securitization and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest) — to tackle NPAs without approaching the Court.
  • Now an important funding source for micro finance companies and NBFCs; commercial MBS emerging.
  • Market dominated by a few players — ICICI Bank, HDFC Bank, NHB.
  • CRISIL: volumes may reach near pre-pandemic highs of ₹ 1.9 trillion (FY19 & FY20) once macro-situation and rates stabilise; key funding for non-banks, investment avenue for banks growing retail assets.
  • SEBI allows FPIs to invest in securitized debt of unlisted companies up to a limit.
