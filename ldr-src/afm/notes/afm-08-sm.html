1, 1.1–1.5 Introduction, mutual benefits, what is a MF, who can invest, how it works, stocks or MF

  • MF = a trust that pools savings of investors with a common financial goal, through issue of units; investor becomes part owner of the fund's assets.
  • Managed by professional money managers as per the scheme's stated investment objective: shares, debentures, bonds and money-market instruments (CP, CD, T-Bills).
  • Net income + capital appreciation shared among unit holders in proportion to units held. Fund house charges fees based on the value of assets.
  • Anybody with an investible surplus of a few thousand rupees can invest.
  • Stocks or MF? Diversification needs 15–20 stocks (₹ 5,000 can't be spread); brokerage eats returns on small lots; SIP (e.g. ₹ 1,000 a month) gives discipline; stock picking needs time, inclination and skill; churning costs capital gains tax (MFs do not pay capital gains tax when they sell a stock). Risk-averse investors → MF.

Establishment / structure of a MF (TYK T1 — ICAI answer):

  • SEBI registration before collecting funds; all MFs under the same regulations, SEBI monitoring and inspection.
  • Sponsor (like a promoter: gives initial capital, appoints trustees) — body corporate in financial services for not less than 5 years, financially sound, fit party.
  • Trust (or trustee company) under the Indian Trusts Act; trust deed executed by sponsor in favour of trustees, registered under the Indian Registration Act, 1908, with Third Schedule clauses. Trustee holds MF property in trust for unit holders. At least two-thirds of trustee directors independent of sponsor.
  • AMC — SEBI-approved, limited liability company, minimum net worth ₹ 100 crore; sponsor contributes at least 40% of AMC net worth; 50% of AMC directors independent. AMC can be terminated by majority of trustees or 75% of unit holders of the scheme. Charges investment management and advisory fees within ceiling + gets operating expenses reimbursed.
  • Custodian — SEBI-registered, independent of sponsor and AMC, holds securities (Depository Participant for demat).

Risks of bond funds:

  • Interest rate risk — bond value moves inversely to interest rate (YTM).
  • Credit risk — issuer defaults on interest/principal; higher for lower credit rating.
  • Prepayment risk — issuer calls callable bonds early when rates fall and refinances at lower rate.
  • Reinvestment risk — maturity proceeds reinvested at lower rate in falling-rate scenario (favourable when rates rise).

2.2.3 Special funds

  • Index — invest in index stocks in the same weightage; passive, low cost; deliver approx. the index return (e.g. NIFTY 50 Index Fund).
  • International — raises money in India, invests globally. Offshore — located outside India, raises money globally to invest in India.
  • Sector — entire fund in one sector (infrastructure, IT, pharma).
  • Money market — debt-oriented; preservation of capital, liquidity, moderate income; CP, CD, T-Bills, G-Secs; park surplus for short periods; less interest-rate sensitive, lower volatility than long-dated bond funds.
  • Fund of Funds — invest in units of other MF schemes (same or other fund house), e.g. Indian MFs investing in international MF schemes.
  • Capital protection oriented — protection comes from portfolio structure, not bank guarantee/insurance; SEBI: close-ended, listed, portfolio structure mandatorily rated; major part in highly rated debt, rest in equity. Not the same as "capital guaranteed".
  • Gold — track gold; generally ETFs, listed; bullion exposure without physical delivery.
  • Quant — data-driven, rule/model-based selection and timing via automated programme; manager checks robustness and monitors models. Differs from index fund manager (who fully hands off to the index). Pros: removes human bias, consistent strategy, lower expense ratio (passive). Con: models tested on historical data — past trends can't be used blindly.

Note (app-written): SM text says "Special Funds are of four types" but lists nine, (a) to (i) (SM p. 8.6–8.9). List all nine.

2.3–2.4 Ownership classification; Direct plans

  • Public sector MF — sponsored by a public sector company/bank. Private sector — by a private company. Foreign — by a foreign company operating from India, raising and investing funds in India.
  • Direct plans (SEBI, 1 January 2013): AMC charges no distribution expenses, trail fees, transaction charges → NAV generally higher than regular plan; have performed better to the extent of lower fees. Before this, direct investments went into the regular plan with a single NAV.

3, 3.1–3.2 Types of schemes: Balanced; Diversified equity; income options

  • Balanced — strategic allocation to debt (stability) and equity (growth); limited equity exposure; moderate returns.
  • Diversified equity — wide array of stocks, low concentration risk:
    • Flexicap — any proportion across market caps, no restriction; true diversification, flexibility.
    • Multicap — at least 25% each in large, mid and small cap; remaining 25% at manager's discretion.
    • Contra — out-of-favour, under-valued companies; blends growth and value; aggressive risk appetite.
    • Index — same securities and proportion as benchmark (Sensex, Nifty); earns market return.
    • Dividend yield — high dividend yield (DPS ÷ market price) stocks, above index yield; less volatile than growth stocks; medium risk; not always resilient in short-term corrections.
OptionWhat the investor gets
Growth / cumulativeNo intermittent income; return accumulates, paid on withdrawal as capital gain.
Dividend payoutDividend paid out; NAV falls to the extent of dividend and statutory levies.
Dividend re-investmentDividend reinvested at ex-dividend NAV → additional units.

3.3–3.8 ELSS, Sector, Thematic, Arbitrage, Hedge, Cash funds

  • ELSS — tax saving under Sec 80C; lock-in 3 years; better return potential than traditional tax-saving instruments; via SIP avoids year-end lump sum and gives averaging.
  • Sector — ride industry cycles; good returns if timing is right; more vulnerable on downside than diversified funds; only a limited portion of portfolio; not for first-time investors. E.g. Real Estate MFs — properties (rent + appreciation) and real-estate company securities; must be close-ended and listed.
  • Thematic — trends/themes (multi-sector, international, commodity); broader than sector; market may take long to recognise the theme.
  • Arbitrage — open-ended equity scheme; mix of cash equities, equity derivatives, debt; captures spot–futures price differential; better than debt, less volatile than equity.
  • Hedge fund — lightly regulated private vehicle for selected clients; reveals nothing publicly; charges a performance fee (cut of profits above benchmark; losses borne by investors); leveraged, long, short, derivative positions; aim is to maximise return (not to hedge).
  • Cash fund — open-ended liquid scheme; very short-dated debt and money market; growth and dividend options.

3.9 Exchange Traded Funds

  • Hybrid of listed stock + index fund; listed, price linked to underlying index; authorised participants act as market makers.
  • Bought/sold any time in market hours at real-time prices close to NAV (open-ended schemes deal at end-of-day NAV); no paperwork, order through broker; low cost, tax efficient, stock-like.
  • Combines MF valuation feature (end-of-day NAV) with close-ended fund tradability (intra-day, at more or less than NAV).
  • Types: Index ETFs (replicate index); Commodity ETFs (precious metals, futures); Bond ETFs (thrive in recessions — money moves from stocks to bonds; indicate economic conditions); Currency ETFs (total return: FX spot change, local institutional interest rates, collateral yield).

3.10 Fixed Maturity Plans

  • Close-ended, invest at NFO; CDs, CPs, money market, NCDs, sometimes bank FDs, over a fixed period; listed but thinly traded → not liquid.
  • Free from interest rate risk (instruments mature with the fund) but carry credit risk (downgrade cuts return).
  • Mostly 3-year tenure for indexation benefit; to earn more, part goes into AA and below rated debt (higher coupon).

4 Advantages of Mutual Fund (also TYK T2)

  • Professional management — skilled managers + research analysts.
  • Diversification — reduces concentration risk.
  • Convenient administration — demat, no share-transfer risk, no delay.
  • Higher returns over medium to long term (judge by long-run average, not boom-phase returns).
  • Low cost of management — SEBI cap 2.50% for equity MFs; excess borne by AMC.
  • Liquidity — open-ended: sale/repurchase by MF; close-ended: listing.
  • Transparency — portfolio disclosed at least half-yearly (many quarterly/monthly); NAV daily for open-ended, published through AMFI.
  • Other benefits — SWP, SIP, switching (except tax saving fund: no switch-out for 3 years).
  • Highly regulated — SEBI registration, MF Regulations.
  • Economies of scale — pooled money is cheaper; retail access to capital market.
  • Flexibility — SIP, SWP, wide range of schemes. Convenience — easy via digital portals.

5 Drawbacks of Mutual Fund

  • No guarantee of return — (i) some schemes underperform benchmark; (ii) fund gain ≠ every investor's gain (different entry/exit points); (iii) massive fall can erode principal short-term (2008 crisis, Covid 2020); remote over long term.
  • Diversification — minimises risk but does not maximise return; one stock doubling doesn't double the fund; neither exceptionally good nor poor.
  • Selection of proper fund — harder than picking a share; past performance can't predict future.
  • Cost factor — fund management fees in annual recurring expenses; exit load if withdrawn before a stipulated period; AMC fee not related to performance.

6, 6.1 Terms — Net Asset Value (NAV) Asked: Sep 2025 MCQ CS-II 4–6 6m Asked: Jan 2026 Q6(a) 6m

  • NAV = value per unit of portfolio holding = what a unit holder would get per unit if the fund were redeemed. Returns depend on NAV and costs.
  • Three aspects: (i) net value — market value of assets less liabilities; (ii) changes daily; (iii) per unit — ÷ outstanding units.
  • Investors' subscription = unit capital (liability side); investments = assets.

6.2 Entry and Exit Load Asked: Sep 2025 Q5(b) 7m Asked: May 2026 Q3(b) 4m

  • Loads = sales charges to compensate distribution costs; funds without them = no-load funds.
  • Entry load — at purchase; % added to NAV → fewer units allotted. At present no scheme has an entry load.
  • Exit load — at redemption or switch; % deducted from NAV; goes to the scheme's pool, not the AMC. Most schemes have none after a stipulated holding period.

6.3–6.4 Trail commission; Expense ratio

  • Trail commission — paid by investor to advisor each year as incentive to review holdings; usually 0.30–0.75% of investment value; on daily average AUM of the distributor, paid monthly; separate from upfront commission paid by the fund company out of its own pocket.
  • Expense ratio (MER / TER) — % of assets spent to run the fund: management and advisory fees, travel, audit, custodian, registrar, consultancy fees. Excludes brokerage on portfolio trading. Can be 2–3% — can seriously undermine performance.

6.5 Side Pocketing

  • Separation of risky/doubtful (stressed, illiquid) assets from other investments and cash; money linked to stressed assets is locked until recovered; avoids distress selling.
  • When rating falls, illiquid assets moved to a side pocket → NAV reflects actual value of liquid assets; benefits current long-term holders; liquidity not a problem despite frequent allotments/redemptions.
  • Common internationally, but also misused to deprive investors of genuine returns. India: IL&FS default — MFs wrote off exposure, low to negative returns.

6.6 Tracking Error

  • Divergence of a fund's return from its benchmark return — passive managers often can't replicate exactly. Computed on benchmark return vs quarterly or monthly average NAVs.
  • Higher TE → higher risk profile; shows benchmark not followed properly. Ideal ≈ zero.
  • Reasons: transaction cost, AMC fees, fund expenses, cash holdings, sampling biasness.

TE = √[ Σ(d − d̄)2 ÷ (n − 1) ]SM: d = differential return; d̄ = average differential return; n = no. of observations. (app-written: d is fund return − benchmark return each period; TE is the standard deviation of these differences.)

7, 7.1 Evaluation of Mutual Funds; Quantitative parameters

  • Purpose: fund keeps generating maximum profit with minimum risk; decide hold or replace; past performance doesn't guarantee future. Not daily; give equity funds 3–5 years; review at least every 6/12 months.
  • Risk-adjusted returns — return relative to risk; same return → choose lower risk.
  • Benchmark returns — excess over benchmark = alpha (index 11%, fund 13% → outperformed; 8% → underperformed).
  • Comparison to peers — same category ("apples with apples"); should consistently beat peers.
  • Returns across economic and market cycles — not just 6/12-month windows.
  • Financial measures: expense ratio; Sharpe = [E(R) − Rf] ÷ σ (total risk); Treynor = [E(R) − Rf] ÷ β (systematic risk; fund beta = volatility vs its benchmark); Sortino — Sharpe with downside deviation instead of total SD.

7.2 Qualitative parameters

  • Quality of portfolio — equity: blue-chip/large companies, diversification, style (growth, value, blend); debt: credit quality, average maturity, modified duration; investments as per fund objective; lower portfolio turnover ratio is better.
  • Track record and competence of fund manager — knowledge, ability, past performance.
  • Credibility of fund house team — redemptions, crediting dividends, information.

8, 8.1–8.2 Role of Fund Managers — Active and Passive funds

  • Actively managed — research, judgement, due diligence to outperform the market and generate positive alpha (stock picking).
  • Passively managed — match the index return with minimum tracking error.
  • Other roles: Compliances (SEBI, AMFI guidelines; reporting expense ratio, redemptions; investor awareness; documents on time); constant monitoring of performance (entry/exit decisions; growth above inflation and interest rate); creation and protection of wealth (no reckless risk; fundamental and technical analysis); control over outsourced work to third parties.

9 Role of FIIs in Mutual Funds

  • FIIs = large foreign groups with substantial investible funds, registered abroad; strong research teams; fuel bullish markets for short periods → strong forex inflow. Buy units of domestic MFs directly from the issuer or through a registered broker on a recognised exchange, within SEBI limits.
  • Importance: enhanced corporate governance (due diligence), improved competition in market, improved capital inflow (employment, forex position).

With loads: buy at NAV + entry load, sell at NAV − exit load; then tax, then real return.

Exam steps — Dividend vs Bonus plan: 1 units = investment ÷ entry NAV → 2 dividend plan: units × ₹ 10 × % ÷ that day's NAV, running total → 3 bonus plan: units × ratio, running total → 4 final units × final NAV (− STT, − STCG on units under a year) → 5 gain ÷ investment × 12 ÷ months (or IRR).
