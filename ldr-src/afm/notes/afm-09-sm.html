1. Introduction

  • Derivative: value derived from one or more basic variables (bases: underlying asset, index or reference rate). Underlyings: equity, forex, commodity.
  • Importance of underlying: every derivative depends on it for value; change in a forward's value ≈ change in the underlying; no valuable underlying → no value; at maturity profit/loss set by the underlying's price (above contract price → buyer gains; below → buyer loses).

2. Forward Contract

  • Agreement obliging the seller to deliver a specified asset (quality, quantity) on a specified date and place, and the buyer to pay a pre-negotiated price. Nothing standardised — parties negotiate every term (gold: weight, fineness, place, period, price).
  • Farmer sells wheat forward: secures cost + reasonable profit, gives up bumper upside.
  • Buyer = long, seller = short. Example: 100 tolas at ₹ 30,000/tola for 31 Dec 2013 → L pays ₹ 30 lakh, S delivers.

3. Future Contract

  • Agreement to buy/sell a financial instrument or commodity at a specific price on a future date; completed by physical delivery, cash settlement or offset before expiry. Both sides put "good faith money" in a margin account (levels set by regulators, exchanges, brokers).
  • Settlement price = representative price of the closing minutes (or exchange's choice, e.g. average of high and low).
  • Marking-to-market: corn 5,000 kg at ₹ 5 → settles ₹ 5.20 → S loses ₹ 1,000, B gains ₹ 1,000 in margin; contract rolled to next day at ₹ 5.20.

4. Pricing / Valuation of Forward / Future Contracts

  • Basis = Spot price − Futures price. Normal market: spot < futures (futures include full cost of carry) → basis negative = contango. Spot > futures (factors other than carry) → basis positive = backwardation / inverted. Basis → 0 at expiry = convergence.
  • Futures price exceeds the cost-of-carry price → cash-and-carry: borrow, buy spot, sell futures, deliver. Below → reverse (sell spot, buy futures).

5.1 Single Stock Futures

  • Standardised contract to buy/sell a specific stock at a future date at an agreed price; completed by offset or delivery. Margin normally 20% of notional value. Most not held to expiry; close a long by an offsetting short and vice versa.

5.2 Index Futures

  • Based on index level (S&P 500, Dow, NIFTY, Sensex); used to speculate on the market or hedge a portfolio. Not tangible → all settlements in cash.
  • Contract value = lot size × index value: 1 Nifty contract = 50 × Nifty; Nifty 8,000 → ₹ 4,00,000.

SM example: index 1,400, yield 6%, CCRRI 10%, 3 months: 1,400 × e(3/12)(0.10 − 0.06) = 1,400 × 1.01005 = ₹ 1,414.07.

5.2.1 Trading Mechanism in Stock Futures

  • Both buyer and seller deposit initial margin (rules by exchanges); margin requirement is continuous — computed every business day; top-up if below minimum.
  • Square off before expiry, or auto squared off at closing price on expiry (India: last Thursday of the month, as printed in SM).
  • ITC at ₹ 120, margin 20% = ₹ 24; rises to 122 → long credited ₹ 200 (₹ 2 × 100), short debited ₹ 200.

5.2.3 Marking to Market

  • At the end of each session all open contracts are repriced at the settlement price; profit/loss credited/debited to margin. Long at 100, next day 105 → (1) cash profit ₹ 5, (2) old contract at 100 cancelled, (3) new contract at 105. Value of futures reset to zero each day. Better risk management than forwards.
  • Margin account (TYK P14 answer): Initial margin = µ + 3σ (µ = average daily absolute change, σ = SD); maintenance margin = % of initial. Balance below maintenance → margin call to restore to initial margin.

TYK P14 (Sensex, multiple 50): initial 10,000 + 3 × 2,000 = ₹ 16,000; maintenance 75% = ₹ 12,000. Daily change = 50 × (today's close − yesterday's). 6/2: 12,695 (above 12,000, no call). 7/2: −905 → 11,790 → call ₹ 4,210 (back to 16,000). 18/2: 18,275 − 7,760 → call ₹ 5,485. TYK T2 = this section.

5.2.4 Advantages of Stock Index Futures over Stock Futures TYK T1

  • Flexibility for portfolio hedging (institutions prefer).
  • Leveraged speculative gains; stock futures — gains limited, liabilities greater.
  • Most cost-efficient hedge; stock futures costlier.
  • Cannot be easily manipulated.
  • Less volatile (average of many stocks) → lower capital adequacy and margins; risk diversification.
  • Sell as readily as buy, same margin.
  • Cash settled (index accepted as settlement price) vs physical delivery for stocks.
  • Less regulatory complexity.
  • Insurance for a portfolio in a falling market.

5.2.5 Uses / Advantages of Stock Index Futures Asked: Sep 2025 Q4(b) 4m

  • Change weightings / risk exposure (e.g. 60% US / 40% Japan: buy US index futures, sell Nikkei).
  • Separate market timing from selection: buy index futures + PV in risk-free = market exposure; sell unwanted stocks and hold futures until new picks are decided.
  • Index arbitrage (program trading): futures above fair value → buy the basket, sell futures.
  • Hedge portfolio value against systematic risk: short hedge / sell-hedge when long the portfolio. Cash and futures must move similarly; amounts invested should be the same. Perfect hedges rare due to basis risk.
  • Flexibility; leverage; keep the portfolio through corrections without selling; sell as readily as buy (same margin); transfer risk quickly and efficiently (vs once-a-day MF pricing).

SM hedge example: portfolio $1.2 million, β 1.00, α 6%; market −15% + 1% dividend = −14%, expected −8%. To halve β: S&P 240 × $500 = $120,000 per contract → sell 5 contracts ($600,000). Cheaper than selling shares (capital gains tax, commissions).

6. Options; 6.1–6.3 Stock options, index options, parties

  • Option: privilege sold by one party giving the buyer the right, not obligation, to buy (call) or sell (put) an underlying at an agreed price within a period / on a date.
  • Stock options: no commitment for the buyer; exercised only if price rises above (call) / falls below (put) the strike. Stock = ownership; option = contract only. Limited risk, high potential reward, less capital.
  • Stock index option: call/put on an index — a bet on the market. Portfolio insurance: for each 100S rupees of a β 1.0 portfolio buy one put with strike X.
  • Parties: buyer and writer (seller). Writing creates a new security (neither company nor exchange issues options). Price = premium. Buyer's loss limited to premium; profit theoretically unlimited.

SM example (6.2): portfolio ₹ 10,00,000 = 100 × index 10,000; buy 1 put at 9,000; index falls to 8,500 → portfolio 8,50,000 + put pay-off 1 × (9,000 − 8,500) × 100 = 50,000 → insured value ₹ 9,00,000.

6.4 Premium for Options; 6.5 Types of Options

  • Writer takes premium and assumes the risk of delivering (call) / taking delivery (put); uncovered writer's loss can be unlimited.
  • American: exercise any time up to expiry (SM: most exchange-traded and all stock options). European: only on expiry (most options in India).

7. Option Valuation Techniques

  • European call on a non-dividend stock at expiry = Max(0, S − X); value can never be negative. Premium < value → buyer profits; premium > value → loses (TVM and costs ignored); writer is the opposite.
  • With time left, a zero theoretical value option can still have positive market value — the price may still rise.

7.1 Binomial Model

  • Breaks time to expiry into steps; at each step price moves up or down; a tree of prices is built forward from today to expiry; terminal option values = intrinsic values; then work backwards.
  • A hedged position (buy stock, write options) offsets price movements; option priced so that the hedge earns the risk-free rate.
  • Advantage over Black-Scholes: prices American options — early exercise checked at every node (e.g. deep in-the-money put, dividend); where exercise is better, node value = intrinsic value and flows up the tree.

The flow (every binomial / risk-neutral question):

  1. Pay-off at expiry: call = Max(0, Spot − X); put = Max(0, X − Spot) (X = exercise price).
  2. u, d, r: u = Sup ÷ S0; d = Sdown ÷ S0; r = growth of ₹ 1 at the risk-free rate for one step — use what the question gives (table below).
  3. Risk-neutral probability p = (r − d) ÷ (u − d); 1 − p for down. Derivation: in a risk-neutral world the stock grows at r: S·r = p·S·u + (1 − p)·S·d → r = d + p(u − d) → p = (r − d)/(u − d).
  4. One-step value: C0 = [p·Cu + (1 − p)·Cd] ÷ r.
  5. Two-step: find the three terminal pay-offs (uu, ud, dd), value each middle node by step 4 (Cu, Cd), then value today from Cu, Cd by step 4 again.

7.2 Risk Neutral Method

  • Developed by John Cox and Stephen Ross (1976). Valuation rests on arbitrage, so it is independent of risk preferences — value under any preference set gives the same answer as the binomial model. Simple model; its probabilities are applied in the binomial model.
  • Logic: today's price = PV at the risk-free rate of the expected price. Equivalent form (Illus 2): (Su − S0)p + (Sd − S0)(1 − p) = S0(ert − 1) — expected gain = risk-free gain. Then option value = [p × pay-off up + (1 − p) × pay-off down] ÷ r.

SM example (Company X): 50 = [60p + 40(1 − p)]/1.05 → p = 0.625; value = [10(0.625) + 0(0.375)]/1.05 = 5.95. Illus 2 = TYK P24 (Penchant, S 420, 500 / 400, X 450, 8%, 3 months, e0.02 = 1.0202): 80p − 20(1 − p) = 420 × 0.0202 = 8.48 → 100p = 28.48 → p = 0.2848; call = 0.2848 × (500 − 450)/1.0202 = 0.2848 × 50/1.0202 = ₹ 13.96.

7.3 Black-Scholes Model Asked: May 2026 Q6(b) 7m TYK T3

  • Theoretical price = what a writer needs as compensation for writing a call and completely hedging by buying stock. Hedger's view of future prices is irrelevant → risk-neutral valuation (underlying assumed to earn the risk-free rate). Based on a normal distribution of returns.
  • Assumptions: European options; no transaction costs; short-term interest rates known and constant; stocks pay no dividend; price follows a random walk; returns normally distributed; variance constant over the option's life.
  • Advantage: speed. Limitation: cannot accurately price American options — values only at expiry, ignores early exercise.

7.4 Greeks; 7.4.1 Factors Affecting Value of an Option TYK T4

  • Greeks: statistical values (as percentages) giving a view of how a stock has been performing; help choose option strategies; based on past trends, not guaranteed.
  • Price of underlying: price up → calls up, puts down; price down → puts up, calls down.
  • Time to expiry: time decay is the buyer's enemy, faster near expiry (final month) — good for the seller.
  • Volatility: statistical (historical) volatility measures the past; models need future volatility → implied by working the model "backwards" from the market price.
  • Interest rate: higher rates → lower PV of exercise price.

7.5 Exotic Options; 7.5.1 Exotic vs Traditional; 7.5.2 Types

  • Structure and features differ from plain vanilla (American/European), including expiry; a hybrid between American and European. vs traditional: vary in pay-off and time of exercise; more complex; mostly OTC.
  • Chooser: after a set period buyer decides call or put. Compound (split fee, option on option): right to buy another option; pay-off depends on second strike. Barrier: activated only if price reaches a level in a set period. Binary (digital): fixed pay-off if an event occurs, nil otherwise. Asian: pay-off on average price over a period. Bermuda: exercise on certain dates or expiry. Basket: on a weighted portfolio. Spread: on difference between two prices. Look back: holder picks the most favourable strike from min/max price during life.

8. Credit Derivatives

  • Started 1996 for banks to hedge lending portfolios. Risks: market risk (adverse stock, interest, FX moves) and credit risk (counterparty / default). Financial derivatives hedge market risk; credit derivatives transfer credit risk from risk-averse investors to those willing to take it.

8.1 Collateralized Debt Obligations (CDOs); 8.1.1 Types; 8.1.2 Risks

  • Securitisation is backed by loans/receivables; CDOs by pools of bonds, ABS, REITs and other CDOs — covers CBOs and CLOs.
  • Cash flow CDO: backed by cash market debt (low risk weight); relies on collateral's risk weight and cash generation.
  • Synthetic CDO: credit risk transferred without transfer of assets (economic effect only) via CDS or Credit Linked Notes; used to hedge rather than fund; customer relations unaffected; regulatory capital relief; popular in Europe (less documentation). Sub-types: unfunded (only CDS), fully funded (CLN), partially funded (both).
  • Arbitrage CDO: issuer captures the spread between collateral return and borrowing cost, plus management fee.
  • Risks: default (credit; hits equity/junior tranche first); interest rate / basis (floating assets vs fixed liabilities; swaps, caps, floors, collars); liquidity (coupon mismatch); prepayment (fixed-rate debtors with call option repay when rates fall); reinvestment; foreign exchange.

8.2 Credit Default Swaps (CDSs); 8.2.1–8.2.4

  • Credit (loan) + Default (non-payment) + Swap (exchange of risk): an insurance (not strictly) against default on debentures/bonds. Buyer pays periodic premium; seller assumes default risk; settlement only on default. No element of choice → resembles a swap more than an option. Premium depends on the underlying's credit risk.
  • Features: non-standardised private contract (forward category); not exchange-traded, free from regulation; ISDA guidelines; bought from third party against borrower default; raises bond investor confidence; premium has positive relation to risk; without exposure = naked CDS.
  • Uses: hedging; arbitrage (CDS + asset swap, fixed coupon swapped to floating); speculation.
  • Parties: reference entity (initial borrower), buyer (investor, buys protection), seller (writer).
  • Settlement: physical — buyer delivers bonds, seller pays par; cash — seller pays par − market value; credit event auction sets one price for cash settlement.

SM example: BB Corp buys CDS from SS Bank on $10 million bonds of Danger Corp. Physical: SS pays $10 million, receives the bonds. Cash: bonds at 30% → SS pays $10 million − $3 million = $7 million.

  • Capital budgeting approach using option pricing theory; improves project value estimates; supplements, does not replace, DCF.

9.2 Valuation of Real Options

  • Same methods as financial options — Binomial, Risk Neutral, Black-Scholes (see 7.1–7.3); difficulty is identifying inputs.

9.3 Type of Real Options; 9.3.1 Growth Options

  • Accept a negative/insignificant-NPV project for future value — like a European call. Examples: R&D, heavy advertisement, initial investment in a foreign market, acquiring making rights, vacant plot for later development. Purposes: competitive position (strategic investment), knowledge of profitability, manufacturing/making flexibility.
  • Patent valued by B-S with delay cost treated like a dividend: C = S·N(d1)·e−Dt − E·e−rt·N(d2); d1 = [loge(S/E) + (R − D + σ²/2)t] ÷ σ√t; d2 = d1 − σ√t. S = PV of cash flows, E = development cost, D = 1/patent life (SM's Illus 4 symbols: E = X, σ = v, R = r).

9.3.2 Abandonment Option Asked: Jan 2026 Q6(b) 4m

  • Like an American put: exercise if the value from the project's assets (disposal) exceeds PV of continuing. Pay-off = Max(0, abandonment value − project value).
  • Risk-neutral p from returns: risk-free rate = p × up return + (1 − p) × down return (as Illus 5: 8% = p × 30% + (1 − p) × (−40%)); value = expected pay-off ÷ (1 + risk-free rate).

Illus 5 (IPL): PV 100 crore → 130 (+30%) / 60 (−40%); disposal 80 → pay-off 0 / 20. 0.08 = 0.30p − 0.40(1 − p) → p = 0.48/0.70 = 0.686; expected 0.314 × 20 = 6.28 crore; ÷ 1.08 = ₹ 5.81 crore (value of put).

10. Commodity Derivatives; 10.1 Necessary Conditions

  • Began to protect farmers; CBOT 1848; Chicago Produce Exchange 1874 (now CME).
  • Attributes: (1) durable and storable (temporal allocation of stocks; hedge for carriers of stock); (2) homogeneous units (allows delivery); (3) frequent, wide price fluctuations, large supply and demand; (4) supply flows naturally to market and breakdowns in forward contracting (cash market risk must exist).
  • Contract must be a close substitute for the cash commodity — hedging effectiveness. Customer needs: instrumental (price risk reduction — core service) and convenience (flexibility, easy access, efficient clearing — process quality, peripheral services).

10.2 Investing in Commodity Derivatives

  • Now an investment tool; most traders don't need the commodity. Direct exposure (steel derivative instead of Tata Steel shares); easier to forecast via demand/supply; cheap (small margin).
  • Cash settlement option currently lies with the seller. Settlement and clearing by the clearing house (guards against default).
  • Benefits: complement investment in commodity-using companies; invest in a country's consumption and production; no dividends, only price returns.

SM example (soybean): buy 1 tonne at ₹ 8,700, sells ₹ 9,000 → ₹ 300 = 3.4%. Futures at ₹ 8,700 with 10% margin ₹ 870 → ₹ 300 profit = 34.5%; at ₹ 8,400 → loss 34.5%.

10.3 Commodity Market; 10.4 Commodity Futures

  • Origin: Sumer clay tokens. Raw/primary products traded on regulated exchanges in standardised contracts. Advantages: money managers prefer derivatives to tangibles; less hassle (delivery); indirect investment in real assets — hedge against inflation.
  • Almost all commodities allowed in futures from April 2003; national multi-commodity exchanges, online. Futures = speculating on price direction; "buy"/"sell" = expected direction. Farmer sells futures (corn); cereal maker (Kellogg) buys futures. Speculators provide liquidity and offset before delivery.
  • Advantages: easiest and cheapest way to invest; 3 categories — agricultural (soft), energy, metals.

10.5 Commodity Swaps; 10.5.1 Types; 10.5.2 Valuing

  • Users: producers (fix selling prices — gold, cattle), end-users (fix purchase prices — university electricity, airline jet fuel), speculators (liquidity; inflation hedge; trend opportunities).
  • Fixed-floating: like IRS but both indices commodity-based (S&P GSCI, CRB). Commodity-for-interest: like an equity swap — total return on the commodity vs a money market rate ± spread.
  • Valuing: a strip of forwards, each priced at inception with zero market value. Peculiarities: cost of hedging; institutional structure; liquidity of the underlying; seasonality; variability of futures bid/offer spread; brokerage; credit risk, capital and admin costs.

10.6 Hedging with Commodity Derivatives

  • Often no exact contract (refined chemical) → OTC price shaded using correlation with a liquid product (crude). Correlation is an un-hedgeable risk for the OTC market maker; his best hope is two-way business (natural hedge). Other swaps (currency, IRS) are in their own chapters.

11. Weather Derivatives

  • Underlying = a weather measure (rainfall, temperature, humidity, wind). Primary objective: hedge volume risk, not price risk. First deal 1997 OTC by Aquila Energy; market jump-started by El Nino winter 1997–98.
  • vs insurance: insurance covers extreme, low-probability events; weather derivatives also cover uncertainty in normal conditions. Seller gets premium; pays if buyer suffers loss from adverse weather.
  • Pricing issues (cannot be stored): data reliability; forecasting (seasonal, not daily levels); temperature modelling (no perfect, universal model).

12. Electricity Derivatives; 12.1 Forwards; 12.2 Futures; 12.3 Swap

  • India: state distribution companies sign long-term PPAs (20–25 years). Spot prices volatile (small market, fuel supply, weather, transmission congestion, RE variation) → hedging needed for generators, buyers and load serving entities. Vanilla forms: forwards, futures, swaps.
  • Forwards: obligation to buy/sell fixed electricity at forward price F at time T; custom-tailored. Pay-off = ST − F; electricity is a different commodity at different times; ST usually the average price over the delivery period.
  • Futures: standardised; smaller quantity than forwards; exchange-traded → more transparent; mostly financially settled; lower credit risk and monitoring costs (margins, daily pay-out). Advantages: market consensus, transparency, liquidity, lower costs. Limitation: basis risks from rigid specifications and limited quantities.
  • Swap: pay fixed for floating electricity price (or vice versa) for a fixed quantity; short-to-medium-term certainty up to a couple of years; a strip of forwards with identical prices. Locational basis swap: pay/receive difference between a futures price and another location's spot price.

13. Derivative Mishaps and Lessons; 13.1 Orange County

  • Warren Buffett: derivatives are "financial weapons of mass destruction".
  • Orange County (1994): Treasurer Robert Citron, no trading background; yield curve play with leverage; $2.8 billion inverse floaters; earned 300–400 bp above peers (1992–93); fund ~$7.5 billion leveraged via repo to ~$20 billion. Fed's six rate rises in 1994 → $515 million margin (Feb–May 1994); collateral $10 billion sold; loss about $1.5 billion (20%); resigned 3 Dec 1994; bankrupt 6 Dec 1994; 1996 one year jail, fine $1,00,000.

Note (app-written): the SM opens 13.1 (p 9.59) with a loss of "about $2 billion" but later (p 9.60) says Citron admitted a loss of about $1.5 billion (20%). Both are ICAI's figures.

13.2 Barings Bank; 13.3 P&G and Gibson Greetings

  • Barings: Nick Leeson, Nikkei 225 futures arbitrage Singapore vs Osaka → switched to speculation (market and event risk). Ran both floor and back office; hid losses in error account '8888'; 1994 bonus $7,20,000; $354 million margin given unquestioned. Jan 1995: $7 billion notional Nikkei + short JGB futures; Kobe earthquake 17 Jan 1995 → loss $827 million ($1.4 billion). 233-year-old bank bankrupt; ING bought it for £1 (March 1995). Gave rise to operational risk capital in Basel II.
  • Bankers Trust (1994): sold complex products misrepresented as tailored. P&G: leveraged (ratio / power) swap — profit capped at 0.75% per 6 months, loss unlimited; loss $106 million. Gibson Greetings (1992): ratio and basis swaps — receive 5.5%, pay floating rate squared ÷ 6; loss $3 million (Feb 1994), $16.7 million (Apr 1994). Settled out of court; BT taken over by Deutsche Bank (1999). Rule: don't sell a product the client cannot understand.

13.4 Lessons from Derivative Mishaps (13.4.1–13.4.6)

  • Don't buy what you don't understand — value the instrument in-house (Orange County, BT).
  • Due diligence before making Treasury a profit centre — don't link treasurer's pay to profit (Citron).
  • Specify risk limits — limit position sizes, daily reports even for star traders (Barings).
  • Separate front, middle and back offices — firewall, defined roles (Leeson).
  • Hedger should not become a speculator — risk limits, risk analysis vs risk appetite.
  • Stress test, scenario analysis — VaR followed by scenario analysis; 10–20 years data for Black Swan; simulation (Gibson).
