1. Introduction — spot and forward market

  • Exchange rate = price of one nation's currency in terms of another (the reference currency). ₹ 100/$ → rupee is the reference currency; $ 0.01/₹ → dollar is.
  • Spot rate: delivery within two business days after the deal. Forward rate: rate fixed today for delivery on a future date (usually 30, 60, 90 or 180 days); payment and delivery at maturity.
  • (a) Spot market: India quotes rupees per one unit of foreign currency. (b) Forward market: buyer and seller agree today a specific amount, rate and future date; rate stays fixed whatever the spot does. The forward rate is not the future spot rate (which depends on demand and supply on that day).
  • Forex is the only market where barter happens — money delivered for money (selling £ forward = buying ₹ forward).

Illus 1: one rupee buys $ 0.0217; ₹ 46.08 buys one dollar (reciprocals).

2. Role of SWIFT in Foreign Exchange

  • SWIFT (Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication): founded 1973, HQ La Hulpe (Belgium), non-profit; purely a messaging system — standardised communication, not a settlement system. Assigns 3-letter currency codes (USD, GBP, CNY, CAD, AUD, HKD, INR, JPY, NZD, SGD, SEK, CHF, EUR). Connects institutions in 200+ countries.
  • Why important: quick settlement (messaging in seconds, plus validation and documentation); security and reliability → lower operational risk; standardisation → efficiency and lower cost; full backup and recovery; catalyst for collaboration among financial agencies.

3. National and International Payment Gateways

  • Payment gateway: virtual equivalent of physical cash transfer; an encrypted channel that authenticates and routes payment details from buyer's PC/mobile to banks for authorisation; completes the order with a reference number.
  • Benefits: 24x7x365; real-time card authorisation; rapid processing; multiple payment options; risk minimised by encryption; real-time reports; customer refund; merchants avoid complex software / huge data; CA-authenticated secure servers; cheap bulk data collection with card validity check; multiple host interfaces; simple administrative control; customers' support and merchants' trust.
  • Challenges: (a) customer may have no account with supporting banks; (b) limited banks in some gateways; (c) reliability, delivery, limited avenues, lack of trust in the provider.
  • International gateways: multi-currency, multi-language; customer pays in own currency → higher conversion, easy benchmarking of prices.

4. Nostro, Vostro and Loro Accounts

  • Current accounts banks keep for quick transfer of funds in different currencies: "our", "your", "their".
  • Nostro ("our account with you"): bank's FC account held abroad in that country's currency — Indian bank's CHF account in Switzerland.
  • Vostro: foreign bank's account with a local bank — Citibank's account with SBI is Vostro for SBI. Nostro for one = Vostro for the other.
  • Loro: using a third bank's Nostro account to settle — IDBI (no Nostro with Citi) asks HDFC (has account with Citi) to settle the import bill.

4.1–4.2 Exchange Position and Cash Position

  • Exchange position: total of purchase and sale commitments of the bank, whether delivery has taken place or not — entered on the contract date. Purchases > sales = overbought; sales > purchases = oversold.
  • Cash position: balance in the Nostro account — credited for purchases, debited for sales, only when actual delivery takes place.
  • Every cash-position item affects exchange position, not vice versa. Only ready deliveries (TT remitted) hit the Nostro; forward deals, bills purchased, cancellations hit only the exchange position.
  • Cancellation of a forward purchase = a sale entry; cancellation of a draft sold = a purchase entry.

Illus 2 (ABC Bank, £): purchases 35,000 + 12,500 + 40,000 + 15,000 (draft cancelled) = 1,02,500; sales 30,000 + 15,000 + 37,500 = 82,500 → overbought £ 20,000. Nostro 65,000 − 37,500 = £ 27,500. To keep Nostro £ 7,500: sell £ 20,000 spot (position → nil); to keep £ 7,500 overbought: buy £ 7,500 forward.

TYK P51 (CHF): purchases 1,60,000, sales 1,65,000 → oversold 5,000; Nostro 1,00,000 − 75,000 = 25,000 → buy spot 5,000 (Nostro 30,000, position nil) and buy forward 10,000.

5.1–5.2 American / European terms; Direct and Indirect quote

  • American terms: US$ per unit of foreign currency. European terms: foreign currency per US$ (most currencies are quoted this way), e.g. INR 44.92 per $.
  • Direct quote: home currency price of one unit of foreign currency — $ 1 = ₹ 48.00 for an Indian. Indirect quote: foreign currency price of one unit of home currency — ₹ 1 = $ 0.0208.
  • Direct quote = 1 ÷ Indirect quote (and vice versa). On reversing a two-way quote, bid and ask swap sides: 1/ask becomes the new bid (TYK P40: $/£ 1.5617–1.5673 → £/$ 0.63804–0.64033).

Note (app-written): SM 5.1 prints "U.S. dollar 0.2 per unit of Indian rupee" as the American quote beside INR 44.92/$; the reciprocal of 44.92 is about $ 0.0223.

5.3 Bid, Offer and Spread

  • Two-way quote: Bid = rate at which the dealer buys the currency; Offer/Ask = rate at which he sells. ₹ 48.80–48.90/$: buys $ at 48.80, sells $ at 48.90. Offer is always higher — dealer buys at bid, sells at offer.
  • A bid in one currency is simultaneously an offer in the other.
  • Spread % = (Offer − Bid) ÷ Bid × 100.
  • Dealer jargon: "ONE MINE" = I buy one million $ from you (at your ask); "ONE YOURS" = I sell one million $ to you (at your bid).
  • Customer side (logic used in all TYK answers): customer sells FC → bank's bid; customer buys FC → bank's ask. Always the rate worse for the customer.

5.4 Cross Rates Asked: Jan 2026 Q3(b) 4m

  • Pick each leg from the side the bank actually deals on: when the bank buys the third currency, it buys $ at ask locally and sells $ for the third currency at the bid abroad.

5.5 Pips

  • PIP (Price Interest Point) = smallest unit by which a quote can change (like tick size). USD/INR to customer ₹ 61.75 → pip 0.01; Indian interbank quotes to 4 decimals → 0.0001. EUR/USD 1.0213/1.0219 → spread 0.0006 = 6 pips.

5.6 Forward exchange rate quotation

  • Market convention: forward quoted as spot + forward margin (points), not as an outright rate. Spot 61.53/54, 6-month outright 61.93/98 → dealer quotes only 40/44 (61.93 − 61.53; 61.98 − 61.54). The "big figure" (61) is assumed known.
  • Forward margin is not the dealer's profit. Higher future price → forward premium; lower → forward discount.
  • Annualised: 40 paise per 6 months → 80 paise a year → 0.4 × 2 × 100 ÷ 61.53 = 1.30% p.a. The forward market is really an interest rate market (compares the two currencies' interest rates).

Note (app-written): SM prints "61.98 – 62.54"; the 44 points come from 61.98 − 61.54.

5.7 Forward point determination

  • Direct quote: points added when the FC is at a premium, deducted at a discount. Indirect quote: exactly the opposite.
  • How to tell without a sign: spread must widen with time (uncertainty and liquidity). Ascending points (5/8, 43/45) → premium → add; descending (25/20, 35/25) → discount → deduct. GBP 1 = $ 1.6000–1.6010, points 5–8 → $ 1.6005–1.6018.
  • Points reflect the interest rate differential: the currency with the lower interest rate is at a premium; higher yield → cheaper forward. Aberration → arbitrage pushes back to equilibrium.
  • Points = pips of the forward; EUR/USD spot 1.1323, forward 1.1328 → 5 points (1 point = 0.0001).

5.8 Broken period forward rate Asked: Sep 2025 MCQ CS-IV 6m

  • Interbank forwards are for standard amounts and standard month-end dates; customer deals are odd amounts and odd dates (export bill realised 23 Feb). Points for the odd date are interpolated — assumed to grow uniformly (pro rata) over the period.
  • SM: premium for the odd date = 10*53/59 paise, "53 & 59 are broken & full periods", i.e. full-period points × broken period ÷ full period. Between two standard dates (TYK P5): (c) = (b) − (a) for the month between, (d) = (c)/2 for 15 days, (e) = (a) + (d).
  • Then: merchant forward rate = spot ± broken points ± bank's margin (margin deducted for exporter, added for importer).

SM example: 1 Jan spot ₹ 62, 2-month (28 Feb) premium 10 paise → 62.10; margin 3 paise → importer 62.13, exporter 62.07. For 23 Feb: 10 × 53/59 = 8.98 paise → 62.0898 ≈ 62.09 → exporter 62.06, importer 62.12.

5.9 Merchant Rates

  • Nobody fixes the rate — market (demand and supply) decides. RBI does not fix but monitors volatility and intervenes by buying/selling USD. Wholesale interbank rate is the base for customer rates.
  • Merchant rates = rates to all customers (even an NRI remitting $) vs interbank rates between banks. FEDAI guidelines apply because of the customer-service element.
  • Till 1998 FEDAI prescribed margins; now banks set their own margin depending on their position. Only surviving rule: FEDAI rule 5A.8 — merchant settlements rounded to the nearest whole rupee: USD 1,234 × 61.32 = ₹ 75,668.88 → ₹ 75,669.
  • History: gold standard ended 1971; rupee pegged to sterling, from 1975 to a basket; sterling schedule abolished 1984; FEDAI's margin / spread guidelines now redundant.
  • Card rate: merchant rates for import bill, export bill, inward/outward remittance, with standard spread, advised each morning to branches; used for walk-in and small deals. Regular / high-value customers get a better rate from the dealing room.
  • Exchange margin (in all SM illustrations): deducted from a buying rate, added to a selling rate; round as the question says (often to multiples of 0.0025).

6. Exchange Rate Forecasting

  • Needed for hedging, short-term financing and investment, capital budgeting, earnings assessment, long-term financing. Four groups:
  • Technical: historical data (time series); useful to speculators for day-to-day moves; rarely point/range estimates → limited use to MNCs.
  • Fundamental: relationships between economic variables and rates (subjective, regression, sensitivity). Limits: uncertain timing of impact; need to forecast factors with immediate impact; omission of non-quantifiable factors; sensitivity changes over time.
  • Market-based: current spot/forward rates (speculators make them reflect expected future rates).
  • Mixed: weighted average of several forecasts.

7. Exchange Rate Determination

  • Major factors: inflation (→ PPP) and interest rates (→ IRP). Others:
  • Deficit/surplus on capital/current account: current-account deficit → depreciation, surplus → appreciation; net capital inflow positive → appreciation, negative → depreciation.
  • Trade barriers: more import barriers/quotas → own currency appreciates in the long run.
  • Central bank intervention (buying/selling forex); Government controls (limits on FDI, FPI, repatriation).
  • Expectations (Band Wagon effect): a dominant speculator goes short, others follow → currency falls.

8.2 Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)

  • Focus: inflation – exchange rate relationship; rates reflect relative purchasing power of currencies.
  • Absolute form ("Law of One Price"): prices of similar products in two countries should be equal in a common currency; discrepancy shifts demand until prices converge. Alternative version allows market imperfections (transport costs, tariffs, quotas) via a sectoral constant — prices may differ, but their rate of change should be similar.
  • Problem: millions of products and constants; CPI baskets differ by consumption pattern (Finland vs Malaysia). Relative form overcomes market imperfections and consumption patterns.
  • PPP holds better in the long run and when disturbances are purely monetary.

8.3 International Fisher Effect (IFE)

  • Uses interest rate (not inflation) differentials to explain exchange-rate changes; closely related to PPP since interest rates are highly correlated with inflation.
  • Nominal risk-free rate = real return + anticipated inflation. If all investors want the same real return, interest differentials reflect expected inflation differentials. High-interest currencies will depreciate. Nominal rate also includes default risk.
  • Equation: rD − PD = rF − ∆PF, or PD − PF = ∆S = rD − rF. Terms: r = nominal interest, P = inflation, ∆S = % change in spot. Logic: with free capital flows, investment flows until real returns equalise.
  • If IFE holds, borrowing in one country and investing in another gives no positive return on average. It does not consistently hold because PPP does not (other factors affect rates).

9. Foreign Exchange Market

  • Market where individuals, firms and banks buy and sell foreign currencies; purpose: transfer of purchasing power from one currency to another (trade invoiced in one agreed currency, possibly a third one like $).
  • Demand for FC: tourists going abroad, imports, investing abroad. Supply: foreign tourists, exports, foreign investment received. Commercial banks act as clearing houses.
  • Four levels: (1) tourists, importers, exporters, investors; (2) commercial banks; (3) foreign exchange brokers (banks even out flows among themselves); (4) central bank — lender/buyer of last resort (draws down or adds to reserves).
  • Participants: non-bank entities (MNCs, individuals); banks; speculators (profit from rate fluctuations); arbitrageurs (profit from price differences in two markets simultaneously); governments via central banks (stabilise rates).

10. Foreign Exchange Exposure Asked: May 2026 Q5(c) 2m

  • Exposure: "a contracted, projected or contingent cash flow whose magnitude is not certain at the moment; depends on FX rates and interest rates". FX exposure = parts of business affected if exchange rates change.
  • Examples: traveller (destination currency appreciates); exporter invoicing in FC (FC falls → lower home revenue); importer (FC appreciates → higher cost); fund managers owning foreign assets (currency falls); trade in local currency where rates move the negotiated price.
  • Timing view: translation and transaction exposure look at the moment of the rate change (accounting / settled obligations); operating (economic) exposure looks at expected future cash flows after an unexpected change.

10.1 Transaction Exposure

  • Effect of a rate change on outstanding obligations entered before the change but settled after it — cash flows from existing contractual obligations. Arises whenever there are FC receivables/payables.
  • Exporter with $ 1,00,000 receivable in 6 months: $ depreciates → cash loss; appreciates → gain. Budgeted ₹ 62/$, falls to ₹ 57 → commercial profit wiped off. Most common exposure; runs from budgeting until extinguished by sale/purchase of FC.

10.2 Translation Exposure

  • Gains/losses from translating foreign currency assets and liabilities into the parent's currency for consolidation; also called accounting exposure — accounting-derived changes in owner's equity.
  • SM example: $ 1 m loan for imported capital goods at ₹ 55 → asset and loan ₹ 550 lacs; at year end ₹ 58 → translation loss ₹ 30 lacs, capitalised → asset ₹ 580 lacs → higher depreciation, lower profit.
  • Both transaction and translation exposures hit the bottom line (gain if movement favourable). Translation exposure becomes transaction exposure at some stage — the loan must be repaid by buying $.

11. Hedging Currency Risk — (A) Internal techniques

  • Natural hedging by exporters: short-term FC loans — PCFC (pre-shipment credit in FC) / FCNR(B) loans: (1) cheaper than domestic-currency rates; (2) export collections settle the PCFC loan → transaction risk hedged.
  • Internal techniques involve no explicit transaction cost; offset exposure fully or partly:
  • (i) Invoicing in domestic currency: avoids exposure but currency is part of the marketing package; buyer may prefer its own; markets like oil/aluminium follow competitors' currency; in a buyer's market sellers invoice in the buyer's currency. If invoicing in FC, choose a major currency with an active (deep) forward market at least as long as the credit period; avoid limited-convertibility, chronically weak currencies.
  • (ii) Leading and lagging: leading = pay before due date; lagging = delay after due date — to gain from expected devaluation/revaluation. Payer's currency expected to devalue → lead; expected to revalue → lag.
  • (iii) Netting: group companies settle inter-affiliate debts for the net amount. Bilateral netting between pairs; flows reduced by the lower of purchases/sales. Main problem: settlement currency. Reduces banking costs, spreads and charges; increases central control.
  • (iv) Matching: matches FC inflows with FC outflows by amount and timing; applies to intra-group and third parties (netting = within group only). Needs two-way flows in the same currency (natural matching); parallel matching = different currencies expected to move together. Both need enabling exchange control rules — an MNC subsidiary in India cannot net receivables and payables.
  • (v) Price variation: raise selling prices against adverse rate moves; in some countries the only legal tactic. Transfer price variation in inter-company trade — but tax, customs and exchange control authorities require arm's length price.
  • (vi) Asset and liability management: for balance sheet, income or cash-flow exposure (cash-flow focus makes sense; pure translation focus misplaced). Aggressive: increase exposed inflows in strong currencies / outflows in weak ones. Defensive: match inflows and outflows by currency regardless of strength.

12.1 Forward Rate — Premium and Discount

  • Forwards: customised, not standardised (size, grade, place, date, credit period negotiated). Value change ≈ value change in the underlying; payoff profile differs from options. Buyer gains if price at maturity > contract price; buyer's gain = seller's loss.
  • Premium: currency costlier forward → in direct quote added to both buying and selling rates. Discount: cheaper forward → deducted.
  • Annualised premium/discount = [(Forward Rate – Spot rate) / (Spot rate)] x (12/6) (SM, 6-month example; × 12/months of the forward, × 100 for %; TYK P19 uses × 365/60 for days). Sign + = premium, − = discount. Work it for each currency in its own direct form.

SM example: spot ₹ 55, 6-month ₹ 60 → $ premium (60 − 55)/55 × 12/6 = 18.18%; rupee: (0.01667 − 0.01818)/0.01818 × 12/6 = −16.61% (discount). Premium ≠ discount.

Note (app-written): the SM computes the rupee discount as −16.61% and then says "16.67%"; the gap is rounding (exactly (55/60 − 1) × 2 = −16.67%).

Automatic cancellation (FEDAI Rule 6): an overdue forward contract with no instructions on or before due date is automatically cancelled within 3 working days after maturity. Customer pays any exchange difference but is not entitled to any profit. Late delivery → fresh contract at spot; extension after due date → forward rate for the desired period. Charges:

  • (i) Exchange difference: spot rate of offsetting position on the cancellation date (or 3 working days, whichever is earlier) vs contract rate.
  • (ii) Swap loss: bank offsets at spot on the due date and takes the opposite position at the earliest forward.
  • (iii) Interest on outlay of funds: (bank's interbank cover rate − spot of opposite position on due date) × FC amount, for the period from due date to actual cancellation or 3 working days, whichever is later.
  • Any profit to the bank is not passed on to the customer.

12.3 Non-deliverable Forward Contract (NDF)

  • Cash-settled, short-term forward on a thinly traded or non-convertible currency; P/L = (agreed rate − spot at settlement) × notional amount. Has a fixing date (difference calculated) and settlement date (difference paid). Usually 1 month to 1 year, quoted and settled in US$.

12.4 Rollover of Deliverable Forward Contract

  • Exporter has no FC / importer has no local currency to deliver at maturity → spot cancellation + new contract for a later date.
  • Rationale: non-receipt of FC from client (export); shortage of local currency (import); non-agreement of payment with clients; forwards normally available only up to one year, so longer exposures use rollovers.

15. Swap Contracts

  • "Infinitely flexible": exchange the underlying economic basis of a debt or asset without affecting the principal obligation. Participants exchange cash flows at payment/settlement dates (fixed, or quantity × reference rate).
  • Groups: interest rate, currency, commodity, equity. Notional principal (except currency swaps) is used only to compute payments, not exchanged; interim payments usually netted. Users: large banks and corporations; swaps create credit exposure.

15.1–15.2 Interest Rate Swaps; Currency Swaps

  • 15.1 IRS: covered in Chapter 12 of the SM.
  • 15.2 Currency swap = exchange of liabilities between currencies. Three stages: (1) spot exchange of principal; (2) continuing exchange of interest — a series of forwards, usually at the initial spot rate; (3) re-exchange of principal on maturity.
  • Benefits: hedge currency risk; cost savings (strong Yen borrower raises in Tokyo and swaps into $); access to currencies that would otherwise command a high premium; diversification of borrowings.
  • Currency coupon swap (Circus swap): fixed or floating interest in one currency for floating in another. Principal exchanged at the start, at the end, both, or neither. Often linked to a Eurobond issue — issue where cheapest, swap into the required currency via the underwriter.

TYK P50 (Drilldip, ₹ 500 crore in, ₹ 740 crore back): swap — buy ₹ 500 cr at ₹ 50 (US$ 100 m), swap back at ₹ 50 = $ 100 m + ₹ 240 cr at ₹ 54 = $ 44.44 m − interest 8% $ 8 m → net US$ 36.44 m; without swap ₹ 740 cr ÷ 54 = $ 137.04 m − 8 → US$ 29.04 m → opt for swap.

15.3–15.4 Commodity Swaps; Equity Swaps

  • Commodity swap: series of futures-like settlements on a notional amount over multiple dates at specified reference prices/indices; mainly for oil price risk.
  • Equity swap: total return on equity or an index (dividend + capital) exchanged for a fixed or floating interest rate.

17. Strategies for Exposure Management (17.1–17.4)

  • Shaped by attitude to risk, financial strength, nature of business, vulnerability; no single strategy fits all. Four options:
  • 17.1 Low risk – low reward: automatic hedging in the forward market as soon as exposures arise. Merits: known yields/costs, little cash-flow destabilisation, no managerial time. Demerit: hardly optimum costs — not an optimum strategy (currencies behave like commodities).
  • 17.2 Low risk – reasonable reward: selective hedging when forward rates are attractive, else keep open; needs quantified expectations; like a bond (hedged = known) and equity (unhedged = uncertain) mix.
  • 17.3 High risk – low reward: leave all exposures unhedged — perhaps the worst; very high cash-flow destabilisation; only merit zero managerial effort.
  • 17.4 High risk – high reward: active trading through continuous cancellations and re-bookings; trading becomes a profit centre; adopt only in full consciousness of risks.

18. Conclusion

  • Globalisation gives better international allocation of capital and diversification, but brings exchange-rate risk, political risk and interdependence. Rising rate volatility, trade, global markets and MNCs make study of these risks and their reduction imperative.
