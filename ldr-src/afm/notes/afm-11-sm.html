1. International Capital Budgeting · 1.1 Complexities Involved

  • Foreign cash flows must be converted into parent's currency.
  • Parent cash flows differ from project cash flows.
  • Remitted profits taxed in home and host country.
  • Foreign exchange risk on parent's cash flow.
  • Inflation changes shift the competitive environment and cash flows.
  • Host-country restrictions on distribution of cash flows.
  • Initial investment in host country to benefit from release of blocked funds.
  • Political risk reduces chance of expected cash flows.
  • Host-country concessions/benefits raise profitability.
  • Terminal value hard to estimate — buyers in parent company have divergent views.

1.2 Problems Affecting Foreign Investment Analysis

  • Foreign exchange risk: forecast host-country inflation over project life; adjust local-currency cash flows for inflation; convert at spot rate × expected depreciation from PPP.
  • Remittance restrictions: limits on transfer of profits, depreciation, technical differences → project CF ≠ parent CF. Diluted by internal transfer prices, overhead payments. Blocked funds adjusted at their opportunity cost.
  • Taxation: use after-tax cash flows. Two tax regimes, royalties/dividends/management fees, withholding tax, tax treaties, tax discrimination (remitted vs reinvested profits) complicate it. MNCs cut overall tax via transfer pricing.
  • Rule: compute parent's after-tax CF at the higher of home/host tax rate. Feasible → acceptable under a more favourable regime too. Not feasible → bring in other tax-saving aspects to see if it crosses the hurdle rate.

1.3 Project vis-a-vis Parent Cash Flows

  • Difference arises from tax rules, exchange controls; management fees and royalty are returns to the parent.
  • Project (own) CF basis: project must beat local competitors, else parent would buy local equity/government bonds. But foreign projects often replace imports, not compete locally. Avoids currency conversion and exchange-rate problems.
  • Parent CF basis: operating + financial cash flows actually remitted are the yardstick (dividends to shareholders, debt service). Evaluate on net after-tax operating CF; keep financial CF separate from operating CF.

1.4 Discount Rate and Adjusting Cash Flows

  • Old practice: raise discount rate for foreign risk. Not proper to combine all risks into one rate:
    • Political-risk premium in the rate penalises early cash flows more than distant ones.
    • Raising the rate for adverse currency moves ignores gains from favourable moves.
  • Better: adjust cash flows (certainty equivalents, probability, decision tree) for political, exchange and other risks; discount at host- or parent-country rate.

1.5 Adjusted Present Value (APV) Asked: May 2026 Q3(a) 6m

  • Value-additive approach: each cash flow discounted separately at a rate matching its own risk.
  • Test basic viability first; if feasible, stop. If not, add the other complexities (tax shields, subsidies).
FormulaTerms / logic
APV = −I0 + Σ Xt ÷ (1 + k*)t + Σ Tt ÷ (1 + id)t + Σ St ÷ (1 + id)tI0 = PV of investment outlay (net of usable blocked funds); Xt = operating CF, discounted at k* = unlevered cost of capital; Tt = tax saving from financing mix (interest tax shield); St = before-tax value of interest subsidies from project-specific financing; last two at id = before-tax cost of debt (they are as certain as debt).
  • Blocked funds: balances abroad that cannot be remitted. Direct = exchange-control bar. Indirect = heavy local/withholding tax on remittance. If released for the new project, they reduce the investment outlay.

2. International Sources of Finance · 2.1 Foreign Currency Convertible Bonds (FCCBs)

  • Convertible bond issued in a currency other than issuer's domestic currency; mix of debt and equity: regular coupon + principal, plus option to convert into stock.
  • Investors: safety of guaranteed payments + share in price appreciation (via warrants activated at a set price). Issuer: lower coupon due to equity element.
AdvantagesDisadvantages
  • Flexibility to convert into equity or redeem at end of period (normally 3 years).
  • Delayed dilution of equity and EPS.
  • Easily marketable: conversion option + minimum fixed interest.
  • Exchange risk on interest in foreign currency → only firms with low D/E and large forex earnings opt.
  • More debt and forex outgo on interest.
  • Low interest (around 3–4%) but exchange risk on interest and repayment if not converted. Only major plus: high-growth firm can issue shares later above current price.

2.2 American Depository Receipts (ADRs)

  • Depository receipts issued in the USA; must follow stringent SEC provisions.
  • Created by deposit of non-US company's securities with a custodian bank in issuer's country; custodian informs the US depository, which issues ADRs.
  • USD denominated, traded like US securities; holder gets same rights as owners of underlying shares.
  • GDR = same structure, but traded in more than one currency and within as well as outside the US.

2.3 Global Depository Receipts (GDRs) Asked: Jan 2026 Q5(b) 6m

  • Negotiable certificate, denominated in a currency not native to the issuer, representing its publicly traded local-currency equity shares. Mostly USD, a few in Euro/Pound. Issued in US = ADR; outside US = GDR. Can represent debt in theory, rarely in practice.
  • Created when local shares are delivered to the depository's local custodian bank; depository bank (e.g. Bank of New York) issues DRs in USD; traded on a foreign exchange, OTC, or among QIBs. Indian issues are GDRs as they are marketed globally.
  • Why Indian companies used them: equity (no principal repayment, lower servicing cost); no other form of term forex funding was available; fetched higher prices than a domestic issue.

Impact of GDRs on Indian capital market (TYK T2):

  • Market shifting from Bombay to Luxemburg.
  • Arbitrage possibility in GDR issues.
  • Market no longer independent of the world — investors must track worldwide events.
  • Retail investors sidelined — GDR/FII placements + free pricing end easy money on discounted rights/public issues.
  • Result: considerable foreign investment flowed into India.

Markets of GDRs:

  • Sold primarily to institutional investors; demand dominated by emerging market funds.
  • FIIs likely to switch from ordinary shares into GDRs.
  • Major demand: UK, USA (QIBs), South East Asia (Hong Kong, Singapore), some continental Europe (France, Switzerland).

Mechanism: company issues ordinary shares → kept with custodian/depository bank → GDRs issued to foreign investors.

Characteristics:

  • Economic benefits of shareholders but no voting rights.
  • Settled through CEDEL & Euro-clear; listed on Luxemburg stock exchange.
  • Traded OTC between professional market makers; freely traded; marketed globally in more than one currency.
  • Dividends paid in issuer currency, converted into dollars by depository → exchange risk with investor.
  • Cancellation any time after a cooling period of 45 days: depository asks domestic custodian to release underlying shares; BSE/NSE price on date of redemption advice = cost of acquisition.

2.4 Euro-Convertible Bonds (ECBs)

  • Debt with option to convert into a predetermined number of equity shares; conversion price usually at a premium; fixed interest.
  • Call option (issuer's): issuer can call bonds before maturity when share price is far above redemption value — forces conversion; usually when price reaches 130% to 150% of conversion price.
  • Put option (holder's): holder can sell bonds back to issuer at a pre-determined price and date.
  • Interest and redemption paid by issuer in US dollars.

2.5 Other Sources

  • Euro-convertible zero coupon bonds: no interest; converted at maturity at a pre-determined price; usually 5 years; a deferred equity issue.
  • Euro-bonds with equity warrants: market-based coupon; detachable warrants; pure bonds trade at a discount; suit fixed income funds.
  • Syndicated bank loans: large loans from banks, good credit rating, quick, few formalities; 5 to 10 years; rate = index (say SOFR) + spread by credit rating; covenants (key ratios).
  • Euro-bonds: debt denominated in a currency, issued outside the country of that currency (e.g. Yen bond floated in France); refuge from tax/regulation, yield arbitrage; usually bearer; forms: fixed rate, FRNs, convertible.
  • Foreign bonds: denominated in a currency foreign to the borrower and sold in the country of that currency; subject to that country's restrictions on foreigners.
  • Euro commercial papers: short-term, issued at a discount, maturity < 1 year.
  • Credit instruments: used for foreign remittances; differ in speed of receipt; rate varies with loss of interest and risk → different exchange rates for different instruments.

3. International Financial Centre (GIFT City)

  • IFC: financial centre catering to customers outside their own jurisdiction; hub for funds, products and services in own land but under a different set of regulations and laws; flexibility in currency trading, insurance, banking.

3.1 Benefits of IFC

  • Qualified professionals working abroad can come and practise here.
  • Platform to pursue global opportunities without leaving homeland.
  • Stops brain drain.
  • Brings back financial services transactions now done abroad.
  • Trading of complicated financial derivatives from India.

3.2 Constituents of IFC

  • Highly developed infrastructure.
  • Stable political environment (avoids country risk).
  • Strategic location — near airport, seaport; friendly weather.
  • Quality life — to retain highly paid professionals.
  • Rational regulatory framework — fair and transparent.
  • Sustainable economy — absorbs shocks, boosts investor confidence.

3.3 GIFT City — India's International Financial Services Centre

  • Idea coined in 2007 to compete with Dubai, Hong Kong; aim: retain financial services business moving out of India; separate jurisdiction with relaxed tax and other laws.
  • IFSC operationalised at GIFT Multi Services SEZ in April 2015; Union Budget 2016 gave competitive tax regime.
  • India INX (wholly owned subsidiary of BSE) inaugurated 9/1/2017 — index, currency, commodity, equity derivatives. NSE launched at GIFT on 5 June 2017.
  • Competitive cost and tax, single window clearance, relaxed company law, international arbitration centre; moving to unified regulator. FEMA not applicable at GIFT City. Units pay SEZ-level taxes and can offer foreign currency loans.

5. International Working Capital Management

Reasons for complexity vs a domestic firm:

  • Wider financing options — local or global markets.
  • Interest and tax rate differentials between countries.
  • Foreign exchange risk on inflows/outflows and imports/exports.
  • Home/host restrictions on movement of cash and inventory (political).
  • Limited knowledge of politico-economic conditions of host countries (eased by communication).
  • Capital convertibility: full convertibility lets MNC position funds efficiently; not available in countries like India.

Covers multinational cash, inventory and receivables management.

5.1 Multinational Cash Management

  • Traditional objectives: (1) manage and control cash — better collections/disbursements, accurate forecasts; (2) optimum utilisation and conservation — cash when needed, minimum balance, higher risk-adjusted return.
  • Constraints: country rules, forex conventions, FDI restrictions, repatriation time limits, blocked funds, multiple tax jurisdictions and currencies, no integrated exchange facilities.

Objectives of effective international cash management:

  • Minimise currency exposure risk.
  • Minimise overall cash requirement without disturbing subsidiaries.
  • Minimise transaction costs.
  • Minimise country's political risk.
  • Economies of scale and superior knowledge.

Centralised cash management helps to:

  • Keep minimum cash balance; manage liquidity of the centre.
  • Use hedging strategies optimally to minimise exposure.
  • Generate maximum returns by investing all cash.
  • Use multinational netting — lower transaction costs and exposure.
  • Use transfer pricing to enhance profitability.
  • Exploit currency correlations: payables and receivables in positively correlated currencies; payables in negatively correlated currencies; pooling — group variance smaller than sum of individual variances.

Cash flows of an MNC: subsidiaries send fees, dividends, excess cash to parent; parent sends loans and return on cash invested; subsidiaries pay each other for supplies. Two basic objectives: optimising cash flow movements and investing excess cash — no single strategy achieves both.

Ways of optimising cash flows: accelerating inflows; managing blocked funds; leading and lagging; transfer pricing; netting; investing excess cash.

5.1.1 Accelerating Cash Inflows

  • Customers send cheques to lockboxes at various locations → less time and collection cost.
  • Pre-authorised payment — firm charges customer's bank account up to a limit.

5.1.2 Managing Blocked Funds

  • Host country may block remittance or require local reinvestment (jobs).
  • Subsidiary may borrow locally for the parent so blocked funds repay those bank loans.
  • Assess future blockage (political risk); move funds via transfer pricing, direct negotiations, leading and lagging.

5.1.3 Leading and Lagging

  • Adjust timing of payments (credit terms between units) by expected currency moves — reduces exposure or increases working capital.
  • Lead (accelerate) hard currency payables; lag (delay) soft currency payables.
  • SM example: Indian subsidiary buys from Japanese subsidiary. Rupee expected to fall vs yen → pay early (leading). Rupee expected to strengthen → delay (lagging).
  • Check government restrictions first.

5.1.4 Minimising Tax on Cash Flows through Transfer Pricing

  • Higher transfer price → larger profit for transferor division vs transferee.
  • For MNCs complicated by exchange restrictions, inflation differentials, import duties, tax rate differentials, host-country quotas. (Applied in TYK P2: $10 mn transfer price.)

5.1.5 Netting

  • Optimises cash flows by combined effort of subsidiaries → lower administrative and conversion costs; minimises total inter-company fund flow.
  • Advantages: fewer cross-border transactions; less forex conversion; better cash forecasting (net transfers at period end); accurate reporting and settlement.
  • Bilateral: parent–subsidiary or two subsidiaries. X buys $20 mn from Y, Y buys $30 mn from X → gross $50 mn; Y pays X only $10 mn.
  • Multilateral: each affiliate nets all receipts against all payments, pays/receives the balance. Needs centralised communication and disciplined subsidiaries. P → Q → R → P, $50 mn each → transfers completely eliminated.

Method: matrix of receivables and payables in a common currency → each affiliate's net receipt / net payment.

SM netting matrix (US parent; France, Germany, UK, Italy; $ '000): receipts France 200, Germany 180, UK 210, Italy 190; payments France 240, Germany 130, UK 160, Italy 250; gross 780. Net: Germany +50, UK +50 receive; France −40, Italy −60 pay → net transfers 100, a reduction of 87%.

5.1.6 Investing Excess Cash

  • Euro currency market: Euro dollar deposits yield more than US bank deposits; also foreign T-bills/CP.
  • Centralised pooling of subsidiaries' surplus → larger deposits, higher returns; separate investment account lets one subsidiary fund another without bank conversion charges.
  • Converting pooled funds into one currency costs money → cost-benefit analysis.
  • Choose currencies with higher interest or expected appreciation; foreign deposit if effective yield beats domestic after exchange risk (forecast rates); diversify across currencies to avoid big loss on sudden depreciation.

5.2 International Inventory Management

  • MNC holds stock above EOQ — stock piling (vertical set-up; political bottlenecks; importing currency expected to depreciate).
  • Decision: cumulative carrying cost vs expected price rise from exchange rate change; if probability of supply interruption is very high, stockpile even at higher cost.
  • Longer lead time + customs → re-order point earlier; depends on quantity imported vs locally available.
