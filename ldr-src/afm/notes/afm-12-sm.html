1 Introduction

  • Interest = cost of borrowing and compensation for the service and risk of lending (default risk + inflation risk; also covers the lender's processing cost).
  • Firms with low margins and high capital expenses are very sensitive to rate rises; interest rate derivatives mitigate risk and build value.
  • Factors affecting interest rates (macro-economic): (a) demand/supply of money — high growth → higher demand → rates up; (b) inflation — higher inflation → rates rise; (c) Government — biggest borrower; RBI changes key rates (CRR, SLR, bank rate) by printing notes or Open Market Operations (OMO).

2 Benchmark Rates

  • Benchmark (reference) rate = rate that forms the basis for other rates: loans, overdrafts, mortgages, floating-rate loans, and derivatives (forwards, futures, options, swaps). Basis points (BPs) are added over it depending on credit rating. Set by an independent body.
  • LIBOR manipulated (2012) → decided in 2017 to cease; from 1 Jan 2022 contracts use Alternative Reference Rates (ARRs).
  • ARR vs LIBOR: (i) ARRs are based on actual overnight transactions (secured or unsecured); LIBOR is unsecured and relies on judgment of panel banks; (ii) ARRs are near risk-free with no term premium. Each currency has its own ARR, regulator and nature.

3 / 3.1 Interest Rate Risk; Types of Interest Rate Risk

  • Change in prices of bonds due to change in interest rates, plus impact on interest income → both price risk and reinvestment risk need focus. Banks gain or lose with every change in rates on fixed-term deposits.

3.1.1 Gap Exposure Asked: May 2026 Q5(b) 7m

  • Gap (mismatch) risk: assets, liabilities and off-balance sheet items with different principal amounts, maturity dates or re-pricing dates. Most important for banks.
FormulaTerms / logicEffect on NII
Gap = RSA − RSLRSA = interest rate sensitive assets; RSL = interest rate sensitive liabilities (re-priced in the time band). Gap = measure of rate sensitivity.Positive (asset sensitive): rate rise → NII up. Negative (liability sensitive, RSL > RSA): rate fall → NII up; rise → NII down.
EaR = Gap × assumed change in interest rateEarnings at Risk: how much earnings may be hit by an adverse rate move. Rate change estimated from past trends / forecasts.Bank fixes an EaR limit (based on last/current year income) and a trigger point at which on- or off-balance sheet hedging starts.

Gap figures can be augmented with the average coupon in each time band to estimate NII; periodic gap analysis shows exposure over distinct maturities and the portfolio change needed to alter the risk profile. (In May 2026 Q5(b) the gap was for a quarter, so EaR used × 1/4, compared with a limit of 8% of NII.)

Limitations of Gap report:

  • Measures only the time difference in re-pricing; ignores basis and embedded option risks.
  • Assumes all items in a time band mature/re-price simultaneously; ignores effect on economic/market value of assets, liabilities and off-balance sheet positions.
  • Ignores differences in timing of payments due to changed rate environment.
  • Assumes parallel shift in yield curve, which seldom happens.
  • Ignores variability in non-interest revenue and expenses.

3.1.2–3.1.6 Basis, Embedded Option, Yield Curve, Price, Reinvestment Risk

RiskSM point
3.1.2 Basis riskRates on different assets, liabilities and off-balance sheet items change by different magnitudes (e.g. assets on fixed rate, liabilities on floating). High where composite assets are funded from composite liabilities (Indian loan book). NII expands → favourable basis shift; contracts → basis moved against the bank.
3.1.3 Embedded option riskBig rate changes encourage prepayment of loans, exercise of call/put on bonds, premature withdrawal of deposits. Faster and bigger the change, greater the risk. Banks should estimate embedded options, adjust Gap statements and stipulate penalties based on opportunity cost.
3.1.4 Yield curve riskYield curve moves frequently through business cycles; evaluate its impact on portfolio value and income.
3.1.5 Price riskWhen assets are sold before maturity; bond prices and yields are inversely related. Linked to the trading book → limits on portfolio size, holding period, duration, defeasance period, stop loss, marking to market.
3.1.6 Reinvestment riskUncertainty about the rate at which future cash flows can be reinvested; cash-flow mismatches → variation in NII.

3.2 Measuring Interest Rate Risk

  • Risk must be identified and quantified before it can be managed or hedged.
  • The IRR system should cover all sources (gap, basis, embedded option, yield curve, price, reinvestment, net interest position), the characteristics of each position, and the full range of rate movements.
  • Techniques: Maturity Gap Analysis (sensitivity of earnings), Duration (sensitivity of capital), Simulation, Value at Risk; often used in combination / hybrid.

4.1 / 4.1.1 Traditional Methods — Asset and Liability Management (ALM)

  • Tool evolved by RBI; introduced in Indian banks w.e.f. 1 April 1999.
  • Comprehensive and dynamic framework for measuring, monitoring and managing market risk: managing the structure of the balance sheet so that net interest earnings are maximised within the overall risk preference (present and future).
  • Covers liquidity, interest rate, foreign exchange, equity and commodity price risks; integrated with business strategy. Functions extend to liquidity risk, market risk, trading risk, funding & capital planning, profit planning, growth projection.
  • Relevant for banks, finance, leasing and insurance companies. Gives a true picture of the risk/reward trade-off. A first step in long-term strategic planning → planning function for an intermediate term (planning, direction and control of levels, changes and mixes of assets, liabilities and capital).

4.1.2 Forward Rate Agreements (FRAs) Asked: Sep 2025 Q2(b) 4m

Features (TYK T1 — short note):

  • Agreement by which a borrower/lender protects itself from unfavourable rate changes; OTC, not exchange-traded (unlike futures).
  • Used by banks to fix interest cost on anticipated deposits or revenue on variable-rate loans indexed to a benchmark (SOFR, €STER, MIBOR).
  • Off-balance sheet; no transfer of principal — principal is notional.
  • Cash-settled at maturity for the profit/loss. Seller pays buyer the increased interest cost if RR > FR; buyer pays seller if RR < FR. So a borrower buys an FRA (protection against a rise).
  • Differential is discounted at the actual (post-change) rate because it is settled at the beginning of the period, not at the end.
FormulaTerms / logicSM example figures
Settlement = N × (RR − FR) × (dtm/DY) ÷ [1 + RR × (dtm/DY)]N = notional principal. RR = reference rate for the contract maturity on the settlement date (SOFR/€STER/MIBOR). FR = agreed forward rate. dtm = FRA period in days. DY = day-count basis, 360 or 365. Numerator = extra (or saved) interest for the FRA period; denominator = discount at RR for the same period (paid upfront). Positive → seller pays buyer; negative → buyer pays seller.$1 m, FR 5%, 3-month SOFR 6%: 1 bp on $1 m for 90 days = $25 → $25 × 100 bp = $2,500 → buyer receives $2,500 ÷ [1 + 0.06(90/360)] = $2,463.05.
a × b FRASettlement after a months; deposit/loan matures b months from contract date → FRA period = b − a months. Use RR for that (b − a) period.1 × 4: deposit placed after 1 month, maturing 4 months from contract (3-month period).

4.2 / 4.2.1 Modern Methods — Interest Rate Futures (IRF)

  • Futures contract on an interest-bearing asset (future delivery); locks in the price of that asset for a future date. Bonds are the underlying, not the interest rate.
  • Logic: rates and bond prices move inversely. Borrower fears a rise → sells futures today; if rates rise, futures price falls → profit on closing out (buying back).
  • NSE IRF: futures on 6, 10 and 13 year GOI Security and 91-day T-Bill (91DTB). Settlement at two levels: daily MTM and physical delivery on any day in the expiry month; final settlement only on expiry date. Prices by demand and supply.
FormulaTerms / logic
Delivery price = Conversion factor × Futures priceConversion factor (introduced by RBI, published by NSE per bond per expiry) makes deliverable bonds of different maturities/coupons comparable with each other and with the notional bond.
Seller's profit = (Futures settlement price × CF) − Quoted spot price; Seller's loss = Quoted spot price − (Futures settlement price × CF)Cheapest to Deliver (CTD) = bond that minimises the gap between quoted spot price and CF-adjusted futures settlement price, i.e. the bond that maximises profit or minimises loss of the futures seller — least expensive in the deliverable basket.

4.2.2 Interest Rate Options (IRO) Asked: Jan 2026 Q4(a) 6m

  • Also called Interest Rate Guarantee (IRG): a right, not an obligation; acts as insurance — protection against adverse moves while keeping benefit of favourable moves. Cost = premium.
  • An IRO is basically a series of FRAs exercisable at pre-set benchmarked rates for each period (3 months, 6 months …).

4.2.2.1–4.2.2.2 Cap Option and Floor Option Asked: Jan 2026 Q4(a) 6m

  • Cap (call option): buyer pays premium for the right to receive the excess interest whenever the index rises above the cap rate; no obligation if rates fall. Bought by a borrower on floating-rate debt. Evolved from guarantees on floating-rate loans; OTC caps from 1985 (banks stripped guarantees from FRNs); spurred by the leveraged buyout boom of the 1980s (lenders required LBO borrowers to buy caps); activity declined as LBOs fell.
  • Cap is characterised by: notional principal; benchmark (SOFR, MIBOR, PLR) of a specified maturity; cap rate (= strike); period incl. payment and reset dates. Payments follow swap conventions; reset intervals usually equal the benchmark's term.
  • First period is excluded — its rate is known on the agreement date.
  • Floor (OTC): protects the buyer against a fall in rates — bought by a lender/depositor. Payoff when rate < floor rate = payoff from selling an FRA; above floor → nothing, premium lost. Floor = European put on an FRA; multi-period floor = bundle of European puts on a sequence of FRAs.
FormulaTerms / logicSM example figures
Cap payoff = N × max(0, r − rc) × (dt / days in year)r = actual spot rate on reset date; rc = cap rate (decimal); dt = days from reset date to payment date. Paid on the next payment date (in arrears).$1 m, 6-month SOFR cap 5%, 15 Jan–15 Jan, reset 15 July; SOFR 5.5% → on next 15 Jan (184 days): $1,000,000 × (0.055 − 0.050)(184/360) = $2,555.56.
Floor payoff = N × max(0, rf − r) × (dt / days in year)rf = floor (strike) rate; dt = days from last reset to payment date.—
Premium per reset period = (Lump-sum premium rate × A) ÷ PVAF(i, t), i.e. P = rp × A ÷ [(1/i) × (1 − 1/(1+i)t)]Upfront lump-sum premium spread into equal amounts per reset period. A = principal; rp = premium rate; i = fixed rate per period; t = number of periods.TYK P3 below.

4.2.2.3 Interest Rate Collars Asked: May 2026 Q4(c) 4m

  • Collar = buy a Cap + sell a Floor → floating rate bounded on both high and low side.
  • Limits benefit from a big fall in rates, but cheaper than a cap alone: floor premium received offsets cap premium.
  • Zero-cost collar: premium earned on the floor exactly offsets the cap premium (value of cap = value of floor; solve for the unknown floor or cap rate).
FormulaTerms / logic
Collar payment = N × [max(0, r − rc) − max(0, rf − r)] × (dt / days in year)First bracket = cap receipt (r above cap); second = floor payment owed (r below floor); between rf and rc → nil. r = actual spot rate on reset date; dt = term of the index in days.

4.2.3 Interest Rate Swaps; 4.2.3.1–4.2.3.4 Dealers, characteristics, timing, price quotation

  • Swap counterparties exchange payments indexed to two different interest rates on a notional principal that is never exchanged.
  • 4.2.3.1 Swap dealers: intermediary took a brokerage fee, no continuing role; contract between the two ultimate users, who paid each other directly.
  • 4.2.3.2 Fixed/floating swap is characterised by: a fixed rate; a floating rate periodically reset; a notional principal; the term, with schedule of reset dates and payment dates.
  • 4.2.3.3 Timing: negotiated on trade date; takes effect two days later = settlement date.
  • 4.2.3.4 Price quotation (swap rate): fixed rate quoted as All-In-Cost (AIC) — the fixed rate relative to a flat floating index (e.g. "8% against six month SOFR flat").

4.2.3.5 Types of Swap

TypeSM point
(a) Plain vanilla (generic / coupon swap)Exchange of fixed for floating over a period on notional principal; floating basis SOFR, €STER, SONIA, MIBOR, PLR. Fixed leg: 30-day months, 360-day year.
(b) Basis rate swap (non-generic)Both legs floating, on different benchmarks (e.g. 1-month SOFR vs 3-month SOFR).
(c) Asset swapExchange of fixed-rate investments (bonds with guaranteed coupon) for floating-rate investments (an index).
(d) Amortising swapNotional principal declines over the life; useful for issuers of redeemable bonds/debentures — hedges rate risk in line with the redemption profile.

4.2.4 Swaptions; 4.2.4.1–4.2.4.3 Features, pricing, uses

  • Option on an interest rate swap: right, not obligation, to enter an IRS at a specific future date, at a particular fixed rate, for a specified term. (TYK T2)
  • Fixed rate payer swaption (Call swaption): right to pay fixed, receive floating. Fixed rate receiver swaption (Put swaption): right to receive fixed, pay floating.

4.2.4.1 Principal features:

  • A. Option on a forward-start IRS; fixed rate, floating reference rate and tenure fixed when the swaption is concluded.
  • B. 3-month into 5-year swaption = option to enter a 5-year IRS, 3 months from now.
  • C. Option period = time from transaction date to expiry date.
  • D. Premium expressed in basis points.
  • E. Can be cash-settled: at expiry marked to market off the applicable forward curve, difference settled in cash.

4.2.4.2 Pricing: model of the probability distribution of the forward zero-coupon curve which undoes a market process.

4.2.4.3 Uses (SM prints this as "2.2.4.3"):

  • Applied by both active traders and corporate treasurers.
  • Swap traders: speculation or to hedge part of swap books.
  • Hedging embedded optionality in many businesses.
  • Borrowers targeting an acceptable borrowing rate.
  • Businesses tendering for contracts.
  • Protection on callable/puttable bond issues.

Exam steps — FRA: identify buyer (borrower) / seller → FRA period from a × b (b − a months) and pick RR for that period → (RR − FR) × N × dtm/DY → ÷ [1 + RR × dtm/DY] → sign: + seller pays buyer, − buyer pays seller → state who pays whom.
Exam steps — Cap / Floor: borrower buys cap, depositor buys floor → premium = rp × N, spread per period ÷ PVAF(fixed rate per period, n) → each reset: payoff = N × max(0, r − rc) [or max(0, rf − r)] × period → net = payoff − premium per period → total net gain / risk reduced.
Exam steps — Collar: buy cap + sell floor → payoff = cap receipt − floor payment each period → zero-cost: PV of cap = PV of floor → solve unknown rate → effective rate lies between floor and cap.
Exam steps — Plain vanilla / OIS: fixed leg N × AIC × 180/360 → floating leg N × R × actual days/360 (OIS: compound daily, holidays carry previous rate) → net = difference → solve fixed rate from net if given.
Exam steps — Comparative advantage swap: table of each party's rates → each borrows where relatively cheaper → list receipts/payments under swap → net cost vs cost without swap → gain to each.
Exam steps — Gap / EaR: RSA − RSL per time band → sign (positive gains on rise, negative gains on fall) → EaR = gap × rate change × fraction of year → compare with EaR limit → hedge if beyond trigger.
