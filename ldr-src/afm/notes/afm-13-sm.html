1. Conceptual Framework of Valuation

  • Valuation = assessing the 'true worth' of an enterprise; both science and art; part of strategic financial management. Used here mainly for M&A.
  • Need for valuation: (a) information for internal stakeholders; (b) comparison with similar enterprises (management efficiency); (c) future public listing; (d) strategic planning (value driver, deployment of surplus cash); (e) ball-park price for acquisition.

3. Approaches / Methods of Valuation

  • Unlisted target → price = negotiated. Listed target → market cap is a guide but not the worth (depends on market efficiency; often only a small portion of shares is quoted).
  • Three broad approaches: (a) Asset based, (b) Earning based, (c) Cash flow based; plus other methods.

3.1 Asset Based Approach (3.1.1–3.1.3)

  • Value of shares = net assets acquired; based on income-generating assets, not income. Least important for IT companies (assets are IPR and human resources).
MethodBasis / formulaPlus / minus
3.1.1 Net Asset Value (Book Value)Net assets = Fixed Assets + Net Current Assets − Long Term Debt (historical cost)Simplest, data easily available; but Balance Sheet is not a valuation device → gives the lower limit; ignores current values, intangibles (brand, IPR).
3.1.2 Net Realizable Value (Liquidation / Adjusted Book Value)Realisable value of all assets − liquidation expenses − liabilities (liquidation expenses may be ignored if acquired as going concern)Off-market purchase → buyer may offer lowest price; not popular (total break-up); useful where acquirer sells one part and integrates the rest.
3.1.3 Replaceable ValueCost of an equivalent group of assets and liabilities in the open marketBetter than book value; slightly higher than NRV (quick disposal not encouraged) → many treat it as the maximum price an acquirer would pay; cannot value staff loyalty.

3.3 Cash Flow Based Approach (DCF)

  • Takes the free cash available in future periods and discounts it at a rate matching the flow's risk; overcomes over-reliance on historical data. PV > current cost of investment → valuation attractive.
  • Five steps: (a) arrive at Free Cash Flows; (b) forecast future cash flows; (c) discount rate from cost of capital; (d) Terminal Value; (e) PV of FCFs and TV, and interpret.
  • Limitation: result gravitates heavily on WACC and TV; TV is often a significant portion → growth rate and assumptions must be validated.
FormulaTerms / logicSM use
FCF = EAT − one-time incomes + one-time expenses + depreciationOne-time events eliminated; depreciation is a book entry.SM example
Adjusted FCF = FCF − change in working capital − investment in fixed assetsReinvestment needed to earn the flow.SM example
Free Cash Flow (FCF) = EAT − (Capital Expenditure − Depreciation) − ∆ Working CapitalEAT = EBIT after tax (no interest in FCFF); net capex and extra working capital are cash out.TYK P3, P7, P8
Terminal Value = FCF of terminal year ÷ (Cost of capital − growth rate); PV = TV × PVF of last explicit yearApp-written generalisation of the TYK P7/P8 working: e.g. 375.32/(0.12 − 0.10); cost of capital of the stable phase.TYK P7, P8
V0 = FCFF1 ÷ (Kc − gn)FCFF1 = next year's FCFF; Kc = cost of capital; gn = growth forever.TYK P6

4.3 Estimating Beta and Valuation of Unlisted Companies

  • Biggest challenge for a private firm: cost of capital → beta. Listed peers are leveraged; private firms have nil/insignificant debt → use unlevered beta.
  • Same problem for a listed company entering a new line of business: do not use own WACC; use asset (ungeared) beta re-geared to own gearing. Asset beta = only business (systematic) risk, no financial risk.
  • Equity beta is always > asset beta; debt-free → equal. Debt beta assumed zero (bond returns not linked to market volatility).
FormulaTerms / logic
βa = βe × E/[E + D(1 − t)] + βd × D(1 − t)/[E + D(1 − t)]βa asset (ungeared) beta; βe equity (geared) beta; βd debt beta; E equity; D debt; t tax rate. Weighted like WACC.
βd = 0: βa = βe × E/[E + D(1 − t)]De-gear a proxy's beta.
Re-gear: βe = βa × [E + D(1 − t)]/EUses the appraising company's own capital structure. App-written simplification: = βa[1 + (1 − t)D/E], the form used in Illus 1.
  • Steps: (1) identify pure-play / proxy firms and their equity betas; (2) de-gear to asset beta; (3) one proxy → use it; more → average (or the most appropriate); (4) re-gear at own D/E; (5) put βe in CAPM → required return / value.

Illus 1 (X Pvt Ltd): levered β = 1.8[1 + (1 − 0.3) × 40/60] = 2.64. Adjusted EBITDA 90 − extraordinary gain 10 − pending write-off 20 = 60 × 5 = EV 300. ke = 5% + 2.64(11% − 5%) = 20.84%; WACC = 20.84 × 60/100 + 12 × (1 − 0.3) × 40/100 = 15.864%. 5. Relative Valuation Asked: Sep 2025 Q1(a) 6m

  • Asset, earnings and cash-flow approaches give intrinsic value. Relative valuation gives a 'relative' value by comparative analysis with peers (similar asset and risk dispositions); increasingly used with the intrinsic approaches to validate them. Also called 'valuation by multiples'. (TYK T2 → this para.)
  • Steps: (1) find the drivers for the multiple; (2) determine results through financial ratios; (3) find comparable firms and compare; (4) iterate to smoothen deviations.

6. Other Approaches — 6.1 Contemporary Approaches to Valuation

  • Technology-driven / conglomerate businesses → new terms: PEs, Exit Multiples, LBOs, Brand Value; some methods born of industry peculiarities.
  • Internet company (near-zero fixed assets, high brand recall) → Price per Page visited; online play store → Price per Subscriber. The discerning still check cash to sales and apply DCF.
  • Goodwill-based: asset-based value first, then goodwill separately via a multiple of annual sales or footfall (retail giant buying a mom-pop store).
  • PER = Price ÷ EPS (market prices the stock on fundamentals). EPS ₹ 40, avg price ₹ 50 → 1.25. PER is relative; compare within the sector for a median PER.
  • LBO: Private Equity firms buy enterprises "in the rough" with debt and equity (typically 70 : 30 debt to equity), sell in 3–5 years (spin-off control or outright sale). Prime casualty: future can't be predicted — a cyclical slowdown leaves the buyer with huge loan and interest.

SM example (LBO, ₹ lacs): EBITDA 100; consideration 250 (100% premium over BV + goodwill 50); bank 200, VC 50. EBIDTA Y0–Y3 100, 120, 144, 178; interest 36, 30, 24, 18 (debt repaid linearly over 6 yrs); tax 30% → EBDAT 44.80, 63, 84, 112. Exit 7 × 112 = 784 − debt 100 = equity value 684.

6.2 Chop-Shop Method (Break-up value)

  • Identifies multi-industry companies that are undervalued and worth more separated; buy assets below replacement value.
  • Steps: (1) identify segments and average capitalisation ratios of firms in those industries; (2) compute a "theoretical" market value from each ratio; (3) average them = chop-shop value.

Illus 4 (Cornett GMBH, market €4 million): Cap/Sales: 0.75 × 1.5 mn + 1.10 × 0.8 mn + 1.00 × 2.0 mn = €4,005,000. Cap/Assets: 0.60 × 750,000 + 0.90 × 700,000 + 0.60 × 3,000,000 = €2,880,000. Cap/Operating income: 10 × 100,000 + 7 × 150,000 + 6 × 600,000 = €5,650,000. Average = 4,178,333.33 → €4,178,000.

6.3 Economic Value Added (EVA) Asked: Jan 2026 Q4(b) 4m

  • Holistic measure of financial performance and economic contribution. A company creates value only if returns exceed its cost of capital. EVA separates operations from financing. Negative EVA → not generating value; positive → producing value.
  • Why EVA: ROI, ROCE, asset/earnings/FCF methods are "number-crunching"; EVA makes management accountable for decisions (e.g. choosing only the earlier break-even project although both had positive IRR) and shows whether returns cover the cost of capital.

6.4 Market Value Added (MVA)

  • MVA = Current Market Value of the firm − Invested Capital. SM: MV 1,000 − invested capital 920 (Illus 5) = 80. TYK P14 applies it on equity: market cap − equity funds.
  • Resolves some EVA issues (ignoring value drivers, book value). Gives no basis of share valuation, but gauges performance efficiency from a market capitalisation view — market discounts management's efforts fairly.
  • Higher MVA → "darlings of the share market" → stock becomes 'pricey'; EVA may then have a slightly negative correlation with MVA — a short-term phenomenon (gap closed by investors), e.g. a stock going ex-dividend.

6.5 Shareholder Value Analysis (SVA)

  • Why: NOPAT is historical, ignores future earnings, cash flows and future investment opportunities. SVA adds value drivers: earnings potential (sales), investment opportunities, cost of incremental capital.
  • Steps: (a) FCFs from value drivers; (b) discount at WACC; (c) add terminal value; (d) add current market value of non-core assets / marketable investments; (e) deduct debt → value of equity.

7. Arriving at Fair Value

  • Acquirer wants the fair price — no less no more. CA: arm's length; financial analyst: PV in cash terms; speculator: arbitrage among similar entities with dissimilar values. In booms (dot com) valuations defy fair value.
  • No single correct method → determine a range: minimum acceptable by seller to maximum payable by buyer; final deal by negotiation (TYK P1 range).
  • Exam approach: (i) unless specified, value by as many methods as data allow; (ii) comment on each; (iii) supplement the conclusion with any additional information.

8. Going Concern and Non-Going Concern Valuation

  • Going concern: no intention or need to liquidate or curtail operations materially; otherwise a different basis, disclosed. Asset valuation depends on it (historical cost).
  • Non-going concern = Liquidation Valuation: net value after disposing all assets and discharging all liabilities. Going concern value = Total Value (adds value of continuing profits).
  • Going-concern value generally > liquidation value: includes future profitability, intangibles, goodwill (premium); liquidation means laying off employees and bad reputation among investors. Use non-going-concern only when the firm has no value as a going concern.

9. Valuation of Distressed Companies

  • Distress: unable / difficulty paying financial obligations — high fixed costs, illiquid assets, revenues sensitive to downturns (too much debt, or inability to meet operating expenses). Defaulting does not make the company worthless.
  • Conventional methods fail: (1) DCF terminal value assumes infinite life, ever-growing cash flows — not valid with negative cash flows; (2) negative, declining revenues, high bankruptcy risk; DCF values a going concern — projections needed until cash flows turn positive, else negative equity value; (3) discount rates reflect sound companies — must be adjusted for probability of failure.
MethodFormula / logic
9.1 Modified DCFProbability distributions of cash flows (adjust for default); ke from updated D/E and unlevered beta; kd from updated default risk. If distribution can't be estimated: Expected cash flowt = Cash flowt × (1 − Probability of distresst)
9.2 DCF + Distress valueSteps: (i) going-concern value on the path back to health; (ii) probability of distress over DCF life; (iii) distress sale value as % of book value or of DCF value. Value of Equity = DCF value of equity (1 − Probability of distress) + Distress sale value of equity (Probability of distress). Plain DCF understates value when the firm may fail and sell assets below PV.
9.3 Adjusted Present ValueSeparates investment from financing. Firm Value = Unlevered Firm Value + (Tax Benefits of Debt − Expected Bankruptcy Cost); unlevered value = FCFF at unlevered cost of equity; tax benefit = PV of expected tax savings. Expected Bankruptcy Cost = (Unlevered firm value − Distress sale value) × Probability of distress
9.4 Relative ValuationRevenue and EBITDA multiples (P/E, P/BV often unusable); adjust industry multiple subjectively: telecom avg 2× revenue → 1.25× for a distressed telecom firm.

10. Valuation of Start-ups

  • Three common methods: (i) Earning / cash flow (discount foreseeable cash flows); (ii) Asset (not going concern — liquidation, untimely losses; current realisable values); (iii) Market (comparable companies, adjusted). All presuppose an established, cash-generating business.
  • Start-up: no historical data; value rests on future growth potential (often an untested idea) and the founders' competence and drive. Valuation helps investors navigate "using facts, rather than fairy tales".

10.1 Why traditional methods cannot be applied

MethodWhy it fails for new-age start-ups
IncomeNo / minimal positive cash flows in the foreseeable future (model accepted by investors).
Asset(i) Negligible assets — mostly IP and intangibles, hard to value and agree; (ii) operate as going concern, so value shouldn't be limited to realisable value today.
MarketDisruptors without established competitors → numbers skewed, not comparable; some market-approach elements used in advanced funding rounds.

10.2 Value Drivers for start-ups

  • Product — uniqueness, readiness (functional product/prototype > idea); market testing and customer response.
  • Management — credentials and balance of team (engineers + finance + MBA better than only engineers); > half of Indian unicorns have IIT/IIM founders.
  • Traction — quantifiable evidence of demand. Revenue — more streams, more value. Industry attractiveness — logistics, distribution, customer base (tourism start-up less valuable if lockdowns expected).
  • Demand–supply — attractive industry → more investor demand. Competitiveness — fewer competitors, more value; first-mover advantage (Ola after Uber: easier to convince, but must differentiate).

11. Valuation of Digital Platforms

  • Software-based online infrastructure for many-to-many interactions. Types: Marketplace (Booking.com, Uber, Amazon), Search engine (Google, Bing, Baidu), Repository (Spotify, YouTube, GitHub), Digital communication (WhatsApp, Teams, Telegram, Slack), Digital community (Facebook, LinkedIn), Payments (Paytm, GPay).

11.1 Income Approach (11.1.1 Discounting rate, 11.1.2 Specific considerations)

  • Top-Down: backward working from TAM (Total Addressable Market) → SAM (Serviceable Addressable) → SOM (Serviceable Obtainable) and time to reach it → business plan. Ambitious for a nascent company. Bottom-Up: earnings from limited resources (high-margin sales, cost cutting) — like capital budgeting.
  • Revenue model differs (streaming: subscribers; payments: transactions). Early cash burn / low margins to gain share; cash need falls as margins stabilise. Value depends on quality of forecasts and promoters' execution.
  • 11.1.1: FCFF at WACC, FCFE at Cost of Equity Capital (Ke); Ke by CAPM R = rf + β (rm − rf).
  • 11.1.2: (a) beta hard to estimate — unique platforms, scarce listed comparables → use diversified sector, revenue-driving industry or international comparables; (b) survival depends on management quality and adaptability → add a Company Specific Risk Premium (CSRP) / Alpha to ke; CSRP higher for nascent than mature platforms.

11.2 Market Approach · 11.3 Cost Approach

  • Multiples: P/E, EV/EBITDA, P/BV, Price/Sales; comparables selected by market cap, revenue, margins, capital structure. Difficult because: listed comparables scarce/absent; profit and EBITDA may be negative; capital-lite → very low book value. So emphasis on revenue drivers:
PlatformDrivers of revenue
Market placeNo. of bookings, registered users, volume of transactions
PaymentActive subscribers, merchants registered, compatibility and speed of OS, security, ease of use

CommunicationNumber of users, sponsored links, advertising revenue

  • Repository: compare users, average views per user, average revenue per user. Example: search platform valued 100.00 Cr with 50 mn users and 100,00 hours of content used as base for a similar platform with fewer users.
  • 11.3 Cost Approach: cost to build the same/similar platform — mainly developer hours spent writing code; not most appropriate — ignores revenue-generating capacity. Fundamentals remain: business understanding, revenue model, management quality, risk-reward.

12. Valuation of Professional / Consultancy Firms

  • Firms giving customised, knowledge-based services (CAs, advocates, management consultants); vary widely by service.
  • Compare historical data with industry KPIs and benchmarks and with competitors; sources: audited statements, income tax returns.
  • Income approach: projected growth (TV) matters, but future earnings may not materialise → factor in the risk.
  • Normalisation of net income and cash flows (add back non-cash and firm-specific items) → compare on equal footing; choose KPIs aligned with acquirer's goals.

13. Impact of ESG on Valuation

  • ESG = framework embedded in strategy to generate value for all stakeholders.
  • E: climate change, water, waste generation, emissions, biodiversity. S: employee development, diversity & inclusion, community development, health & safety, customer. G: board independence, board diversity, anti-corruption & bribery, tax transparency, ethical conduct.
  • Developments: ESG funds tapped > $50 bn in 2020, ESG assets > $35 trn; green bonds crossed $1 trn (2020); sustainability taxonomy (EU); IFRS single global ESG standard; SEBI proposed ESG disclosure framework (Feb 2023).
  • High ESG focus → lower cost of debt, access to Green / Social / Sustainability-linked bonds; ESG moved from 'good to have' to 'must-to-have'.
  • Incorporate in DCF via discount rate (risk premium — practical but less explicit) or cash flows (more explicit): E — 2-degree scenario analysis, carbon points; S — cost of social measures (labour conditions, CSR, welfare) on revenue; G — impact of poor governance (penalty, fines, taxes).

Case Studies

  • Case 1 — Vodafone / Idea Cellular: methods: (a) market value (NSE price), (b) comparable companies' market multiple (BSE/NSE), (c) NAV as of 31 Dec 2016; DCF not used (no projected cash flows provided) → share exchange. Idea slumped 9.6% (fell up to 14.57%, reversing 14.25% gains) → 'perceived value' is not quantifiable.
  • Case 2 — Facebook / WhatsApp: USD 21.8 bn; 450 mn users at $1/year fee, 70% active, +1 mn users/day (≈ 1 bn in a year); gross per-user value ≈ USD 55 incl. 4 bn retention sweetener; payback by monetising international messaging.
Exam steps — Relative valuation / EV (Sep 2025 type): ke = rf + β (rm − rf) → adjust EBITDA for extraordinary / pending items → EV = EBITDA × multiple → Equity value = EV − debt + surplus cash (÷ shares) → or EV = shares × CMP + debt − cash.
Exam steps — EVA (Jan 2026 type): PAT ÷ (1 − t) = PBT → + interest = EBIT → NOPAT = EBIT(1 − t) (+ non-cash items) → invested capital (equity + debt, or assets − non-interest CL, + unrecorded patents) → WACC (ke by CAPM, kd(1 − t), weights) → capital charge → EVA → EVA ÷ shares = max DPS. MVA = market value − invested capital.
Exam steps — DCF / FCFF: high-growth WACC and stable WACC (CAPM + kd(1 − t)) → FCFF = EBIT(1 − t) − (capex − dep) − ∆WC each year → PV at high-growth WACC → TV = terminal-year FCF ÷ (stable cost of capital − growth rate) → discount TV by PVF of the last explicit year → firm value (− debt + non-core assets = equity, as in SVA).
Exam steps — Unlisted firm / new project: proxy βe → de-gear βa = βe × E/[E + D(1 − t)] → average → re-gear at own D/E → CAPM ke → WACC → discount FCFs.
Exam steps — Several methods / exchange ratio: NAV per share (deduct contingent liability if it will materialise) → earnings capitalisation (maintainable PAT ÷ rate) → PE × EPS → DCF → weighted / average fair value → range (seller's minimum to buyer's maximum) → exchange ratio = target value ÷ acquirer value; allocate shares on fully-paid equivalents.
Exam steps — Chop-shop / distressed: segment data × pure-play ratios (sales, assets, operating income) → average = break-up value vs market. Distressed: DCF value of equity (1 − Probability of distress) + Distress sale value of equity (Probability of distress); APV = Unlevered Firm Value + Tax Benefits of Debt − (Unlevered firm value − Distress Sale Value) × Probability of Distress.
