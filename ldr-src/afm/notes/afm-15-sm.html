1. The Basics of Startup Financing

  • Startup financing = initial infusion of money needed to turn an idea (by starting a business) into reality.
  • Banks / traditional lenders not interested: no security of assets yet, cash flows and repayment capacity cannot be forecast with certainty.
  • Left with: selling assets, borrowing against home, loans from family and friends → risk of bankruptcy and strained relationships; keep such loans strictly business like.
  • Speed up initial operations to the point where outside investors can see and feel the venture and see the risk the promoter has taken.
  • Bootstrapped business: start and build from personal finances or operating revenues of the new company; grows without investors dictating terms.
  • Reaching large investors needs a strong business plan and advice from experienced entrepreneurs and experts (possible future investors).

2. Some of the Innovative Ways to Finance a Startup

Banks are the least likely benefactors for startups → innovation = maximising non-bank financing.

  • (i) Personal financing: own savings; most investors will not put money in if the promoter has contributed nothing personally.
  • (ii) Personal credit lines: based on personal credit efforts (e.g. credit cards); banks give it only when the business has enough cash flow to repay.
  • (iii) Family and friends: believe in you; loan obligations always in writing (promissory note or otherwise).
  • (iv) Peer-to-peer lending: a group of people come together and lend to each other; small and ethnic business groups of similar faith/interest support each other.
  • (v) Crowdfunding: small amounts of capital from a large number of individuals, via social media and crowdfunding websites.
  • (vi) Microloans: small loans by individuals at lower interest rates; from a single individual or aggregated across many contributors.
  • (vii) Vendor financing: company lends to its customer to buy its own products; also manufacturers/distributors defer payment until goods are sold (e.g. 30 days extended to 45 or 60 days) — depends on creditworthiness and paying more money.
  • (viii) Purchase order financing: startup lacks cash to produce and deliver a large new order; PO financing company advances funds directly to the supplier, transaction completes and profit flows to the business.
  • (ix) Factoring accounts receivables: seller who sold on credit (e.g. 6 months) gets most (say 80–90%) upfront from the factor; balance on due date after the factor collects from the customer, less its interest charges → funds day-to-day expenses.

3. Pitch Presentation

Pitch presentation = short and brief presentation (not more than 20 minutes) to investors on the prospects of the company and why they should invest. Pitch deck = brief PowerPoint giving a quick overview of the business plan to convince investors. Face-to-face or online, to potential investors, customers, partners and co-founders. Points to keep in mind:

  • (i) Introduction: who are you, what are you doing — keep it short; most interesting facts and major milestones achieved.
  • (ii) Team: people behind the scenes; investors invest in the team, not only the idea; promoter's background and its relation to the company; past results if the team worked together.
  • (iii) Problem: the problem the startup will solve and convince that the product/service solves it. SM example: Facebook (2004) vs Orkut — on Facebook a profile is visible only if the person adds you (privacy), which made it an instant hit.
  • (iv) Solution: how the company plans to solve the problem. SM example: Flipkart (2007) — cash on delivery (credit card payment rare) and its own supply chain system for timely delivery → raised large capital.
  • (v) Marketing/Sales: market size, target customer profiles, how customers will be attracted; if already selling, growth and future revenue forecasts.
  • (vi) Projections or Milestones: educated guess where no long financial history; projected statements show where the business is heading (profit or loss). Three documents:
    • Income statement: revenue generated, cost to generate and support it, paid to employees, rent. Monthly in year 1, quarterly in year 2, annual thereafter.
    • Cash flow statement: cash in and cash used; per period shows cash burn or cash generated and closing cash balance.
    • Balance sheet (typically annual): assets, liabilities, equity — cash in bank, owed to suppliers, money invested in the company.
  • (vii) Competition: every business has competition even if new/unique; show how products differ; if any competitor was acquired, give details (name, acquisition price etc.).
  • (viii) Business Model: core aspects — purpose, business process, target customers, offerings, strategies, infrastructure, organisational structures, sourcing, trading practices, operational processes and policies incl. culture. (Investopedia, as quoted in SM: the way a company generates revenue and makes a profit; compared using gross profit = revenue − COGS.) Pitch should show the revenue streams with timeline, pricing vs competitors, cost of acquisition, lifetime value of the customer and customer retention strategy.
  • (ix) Financing: money already raised, who invested, how used; if none, work done with minimum funding; investors like promoters who invested their own money; state how much is to be raised and intended use.

SM example (business model): Co A and Co B rent movies; revenue ₹ 5 crore, COGS ₹ 4 crore → GP ₹ 1 crore (20%). Co A moves online: revenue still ₹ 5 crore, COGS ₹ 2 crore (lower storage and distribution) → GP margin 20% → 60%; A has an additional 40% margin to play with.

4. Concept of Unicorn

Unicorn = privately held start-up that has achieved a valuation of US$ 1 billion. Term coined by venture capitalist Aileen Lee in 2013; mythical animal = statistical rarity of successful ventures. Features:

  • (i) A privately held start-up.
  • (ii) Valuation reaches US$ 1 billion.
  • (iii) Emphasis on the rarity of success.
  • (iv) Other common features: new ideas, disruptive innovation, consumer focus, high on technology.
  • Valuation slips below US$ 1 billion → loses Unicorn status; can be a Unicorn at one time and not at another.
  • InMobi (ad-tech, founded 2007): India's first Unicorn, Sept 2011 — SoftBank invested US$ 200 million, valuing it over US$ 1 billion (four years). Udaan (B2B e-commerce, 2018): fastest, in just over two years.
  • SM year list (selected): 2011 InMobi; 2012 Flipkart; 2013 Mu Sigma; 2014 Ola, Snapdeal; 2015 Paytm, Quikr, Zomato; 2016 Shopclues, Hike; 2017 ReNew; 2018 BillDesk, Byju's, Oyo, Swiggy, Udaan and others; 2019 onwards many more (2021 the largest list).
  • India = 3rd largest startup ecosystem (after US and China), over 59,000 DPIIT-recognised startups; as of 3 Oct 2023, 111 unicorns, combined valuation US$ 349.67 billion (InvestIndia.gov.in).
  • Next milestone: Decacorn = valuation of more than US$ 10 billion.

5. Modes of Financing for Startups — 5.1 Sources of financing

5.1.1 Bootstrapping Asked: May 2026 Q2(c) 4m

Bootstrapping = start and build a company from personal finances or operating revenues of the new company. Logic: more money at inception → complacency and wasteful spending (marketing, offices, equipment); own savings → cautious approach, curbs waste, keeps promoter on toes. Methods:

  • (a) Trade credit: suppliers reluctant to new businesses (want cash/credit card). Way out: well-crafted financial plan, visit supplier (owner if small, CFO if big firm), communication skills, reference of someone known to the supplier. May get half on credit, balance on delivery. Trick: get goods shipped and sell before paying. Reduces working capital needed, especially in retail.
  • (b) Factoring: receivables sold to a commercial finance company; factor collects and does the paperwork; can be on a non-notification basis (customers not told). Merits: cuts receivable-maintenance costs (bookkeeping, collections, credit verification) — often cheaper than the factor's fee; frees money tied in receivables, esp. sales to businesses/government with long payment delays.
  • (c) Leasing: lease equipment instead of buying → lower capital cost, lessee claims tax benefit. SM figures: photocopier at ₹ 5,000 per month vs ₹ 1,00,000 or more to buy; automobile/van to avoid ₹ 5,00,000 or more. Lessor: depreciation tax benefit, possible capital appreciation, profit on lease. Lessee: smaller payments, ability to walk away at end of lease term, tax benefit on lease rentals.

5.1.2 Angel Investors Asked: May 2026 Q2(c) 4m

  • Context: India ranks low on entrepreneurship, innovation and ease of doing business — outdated draconian rules, red tapism, time-consuming procedures, lack of general support; changing in recent times.
  • Invest in small startups or entrepreneurs; often among the entrepreneur's family and friends; one-time investment or ongoing injection through difficult early stages.
  • More favourable terms than other lenders — invest in the entrepreneur rather than the viability of the business; focus on helping first steps, not possible profit → different from VCs.
  • Also called informal investors, angel funders, private investors, seed investors, business angels: affluent individuals; capital in exchange for ownership equity or convertible debt; some via crowdfunding platforms or angel networks.
  • Use their own money (VCs manage pooled money in a strategically managed fund). Vehicle may be an LLC, business, trust or investment fund.
  • Startup fails early → angel loses investment completely; so professional angels look for a defined exit strategy, acquisitions or IPOs.

5.1.3 Venture Capital Fund; 5.1.3.1 Venture Capitalists generally

VC Fund = investment vehicle managing funds of investors seeking to invest in startups and businesses with exceptional growth potential. Venture capital = money provided by professionals who, alongside management, invest in young, rapidly growing companies that can become significant economic contributors. VCs generally:

  • Finance new and rapidly growing companies.
  • Purchase equity securities.
  • Assist in development of new products or services.
  • Add value through active participation.

5.1.3.2 Characteristics of Venture Capital Financing

  • (i) Long time horizon: minimum 3 years, maximum 10 years.
  • (ii) Lack of liquidity: less liquidity on the equity it gets → adds an illiquidity premium in price and required return.
  • (iii) High risk: high risk, high return; high risk does not eliminate an investment choice.
  • (iv) Equity participation: mostly equity → participates in management, helps growth; with a Board seat supervises many board decisions.

5.1.3.3 Advantages of bringing VC in the company

  • Injects long-term equity finance → solid capital base for future growth.
  • VC is a business partner, sharing risks and rewards; rewarded with business success and capital gain.
  • Practical advice and assistance from experience with similar companies.
  • Network of contacts that adds value.
  • May provide additional rounds of funding to finance growth.
  • Experienced in preparing a company for IPO (Indian or overseas exchange such as NASDAQ).
  • Can facilitate a trade sale.

5.1.3.4 Stages of funding for VC Asked: May 2026 Q2(c) 4m

  1. Seed Money: low level financing to prove a new idea.
  2. Start-up: early-stage firms needing funds for marketing and product development.
  3. First-Round: early sales and manufacturing funds.
  4. Second-Round: working capital for early stage companies selling product but not yet in profit.
  5. Third Round (Mezzanine financing): expansion money for a newly profitable company.
  6. Fourth-Round (Bridge financing): finances the "going public" process.

5.2 VC Investment Process

  1. Deal Origination: directly or through intermediaries (mainly practising CAs). VC tells intermediary its sector, stage of business, promoter and turnover focus. Company gives a detailed business plan (business model, financial plan, exit plan) = Investment Memorandum (IM), with a tentative valuation.
  2. Screening: by a committee of senior VC people; selects company for further processing.
  3. Due Diligence: verifies veracity of documents; usually by external renowned consultants; fee generally paid by VC, may be shared between investor and investee.
  4. Deal Structuring: structured so both parties win; convertible structure so promoter retains right to buy back shares; tag-along clause — promoter must sell part of stake along with the VC to facilitate exit.
  5. Post Investment Activity: VC nominee on board; company keeps strong MIS, budgeting, corporate governance and VC covenants; reports milestones and explains misses; VC ensures professional management.
  6. Exit plan (spelt out at investment): (a) sell to third party(ies) — IPO or private placement to other VCs; (b) promoter buy-back at a pre-agreed rate (generally IRR of 18% to 25%) if no IPO/third-party sale; often promoter gets first right of refusal.

6. Venture Capital Funds in India — 6.1 Evolution

  • 1970s: GoI committee on inadequate funding to entrepreneurs/start-ups; ten years later first all-India VC funding by IDBI, ICICI and IFCI.
  • Nov 1988: institutionalisation; guidelines under CCI (Controller of Capital Issues) — narrow, restrictive (innovative technology by first-generation entrepreneurs) → VC risky and unattractive.
  • World Bank VC awareness seminar → TDICICI, GVFL, Canbank, Pathfinder. CCI abolished; 1995 foreign finance companies allowed.
  • 1996: new regulation; loopholes favoured foreign players, no incentive to domestic HNIs.
  • 1997: IT revolution boosted VC (IT and telecom). After the dotcom bust, survivors shifted to later stage financing, leaving seed/start-up to a few daring funds.
  • SM diagrams: formation of VC fund — Trust, Company, Limited Liability Partnership; investors in VC funds — financial institutions, banks, pension funds, corporations, high networth individuals.

6.2 Structure of Venture Capital Fund in India

Three structures: one domestic, two offshore.

StructureHow it works
(a) Domestic fund(i) Domestic vehicle pooling investor funds + (ii) separate investment adviser as asset manager. Pooling vehicle: trust or company (India does not recognise a limited partnership); trust prevails for operational flexibility.
(b) Offshore structureOffshore vehicle (LLC or LP outside India) invests directly into Indian portfolio companies; assets managed by an offshore manager; Indian investment advisor does due diligence and identifies deals.
(b) Unified structureUsed when domestic investors also participate: overseas investors pool in an offshore vehicle that invests in a locally managed trust; domestic investors contribute directly to the trust, which makes the local investments.

7. Startup India Initiative Asked: Jan 2026 Q6(c) 4m

Launched by GoI on 16 January 2016. Per GSR Notification 127(E) dated 19 Feb 2019, an entity is a Startup if:

  • (i) Up to 10 years from incorporation/registration, as a private limited company (Companies Act, 2013), registered partnership firm (s. 59, Partnership Act, 1932) or LLP (LLP Act, 2008) in India.
  • (ii) Turnover for any financial year since incorporation/registration has not exceeded ₹ 100 crore.
  • (iii) Working towards innovation, development or improvement of products/processes/services, or a scalable business model with high potential of employment generation or wealth creation.
  • Proviso: entity formed by splitting up or reconstruction of an existing business is not a Startup.

SM box (startupindia.gov.in) words the third test as innovation, development, deployment or commercialisation of new products, processes or services driven by technology or intellectual property.

Government support (SM):

  • Startup India and StandUp India (2016): widespread awareness, boost to entrepreneurial mindset.
  • SIDBI-run Electronic Development Fund (EDF): Government became a Limited Partner in a fund for the first time.
  • Easy finance: Mudra Scheme; tax benefits: 100% tax holiday u/s 80-IAC; exemption from angel taxation.
  • Startup India Seed Fund Scheme (SISFS), DPIIT, Jan 2021: outlay ₹ 945 crore for proof of concept, prototype, product trials, market entry, commercialisation; ~3,600 entrepreneurs through 300 incubators in next 4 years. Eligibility: DPIIT-recognised, incorporated not more than 2 years ago at application, idea with market fit, viable commercialisation and scope of scaling. Seed fund up to ₹ 50 lakh. Priority sectors: social impact, waste management, water management, financial inclusion, education, agriculture, food processing, biotechnology, healthcare, energy, mobility, defence, space, railways, oil and gas, textiles.

Other reasons India is a sustainable environment for start-ups:

  • (i) Pool of talent: millions graduating from colleges and B-schools start own ventures (earlier went only to big companies).
  • (ii) Cost effective workforce: young country, over 10 million join the workforce every year → lower cost of setting up and running a business.
  • (iii) Increasing use of internet: affordable telecom; reached rural areas; second-largest internet user base after China.
  • (iv) Technology: quick, simple, efficient processes; easy data storage; startups in AI and blockchain.
  • (v) Variety of funding options: angel investors, VCs, seed funding investors; easing of FDI norms, most sectors on 100% automatic route.

8. Succession Planning in Business — 8.1 Meaning

  • Process of identifying critical positions and developing action plans for individuals to assume them; identifies future need of people with skills and potential for leadership roles.
  • Important for family owned businesses (often managed by a non-family leader though family owns).
  • Holistic view of current and future goals → right people for the right jobs today and in future.
  • Can provide a liquidity event — transfer of ownership in a going concern to rising employees.
  • Prepares the business to promote and advance all employees, not only management/executives.

8.2 Why is there a need for succession planning?

  • Risk mitigation: if leader quits, search can take six–nine months; leaderless organisation → disruption, uncertainty, conflict, endangered competitiveness.
  • Cause removal: leader culpable of gross negligence, fraud, wilful misconduct or material breach and barred by court, arbitral tribunal, management, stakeholders or other agency.
  • Talent pipeline: keeps employees motivated with visible career paths; retains and leverages the company's knowledge bank.
  • Conflict resolution mechanism: promotes open and transparent communication and settlement of conflicts.
  • Aligning: in family businesses aligns with culture, vision, direction and values.

8.3 Business succession strategy Asked: Sep 2025 Q1(c) 4m

  1. Evaluate key leadership positions: risk or impact assessment to find critical roles — those that transform the business or set strategic direction.
  2. Map competencies required for those positions: qualifications, behavioural and technical competencies.
  3. Identify competencies of current workforce: internal options that can deliver Step 2, training and development needs, and whether to search outside.
  4. Bridge leader: in family businesses, an outsider appointed to develop the business and prepare young family members for leadership.

8.4 Challenges (in context of Start-ups)

  • (1) Founder mindset ≠ corporate mindset: puts off seasoned corporate leaders from joining even matured start-ups.
  • (2) Premature for startups: early growth stage; too many processes slow growth.
  • (3) Founders are the face of startups: hard to imagine the startup without the founder who initiated and executed the idea.
