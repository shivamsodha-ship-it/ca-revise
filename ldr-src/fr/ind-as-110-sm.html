  • Consolidated FS present the financial position of the entire group (parent + group companies); separate FS present a single entity.

53.3 Control evaluation 6–9 · B2

An investor controls an investee if and only if it has all three: (1) power over the investee · (2) exposure, or rights, to variable returns from the investee · (3) ability to use its power over the investee to affect the investor's returns.

  • After the initial assessment, reassess when there are changes to one or more of the three elements.
  • The investor may need to act in co-operation with another entity, or no single investor may control → account under Ind AS 111 (joint arrangement), Ind AS 28 (associate) or Ind AS 109 (financial instrument).

63.3.1 Power 10–14 · B9–B54

An investor has power if it has: A. existing rights that give it B. the current ability C. to direct the relevant activities of the investee. (SM explains in reverse: relevant activities → current ability → rights.)

How to identify relevant activities and how decisions about them are made

  • Relevant activities = activities of the investee that significantly affect the investee's returns. Examples (not limited to): selling and purchasing of goods and services · managing financial assets during their life (incl. upon default) · selecting, acquiring or disposing of assets · researching and developing new products or processes · determining a funding structure or obtaining funding · establishing operating and capital decisions incl. budgets · appointing, terminating and remunerating KMP or service providers.
  • Power to direct relevant activities can be shared by multiple parties without meeting the definition of joint control.
  • Activities before and after a particular event may both be relevant; where investors can direct activities at different times, decide who directs the activities that most significantly affect returns (same as concurrent rights); reconsider if facts change.
  • Two or more investors can each unilaterally direct different relevant activities → the one directing the activities that most significantly affect returns has power.

Example 2: five parties own 20% each, one board seat each; all strategic decisions need consent of any four of five → not joint control (no unanimity required), but power is shared and no single party controls.

Illustration 3: C Ltd. builds and operates a toll road; A constructs (2 years, recovers cost + fixed margin, first call on toll cash); B then runs operations, maintenance, and takes residual cash as management fee; B sets the tolls → A is merely a contractor; residual risks and rewards are B's → B controls C from inception, even during construction.

Illustration 4: A Ltd. (asset manager, 10% units) takes investment/divestment decisions of Fund X; majority unitholders appoint a committee for day-to-day administration → A directs the activities that most significantly affect returns → A has power (control still needs returns and link).

Current ability to direct relevant activities

  • The investor can make decisions at the time they need to be taken. It need not actively direct.
  • Not limited to acting today — steps like calling a meeting don't prevent power, if no other barriers exist.
  • Investees with mostly predetermined decisions: decisions arise only on particular events → the ability to make them if and when they arise is current ability.
  • Current ability always arises from rights (voting, potential voting, rights in other arrangements, or a combination).

Illustration 5: vehicle funded by a debt investor and equity investors (equity takes first loss); an equity investor with 30% is asset manager and manages the portfolio until defaults use up the equity tranche; then a trustee manages it on the debt investor's instructions → relevant activity = managing the asset portfolio; each of the asset manager and the debt investor must judge who directs the activities that most significantly affect returns, considering purpose and design and their exposure to variability.

1. Substantive rights — holder must have the practical ability to exercise them

Judgement on all facts. Factors (not exhaustive):

  1. Barriers that prevent exercise: financial penalties and incentives · high exercise or conversion price · terms that make exercise unlikely (e.g. narrowly limited timing) · no explicit, reasonable mechanism in founding documents or law · holder can't obtain the information needed · operational barriers or incentives (e.g. no other manager willing or able to provide specialised services and take over the existing manager's interests) · legal or regulatory requirements (e.g. foreign investor prohibited from exercising).
  2. Where exercise needs more than one party or rights are held by many: is there a mechanism giving them the practical ability to act collectively? No mechanism → probably not substantive. An independent board can be such a mechanism.
  3. Would the holders benefit from exercising? Potential voting rights are more likely substantive when the exercise price is below market or there are synergies.

Illustration 5 (SM numbering) — voting rights: A holds 10%; nine others 10% each gave A decision rights for 5 years via a management agreement. (A) the 90% want to terminate after 2 years but a huge penalty is payable → barrier → not substantive. (B) no penalty, but only 4 holders (40%) dispute; removal needs a majority → not substantive.

Illustration 6 — potential voting rights: investor holds 30% with an option for 30% more. (A) exercise price far above market (loss-making company) → not substantive. (B) price at market, but exercisable only within 1 month and the investor can't arrange funds → not substantive. (C) market price unknowable (new, unique business) → can't judge benefit → not substantive.

Illustration 7 — removal rights: other investors can remove the VC fund's asset manager, but no other manager is willing/able to provide the specialised services and buy its stake → not substantive.

  • To be substantive, a right should be exercisable when decisions need to be made — usually currently exercisable, but not always.
  • Example 3: investor holds 30% + option for 40% more exercisable within 25 days; next shareholders' meeting on relevant activities is in 30 days → can reach 70% in time → substantive.
  • Evidence that the investor has been directing relevant activities helps but is not conclusive.
  • Consider substantive rights held by others — rights to approve or block decisions can prevent the investor from deciding. Example 4: 5 investors; any 2 can call a meeting, but every decision needs unanimous consent → each investor's consent is needed.

2. Protective rights

  • Designed to protect the holder's interest without giving power. Mainly relate to fundamental changes or apply in exceptional circumstances (but not every exceptional/contingent right is protective). They neither give power nor prevent another party from having power.
  • Examples: right exercisable only on fraud or default by the investors who have power · a lender's right to appoint a nominee director to ensure the borrower avoids activities that increase credit risk (*protective only so long as it doesn't give majority control of the board) · minority shareholders' right to approve capex beyond the normal course, or issue of equity or debt · a lender's right to seize assets on default · rights to approve/block change of name, amendment of constitutional documents to enter a new business, change of registered office.

3.3.1.3 Voting rights

Generally power comes through voting rights. But even a majority may not give power if (i) the voting rights are not substantive, or (ii) another entity (not the investor's agent) has existing rights to direct the relevant activities. Other rights may still give power.

3.3.1.3.1 Power through voting rights

A. With a majority — power where (a) relevant activities are directed by a vote of the majority holder, or (b) a majority of the governing body that directs relevant activities is appointed by the majority holder (e.g. board appointed by the majority shareholder).

B. Without a majority — through: (1) contractual arrangement with other vote holders (investor directs how they vote) · (2) rights from other contractual arrangements (decision rights over, e.g., manufacturing or financing, combined with votes) — economic dependence alone (e.g. supplier and its main customer) does not give power · (3) the investor's own voting rights — de facto control · (4) potential voting rights · (5) a combination.

De facto control — sufficiency of votesHigherLower
Number of voting rights held by investorMore likely powerLess likely
Size of investor's votes relative to other holdersMore likely powerLess likely
Number of parties needed to outvote the investorMore likely powerLess likely

Illustration 9: 45%; thousands of others ≤1% each; no arrangements → more likely power — can conclude on size alone without other evidence. Illustration 10: 40%; six others at 10% each → less likely; size alone is not conclusive — consider additional facts.

  • Also consider potential voting rights and other contractual arrangements. If the investor holds sufficiently more votes and others are widely dispersed, the three factors may be enough.
  • If still unclear → consider additional facts, e.g. voting patterns at previous meetings. If still not clear that the investor has power → it does not control.

Illustration 11: 35%; three others hold 5% each; rest ≤1% each; decisions by majority of votes cast; 75% of votes cast at recent meetings → active participation means no practical ability to direct unilaterally → no power, even if others happened to vote with the investor.

Potential voting rights

  • Rights to obtain voting rights — convertible instruments, options, forward contracts. Considered only if substantive.
  • Consider the purpose and design of the instrument and other benefits from exercise; consider them together with the investor's votes and other rights.

Illustration 12: A and two others hold one-third each; investee's business closely related to A; A also holds debt convertible at any time at a fixed price (substantive) → 60% on conversion; synergies → A has power.

3.3.1.3.2 Power other than through voting rights

When voting rights can't significantly affect returns (e.g. votes relate to administrative tasks only), consider: (1) purpose and design of the investee · (2) contractual arrangements · (3) special relationship between investor and investee.

(1) Purpose and design

  • Helps identify: the relevant activities, how decisions about them are made, who has current ability to direct them, and who receives the returns.
  • Consider the investor's involvement and decisions at design stage. Involvement in design alone is not sufficient for control, but may show the investor had the opportunity to obtain rights sufficient for power.

Illustration 13: PQR sets up XYZ to build a power plant and distribute electricity under a state contract; PQR involved in design; decisions on generation and price are not determined by voting rights → involvement in design indicates opportunity to obtain power but is not sufficient; consider other facts such as contractual arrangements.

(2) Contractual arrangements

  • Consider call rights, put rights, liquidation rights giving explicit or implicit decision rights closely related to relevant activities — even if they operate outside the legal boundaries of the investee.
  • Some investees are designed so that activities and returns are predetermined until particular events occur → only decisions on those events are relevant activities. The events need not actually occur; the right to decide whenever required is enough for power.

Illustration 14 — rights contingent on future events: investee buys receivables and services them day to day (collect and pass on); on default, the receivable is automatically put to an investor under a separate agreement; managing defaulted receivables is the only relevant activity → agreement + founding documents are integral → investor has power, though it manages defaulted receivables outside the investee.

  • An explicit or implicit commitment to ensure the investee operates as designed increases exposure and the incentive to obtain power — an indicator only; doesn't by itself give power or prevent another party from having power.

Illustration 15: A (pharma) depends entirely on B for packing material (others inferior) → A is committed to ensure B operates as designed → indicator of power; consider other facts before concluding control.

73.3.2 Exposure, or rights, to variable returns 15–16 · B55–B57

  • Variable returns = returns not fixed that can vary with the investee's performance — only positive, only negative, or both. The term is "returns", not "benefits".
  • Judge on substance, regardless of legal form: fixed interest on a bond is variable (default/credit risk); fixed performance fees for managing assets are variable (performance risk). A non-refundable income not affected by credit risk or performance can be a fixed return.
  • Returns include direct (mostly financial) and synergistic (indirect) returns.
Examples of variable returns
Dividends, other distributions (e.g. interest from debt securities) · changes in the value of the investment
Remuneration for servicing the investee's assets or liabilities · fees and exposure to loss from providing credit or liquidity support · residual interests on liquidation · tax benefits · access to future liquidity
Returns not available to other interest holders — combining operating functions for economies of scale, cost savings, sourcing scarce products, access to proprietary knowledge, limiting some operations to enhance the investor's other assets
  • Exposure alone is not enough. A lender exposed to credit risk through interest doesn't control the borrower if it can't affect those payments.
  • Only one investor can control; many can share returns (e.g. NCI).
  • Reputational risk — e.g. banks supporting vehicles they sponsored during the financial crisis to protect reputation, with no legal or contractual obligation. Reputational risk in isolation is not a basis for consolidation; it is a factor (part of exposure, from non-contractual sources) that may increase the incentive to secure power, not an indicator of power in its own right.

83.3.3 Link between power and returns 17–18 · B58–B72

  • Control needs the ability to use power to affect the investor's returns. A decision maker must decide whether it is a principal or an agent, and whether another decision maker is acting as its agent.
  • Agent — a party primarily engaged to act on behalf and for the benefit of another (the principal). An agent does not control when exercising delegated rights. Rights delegated to the investor's agent are treated as held by the investor.
  • Consider the overall relationship and all four factors, with weights depending on facts: (1) scope of decision-making authority · (2) rights held by other parties · (3) remuneration · (4) exposure to variable returns from other interests.

93.4 Relationship with other parties — de facto agents B73–B75

  • Consider whether other parties act on the investor's behalf — de facto agents. Judgement on the relationship and how the parties interact. No contract needed.
  • A party is a de facto agent when the investor (or those directing the investor) can direct it to act on the investor's behalf. Combine its decision rights and the investor's indirect exposure through it with the investor's own, as if held directly. Having a de facto agent doesn't automatically mean control.

Example 5: investor 30% + fellow subsidiary 30% that the investor can direct → de facto agent → 60% → control. If the fellow subsidiary held only 10% → 40% → not control on that basis.

Possible de facto agents: investor's related parties · a party that got its interest as a contribution or loan from the investor · a party that agreed not to sell, transfer or encumber its interest without the investor's prior approval (unless both have mutual approval rights on agreed terms) · a party that can't finance operations without subordinated support from the investor · an investee whose board majority is the same as the investor's · a party with a close business relationship (e.g. professional service provider and a significant client). These are not automatically de facto agents — evaluate each.

103.5 Control of specified assets — silos B76–B79

  • A portion of an investee is a deemed separate entity (silo) if and only if: specified assets (and related credit enhancements) are the only source of payment for specified liabilities or other interests; parties other than those with the specified liability have no rights or obligations on the specified assets or their residual cash flows; in substance none of the returns from the specified assets can be used by the remaining investee and none of the silo's liabilities are payable from the remaining investee's assets — i.e. everything is ring-fenced.
  • Example: mutual fund with sub-funds (debt-oriented, equity-oriented) — check whether each sub-fund is a silo.
  • If a silo exists, assess control of the silo (power, returns, link). The investor that controls it consolidates it; other parties exclude that silo when assessing control of, and consolidating, the investee.

123.7 Investment entities 27–33 · B85A–B85W

3.7.1 Definition — ALL three conditions

  1. Obtains funds from one or more investors for providing those investors with investment management services;
  2. Commits to its investors that its business purpose is to invest funds solely for returns from capital appreciation, investment income, or both;
  3. Measures and evaluates the performance of substantially all its investments on a fair value basis.

Typical characteristics: more than one investment · more than one investor · investors not related parties · ownership interests in the form of equity or similar. Absence of one or more doesn't disqualify — needs more judgement. Reassess if any element or characteristic changes.

  • Exception: a subsidiary that is not itself an investment entity and whose main purpose is providing services related to the investment entity's investment activities → consolidate it (and apply Ind AS 103 on acquiring it). If that service subsidiary is itself an investment entity → FVTPL.
  • A parent of an investment entity consolidates all entities it controls, including those held through the investment entity subsidiary — unless the parent itself is an investment entity.

4.2.9 Elimination of intra-group transactions B86(c)

  • Intra-group balances, transactions and resulting unrealised profits are eliminated in full. Unrealised losses are also eliminated unless cost cannot be recovered.
  • Set off a liability of one group entity against the asset in the other; exclude intra-group sales from turnover and from purchases (or the relevant expense).
  • If the buyer has sold the goods outside the group → only eliminate sales and cost of sales; no profit adjustment. If still on hand → eliminate the intra-group profit; assets at cost to the group.
  • Applies to inventories, PPE, intangibles and investment property, and to all consolidated subsidiaries, even those less than 100% owned.
  • 4.2.9.1 Inventories — profit on goods still held by the buyer isn't earned until sold outside the group → eliminate from group profit; inventory at cost to group.
  • 4.2.9.2 Non-current assets — unrealised profit on intra-group transfers of PPE, intangibles, investment property also eliminated.
  • 4.2.9.3 Unrealised losses — eliminated unless cost cannot be recovered (i.e. a real impairment).

4.2.11 Preparing the consolidated FS

  • Balance sheet: combine assets and outside liabilities with intra-group eliminations; eliminate subsidiary's share capital against parent's investment → goodwill/capital reserve and NCI; add parent's share of post-acquisition profits to the parent's relevant accounts.
  • Profit and loss: add income and expenses line by line; eliminate intra-group transactions in full (e.g. parent's sales to subsidiary removed from both sales and purchases).
  • Cash flows: add line by line; eliminate inter-company cash flows.

4.2.12 Chain holding

  • Chain control — the parent controls a subsidiary indirectly (P → S1 → S2).
  • Situation 1 — sub-subsidiary: P → 80% → S1 → 60% → S2. P owns 80% × 60% = 48% of S2; NCI in S1 owns 20% × 60% = 12%; NCI in S2 owns 40%. S2 is still a sub-subsidiary (control chain), though P owns only 48%.
  • Date S2 comes under P's control: (1) the date P acquired S1, if S1 already held S2; or (2) the date S1 acquired S2, if later.
  • The order in which the group was built decides which reserves of S2 are pre-acquisition (P bought S1 before or after S1 bought S2).
  • Situation 2 — direct holding too: P → 80% S1; S1 → 75% S2; P → 10% S2 directly. Group interest in S2 = 80% × 75% + 10% = 70%; NCI 30% (direct 15% + indirect 20% × 75% = 15%).
